A hypothesis test answers one narrow question: could what I am seeing plausibly have been produced by chance alone? It does not say whether the effect matters, whether the experiment was designed correctly, or whether the comparison was decided before or after looking at the data — and most of the damage done by testing in the biomedical and chemical literature comes from reading the answer to the narrow question as an answer to the broad ones. This unit builds testing from a single idea, the permutation test, from which every classical test turns out to be a computational shortcut, and then spends most of its length on the three things that decide whether a test result is worth anything: the effect size with its interval, the multiplicity of the comparisons that were actually made, and the power the design ever had. The centrepiece is an experiment on the laboratory's own simulation data in which the same comparison yields p < 10−25 and p = 0.69 depending on whether frames or replicas are counted as the experimental unit — and only one of those two numbers is admissible.

1. What a test is, and what the laboratory tests

A statistical test has four parts, and writing all four down before computing anything removes most of the confusion:

  1. a null hypothesis $H_0$ — a complete, simulatable description of "nothing is going on" (the labels carry no information; the two conditions have the same distribution);
  2. a test statistic $T$ — any number computed from the data that is large when the effect is present (a difference of means, a difference of medians, an area under a ROC curve);
  3. the null distribution — the distribution of $T$ if $H_0$ were true, obtained either from a formula or by simulation;
  4. the p-value — the probability, under $H_0$, of a test statistic at least as extreme as the one observed.

Domain context — the three comparisons this laboratory makes

(a) Actives versus decoys. In virtual screening a computational score ranks a library of molecules; to know whether the score works, it is run on a set of known actives (molecules measured to bind the target) mixed with decoys (molecules assumed inactive, usually chosen to resemble the actives physically but to be structurally different). Our frozen library holds 1,327 ROCK1 actives and 10,691 decoys. The score used below is the maximum Tanimoto similarity of each molecule to a small set of 25 reference actives: the Tanimoto coefficient compares two molecular fingerprints — bit vectors recording which substructures a molecule contains — and ranges from 0 (nothing in common) to 1 (identical fingerprints).

(b) Two simulation conditions. Three replica molecular-dynamics runs at 300 K against three at 340 K, as described in Unit 7: the question is whether the protein is measurably less stable at the higher temperature after 1 ns.

(c) Diseased versus healthy tissue. 69 patients each contributed one gingival (gum) tissue sample from a site affected by periodontitis — a chronic inflammatory disease of the tissues around the teeth — and one from a clinically healthy site[2]. Each sample's RNA was measured on a microarray, giving a log2 expression value for 5,000 genes. Because both samples come from the same patient, the design is paired, and ignoring that pairing throws away most of the information.

2. The permutation test is the whole idea

If the labels are meaningless, then any re-assignment of the labels to the same data is as likely as the one we observed. So: compute the statistic on the real labelling; shuffle the labels many times and recompute; the p-value is the fraction of shuffles that reach or exceed the real value. That is the entire procedure[3]. It makes no assumption about normality, requires no formula, and works for any statistic you can compute — a difference of medians, an enrichment factor, an AUC.

# The permutation test, written out in six lines.
def permutation_test(a, b, stat=lambda x, y: x.mean() - y.mean(), n_perm=5000, seed=0, alternative="two-sided"):
    g = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    pooled = np.concatenate([a, b]); n_a = len(a)
    obs = stat(a, b)
    null = np.empty(n_perm)
    for i in range(n_perm):
        p = g.permutation(pooled)
        null[i] = stat(p[:n_a], p[n_a:])
    if alternative == "greater":
        p_val = (1 + np.sum(null >= obs)) / (n_perm + 1)        # the +1 keeps the p-value valid
    else:
        p_val = (1 + np.sum(np.abs(null) >= abs(obs))) / (n_perm + 1)
    return obs, p_val, null

Figure 1. Null distribution of the difference in mean similarity score between actives and decoys, built by shuffling the active/decoy labels 5,000 times. Under the null the difference is centred on zero with a standard deviation of about 0.004; the observed difference, about 0.24, sits roughly sixty null standard deviations to the right and no shuffle comes close. The reported p-value is 1/5001 — the smallest value this many permutations can produce — and the honest way to write it in a paper is p < 2 × 10−4 (5,000 permutations), not "p = 0".

Two details of the implementation matter. The +1 in the numerator and denominator is not cosmetic: without it a p-value of exactly zero is possible, which is a probability statement that cannot be true, and the corrected version is the valid Monte-Carlo p-value. And the resolution of a permutation test is bounded by the number of permutations: 5,000 shuffles can never report anything below 2 × 10−4, so if you need a smaller p-value you must either run more permutations or use a parametric approximation.

2.1 A worked example traced by hand

The permutation idea is clearest when the number of permutations is small enough to enumerate completely. Take the six replica means of the C-alpha RMSD from the molecular-dynamics dataset — 300 K: 0.1098, 0.1595, 0.2515 nm; 340 K: 0.1211, 0.1884, 0.1504 nm — and ask whether temperature changed the mean.

Observed difference of means: (0.1098 + 0.1595 + 0.2515)/3 − (0.1211 + 0.1884 + 0.1504)/3 = 0.17360 − 0.15330 = 0.02030 nm.

The hand calculation and the code agree: the observed split ranks 15th of 20 (every split and its mirror image give the same absolute difference, so the ranks come in pairs), and the exact two-sided p-value is 0.80. More importantly, the printed line about resolution is a fact about the design, not about the data: with three replicas per condition there are only 20 labellings, so the smallest two-sided p-value the experiment can possibly produce is 2/20 = 0.10. A 3-versus-3 permutation test can never be significant at the 0.05 level, whatever the data look like. No amount of computation fixes that; only more replicas do, which is the subject of §8.

3. The classical tests are special cases

Before computers, enumerating permutations was impossible, so the field derived formulas that approximate the permutation null under extra assumptions. Each classical test is a shortcut of this kind, and knowing which assumption buys which shortcut is how you choose one.

What none of the five numbers tells us is whether the score is useful. They all answer "is the difference exactly zero?", a question whose answer was known in advance. Section 5 replaces them with a number that answers the real question.

3.1 Paired data: the single most valuable design feature

When each measurement in one group has a natural partner in the other — the same patient, the same compound in two assays, the same system before and after a mutation — the paired test removes between-subject variation by analysing the within-pair differences. The gain is usually larger than anything else available to the experimenter.

Figure 2. Expression of PLA2R1 in 69 matched pairs of gingival tissue. Left: each grey line joins the healthy and diseased sample of one patient; the lines are mostly parallel and mostly descending, which is exactly the situation the paired test exploits — patients differ widely in overall level, but within a patient the direction is consistent. The printed statistics show the consequence: on identical numbers the paired test's p-value is several orders of magnitude smaller than the unpaired one (about 10−20 against 10−17), because working within patients removes about a fifth of the standard deviation — and that modest-sounding reduction is amplified by the √n in the test statistic.

The correct permutation null for a paired design is not a shuffle of the group labels but a random flip of the sign of each within-pair difference, because under the null the diseased and healthy label within a patient are exchangeable. The printed sign-flip p-value matches the Wilcoxon and paired-t results. Choosing the wrong exchangeability structure is the most common way to get a permutation test wrong.

4. What a p-value is, and the four things it is not

A p-value is the probability of data at least as extreme as the observed data, computed under the assumption that the null hypothesis and the whole statistical model are true[9][10]. From that definition, four common readings are wrong:

  • It is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true. That quantity requires a prior and is computed in Unit 10.
  • It is not the probability that the result will replicate. A study with p = 0.05 has roughly even odds of reaching p < 0.05 again[11].
  • It is not a measure of effect size. With 12,000 molecules a difference of 0.001 in mean score reaches p < 10−10; with three replicas a difference of 0.1 nm cannot reach 0.05 at all.
  • It is not a decision. The 0.05 threshold is a convention, not a property of nature, and dichotomising a continuous measure of evidence at it is the practice a large part of the statistical community now argues against[12][13].

The constructive statement is the relationship between the test and the interval: a two-sided test at level α rejects exactly when the (1 − α) confidence interval for the effect excludes the null value. The interval therefore contains the p-value's information plus the effect size plus the precision, which is why reporting the interval instead of the p-value loses nothing and gains everything.

5. Effect sizes, and their intervals

An effect size is a number that answers "how big?" in units that transfer between studies[14]. The ones we need:

  • Cohen's d = (mean₁ − mean₂)/spooled, the difference in pooled standard deviations.
  • Hedges' g = d × (1 − 3/(4(n₁+n₂) − 9)), a small-sample bias correction[15].
  • Cliff's delta = P(X > Y) − P(X < Y), a non-parametric dominance measure in [−1, 1][16].
  • Rank-biserial correlation = 2U/(n₁n₂) − 1, the same quantity derived from the Mann–Whitney statistic.
  • Odds ratio for 2 × 2 tables.

def cohen_d(a, b):
    sp = np.sqrt(((len(a) - 1) * a.var(ddof=1) + (len(b) - 1) * b.var(ddof=1)) / (len(a) + len(b) - 2))
    return (a.mean() - b.mean()) / sp

def hedges_g(a, b):
    return cohen_d(a, b) * (1 - 3 / (4 * (len(a) + len(b)) - 9))

def cliffs_delta(a, b):
    gt = sum((a[:, None] > b[None, :]).sum(axis=1))
    lt = sum((a[:, None] < b[None, :]).sum(axis=1))
    return (gt - lt) / (len(a) * len(b))

6. Multiple comparisons

Run one test at α = 0.05 on data with no effect and you have a 5 % chance of a false positive. Run 1,000 such tests and you expect 50 of them. Any experiment that measures many things at once — a transcriptome, a descriptor panel, a docking campaign over many targets — is in this regime by construction, and the correction is not optional.

Two different guarantees are on offer, and confusing them is the usual mistake:

  • Family-wise error rate (FWER) — the probability of at least one false positive among all tests. Controlled by Bonferroni (reject when p ≤ α/m) and, more powerfully and with the same guarantee, by Holm's step-down procedure[17]. Appropriate when a single false claim is costly: one confirmatory endpoint, one go/no-go decision.
  • False discovery rate (FDR) — the expected proportion of false positives among the tests you call significant. Controlled by Benjamini–Hochberg (BH)[18]. Appropriate for screening: a gene list or a hit list is a set of leads, and tolerating 5 % wrong leads to find many more true ones is a sensible trade.

The BH procedure is three lines: sort the m p-values ascending, find the largest rank k with $p_{(k)} \le \frac{k}{m}\alpha$, and reject all hypotheses up to that rank. The corresponding adjusted p-value (q-value in the loose sense) is the running minimum of $\frac{m}{k}p_{(k)}$ from the largest rank downwards. Storey's refinement additionally estimates the proportion $\pi_0$ of true nulls and gains power when it is well below 1[19].

# Benjamini-Hochberg by hand, checked against statsmodels, and drawn.
def bh_by_hand(p, alpha=0.05):
    m = len(p)
    order = np.argsort(p)
    ps = p[order]
    thresh = alpha * np.arange(1, m + 1) / m
    below = np.where(ps <= thresh)[0]
    k = below.max() + 1 if len(below) else 0
    reject = np.zeros(m, bool)
    reject[order[:k]] = True
    q = np.minimum.accumulate((m / np.arange(m, 0, -1)) * ps[::-1])[::-1]   # adjusted p-values
    qvals = np.empty(m); qvals[order] = np.minimum(q, 1.0)
    return reject, qvals, k

7. How significance is manufactured: p-hacking, forking paths and HARKing

None of the machinery above protects against the most common source of false findings, which is not a wrong formula but an unregistered analytic freedom. Three named versions:

  • p-hacking: trying analyses until one is significant — dropping outliers, adding a covariate, switching tests, collecting more data until p dips below 0.05[20]. The published literature shows the footprint of this practice as an excess of p-values just below 0.05[21].
  • The garden of forking paths[22]: the same inflation without any dishonesty, because the analysis choices were made after seeing the data, so the test that was eventually run was one of many that could have been run.
  • HARKing[23]: presenting a hypothesis formulated after the results as if it had been stated in advance, which converts an exploratory finding into a confirmatory claim it has not earned.

The simulation below quantifies the second one on a dataset in which, by construction, there is nothing to find.

# The garden of forking paths: one null dataset, twenty 'reasonable' analyses.
def forking_paths(seed):
    g = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    x = g.normal(0, 1, 60); grp = np.repeat([0, 1], 30)      # no effect whatsoever
    cov = g.normal(0, 1, 60)
    ps = []
    ps.append(stats.ttest_ind(x[grp == 0], x[grp == 1]).pvalue)                      # 1 plain t
    ps.append(stats.ttest_ind(x[grp == 0], x[grp == 1], equal_var=False).pvalue)     # 2 Welch
    ps.append(stats.mannwhitneyu(x[grp == 0], x[grp == 1]).pvalue)                   # 3 rank test
    keep = np.abs(x - x.mean()) < 2 * x.std()                                        # 4 drop 'outliers'
    ps.append(stats.ttest_ind(x[keep & (grp == 0)], x[keep & (grp == 1)]).pvalue)
    ps.append(stats.ttest_ind(x[(grp == 0) & (cov > 0)], x[(grp == 1) & (cov > 0)]).pvalue)   # 5 subgroup
    ps.append(stats.ttest_ind(x[(grp == 0) & (cov <= 0)], x[(grp == 1) & (cov <= 0)]).pvalue) # 6 other subgroup
    ps.append(stats.ttest_ind(np.log1p(np.abs(x[grp == 0])), np.log1p(np.abs(x[grp == 1]))).pvalue)  # 7 transform
    ps.append(stats.ttest_ind(x[grp == 0][:20], x[grp == 1][:20]).pvalue)            # 8 stop early
    ps.append(stats.levene(x[grp == 0], x[grp == 1]).pvalue)                         # 9 test the variance instead
    ps.append(stats.ks_2samp(x[grp == 0], x[grp == 1]).pvalue)                       # 10 test the distribution
    return np.array(ps)

A pre-registered analysis plan for a thesis chapter

Written before the data are looked at, half a page, in the thesis repository with a commit date:

  1. Question and primary endpoint. One sentence, one number. "Does scaffold series A have higher median pActivity than series B?"
  2. Data and exclusions. Which rows, which filters, and the exclusion rules — decided now, not after seeing which exclusions help.
  3. Primary analysis. The test, the effect size, the α, one-sided or two-sided, and the software.
  4. Multiplicity. How many hypotheses belong to the family, and which correction.
  5. Sample size / power. The effect size worth detecting and the n or number of replicas it requires (§8).
  6. Everything else is exploratory, and will be labelled as such in the text. Exploratory findings are hypotheses for the next experiment, not conclusions.

This is cheap, it protects the student rather than constraining them, and the practice is now standard in several fields[24][25].

8. Power: deciding how many replicas or compounds you need

Power is the probability that the test rejects the null when a specified effect really exists. It depends on the effect size, the variability, the sample size and α — fix any three and the fourth follows. A study with low power is not merely likely to miss a real effect; its significant results are also less likely to be true and are systematically exaggerated[26][27].

Closed-form power formulas exist for the t-test, but simulation is more general and far harder to get wrong: generate data under the alternative you care about, run your actual analysis pipeline, count the rejections.

8.1 The winner's curse

Low power does not only cause misses. Among the comparisons that do reach significance in an underpowered study, the estimated effects are systematically too large, because only the luckiest samples cross the threshold. This is the winner's curse, and it explains a large part of why effect sizes shrink on replication.

This is pseudoreplication[28], documented as a pervasive error in neuroscience[29] and equally common in simulation papers, where it is produced by the simple act of pasting a trajectory into a t-test.

Three honest statements can be made about this dataset, and none of them is "340 K destabilises the protein":

  1. The thermostat and barostat worked: temperature (300.4 ± 0.2 K versus 340.2 ± 0.1 K) and box volume separate the conditions with p below 10−5 at replica level, because those observables have tiny between-replica variance.
  2. No structural observable — RMSD, radius of gyration, hydrogen bonds, helix fraction, cage distance — differs detectably at 1 ns.
  3. The design had essentially no power to detect a difference of the size observed (§8), so statement 2 is "not detected", not "not there". Trp-cage unfolds on microsecond timescales, so a 1 ns window is not expected to show it.

The rule for simulation statistics

Identify the experimental unit before choosing a test. For molecular dynamics it is the independent run, not the frame[31]; for a clinical study with several samples per patient it is the patient; for a plate-based assay it is often the plate. If you have three units you have n = 3, and the correct report is an estimate with its between-unit interval plus an explicit statement of what effect size the design could have detected. If the frames must be used — for example to estimate an equilibrium average — then the autocorrelation correction of Unit 7 applies, and a mixed-effects model (Unit 9) is the principled way to use both levels at once.

10. Choosing a test, in practice

Decision sequence

  1. What is the experimental unit? Count those, not the measurements. If n is below about 6, prefer an exact permutation test and check whether significance is even attainable.
  2. Is the design paired? If there is a natural partner for each observation, use it — the gain usually exceeds anything the choice of test provides.
  3. What statistic answers the scientific question? Means, medians, proportions, ranks, an AUC. Write the effect size first, then pick the test that matches it.
  4. Check the assumptions that matter. Independence always; equal variances only for Student and ANOVA (use Welch by default); normality mainly for small n (for large n the CLT covers means, as Unit 7 showed).
  5. Count the family. How many hypotheses could have been tested? Correct with Holm if a single false claim is costly, with BH if the output is a list of leads.
  6. Report the effect size with its interval, the n, the test, the correction and the power. The p-value is the least informative of these and should never travel alone[32].

The two-page "Points of Significance" columns in Nature Methods are an excellent companion to this checklist, in particular those on significance and t-tests[33] and on power and sample size[34]. All computations here used SciPy[35] and statsmodels[1], on data curated from ChEMBL[30] and from a public gingival-tissue expression series[2].

12. Self-assessment

1. Your permutation test with 1,000 shuffles reports p = 0. What do you write in the paper?"p < 0.001 (1,000 permutations)". A Monte-Carlo p-value is computed as (1 + number of shuffles at least as extreme)/(B + 1), so it can never legitimately be zero; the resolution of the test is 1/(B + 1).
2. Three replicas per condition. What is the smallest two-sided p-value an exact permutation test can produce?2/20 = 0.10, because there are only C(6,3) = 20 ways to label six values as 3 + 3 and the observed labelling plus its mirror image are always at least as extreme as themselves. The design cannot reach 0.05 no matter what the data show.
3. When should you use Student's t-test rather than Welch's?Almost never. Welch's test costs essentially nothing in power when the variances are equal and protects the error rate when they are not, particularly with unequal group sizes. Reserve Student's version for teaching and for the rare case where equal variance is a tested property of the system.
4. A referee asks you to "add a non-parametric test because the data are not normal". Is that a good reason?Partly. Rank tests make fewer distributional assumptions but they answer a different question (stochastic ordering, not a difference of means) and they are not assumption-free — they still require independence, and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test assumes symmetric differences. With large samples the t-test on means is robust by the CLT; with small samples a permutation test on the statistic you actually care about is usually the better answer.
5. You test 1,000 genes and 50 reach p < 0.05. Have you found anything?Not yet: 50 is exactly what 1,000 true nulls would produce. Look at the p-value histogram (flat means nothing there), apply BH, and check whether any gene survives. In our null experiment about two dozen genes reached raw p < 0.05 and none survived correction.
6. What is the difference between controlling FWER and controlling FDR?FWER is the probability of making at least one false rejection in the whole family; FDR is the expected proportion of false rejections among the rejections made. Controlling FWER is stricter and appropriate for confirmatory claims; controlling FDR is appropriate when the output is a ranked list of candidates for follow-up.
7. Your paired analysis gives p = 10⁻¹⁵ and the unpaired analysis of the same data gives p = 10⁻³. Which do you report?The paired one, because it matches the design: each diseased sample has a healthy partner from the same patient, and the unpaired test wastes that information by letting between-patient variation into the comparison. Report the design in the methods; a reader must be able to tell which test applies.
8. Why is a result from a small, significant study a bad basis for a power calculation?Because of the winner's curse: among underpowered studies, only the ones whose estimate was luckily large reach significance, so the published effect is biased upwards — by about a factor of three at n = 10 in the simulation of §8.1. Planning a new study on that inflated effect guarantees it will be underpowered too.
9. How can a completely null dataset yield a significant result a third of the time?If the analysis is chosen after seeing the data. Ten defensible analytic choices — a different test, an outlier rule, a subgroup, a transformation, stopping early — each with a 5 % false-positive rate and positively correlated with each other, give roughly a 30 % chance that at least one is significant. Pre-specify the analysis, or correct over all the paths that were available.
10. You have 3,000 MD frames and six runs. How many independent observations do you have?Six, for any question about the difference between conditions. The frames within a run are autocorrelated (Unit 7 measured τ_int between about 36 and 171 ps), so they contribute a handful of effectively independent samples each; but the quantity that varies between repetitions of the experiment is the run, so the run is the unit.
11. The chi-square test and Fisher's exact test disagree on your 2 × 2 table. Which do you trust?Fisher's, and the disagreement itself tells you why: the chi-square approximation degrades when expected cell counts fall below about 5. Check the expected counts (they are returned by scipy.stats.chi2_contingency); if any is small, report the exact test and the odds ratio with its interval.
12. Write the one-sentence result of the temperature comparison in this unit."Across three independent 1 ns replicas per condition, the mean C-alpha RMSD was 0.174 ± 0.042 nm at 300 K and 0.153 ± 0.020 nm at 340 K (Welch t-test on replica means, p = 0.69); with this between-replica variability the design had 80 % power only for differences above about 0.15 nm, so no structural difference was detectable at this timescale."

Glossary

Benjamini–Hochberg (BH)
Step-up procedure controlling the false discovery rate: reject the k smallest p-values where k is the largest rank with p(k) ≤ kα/m.
Bonferroni correction
Test each of m hypotheses at α/m; controls the family-wise error rate, conservatively.
Cliff's delta
Non-parametric effect size: P(X > Y) − P(X < Y), ranging from −1 to 1.
Cohen's d
Difference in means divided by the pooled standard deviation; Hedges' g is its small-sample correction.
Effect size
A measure of how large a difference is, in units comparable across studies; always reported with an interval.
Exchangeability
The assumption that, under the null, the labels can be permuted without changing the joint distribution; it defines which permutation scheme is valid.
False discovery rate (FDR)
Expected proportion of false positives among rejected hypotheses.
Family-wise error rate (FWER)
Probability of at least one false positive in a family of tests.
Garden of forking paths
Inflation of the false-positive rate caused by data-dependent analysis choices, even without any deliberate searching.
HARKing
Hypothesising after the results are known, then presenting the hypothesis as pre-specified.
Holm procedure
Step-down FWER correction: uniformly more powerful than Bonferroni with the same guarantee.
Null hypothesis
A complete, simulatable description of "no effect", against which the data are compared.
Odds ratio
Effect size for a 2 × 2 table: the ratio of the odds of an outcome in the two groups.
Paired design
Each observation in one group has a natural partner in the other; analysis works on within-pair differences.
Permutation test
Null distribution built by relabelling the data; the general case of which the classical tests are shortcuts.
Power
Probability of rejecting the null when a specified effect is real; determined by effect size, variability, n and α.
p-hacking
Searching analyses or stopping rules until a significant result appears.
Pseudoreplication
Treating repeated measurements of the same experimental unit as independent replicates.
q-value
The adjusted p-value of a BH-type procedure; the smallest FDR at which that hypothesis is rejected.
Rank-biserial correlation
Effect size derived from the Mann–Whitney statistic, 2U/(n₁n₂) − 1.
Welch's t-test
Two-sample t-test that does not assume equal variances; the sensible default.
Winner's curse
Upward bias of effect estimates among results selected for significance in underpowered studies.
