Between downloading a dataset and fitting a model lies a stage no paper describes and every project spends most of its time in: turning records as a database delivered them into a table whose rows, columns and units each mean exactly one thing. We take the laboratory's running dataset — 5,089 bioactivity measurements for the kinase ROCK1, exactly as the ChEMBL web API returned them — and work through the repertoire of pandas needed to reshape, join, aggregate, de-duplicate and unit-harmonise it into a table with one row per compound and one trustworthy number per row. Every operation comes with its assumptions, its cost and at least one way of getting it silently wrong.

1. Tidy data: one rule you will use every day

A table can store the same information in many shapes, and most shapes make analysis harder than it needs to be. Hadley Wickham's tidy-data formulation[1] reduces the choice to three rules:

  1. Each variable forms a column. A variable is something that was measured or that labels a measurement: the compound, the assay, the potency, the unit.
  2. Each observation forms a row. An observation is one application of the measuring instrument — here, one compound tested once in one assay.
  3. Each type of observational unit forms a table. Measurements belong in one table; the properties of the assays those measurements came from belong in another, joined by a key.

The rules are a contract with the rest of your toolbox: groupby expects the grouping variable in a column, plotting libraries expect the aesthetic mapping in columns, scikit-learn expects one row per example. Untidy data can always be analysed — with enough bespoke code — but every bespoke line is a place for a bug to live.[2] The same three rules organise the R tidyverse[27] and, in Python, the pandas library that implements them.[22][23]

The most common violation in science is the wide table: a spreadsheet in which the column headers are values, not variable names. A biologist records three concentrations as three columns; a chemist records IC50, Ki and Kd as three columns. The information is all there, but "which measurement type" has been smuggled into the header row, where no software can reach it. The inverse operation that rescues such a table is melt (also called gather or pivot longer): it turns a block of columns into two columns, one holding the former header and one holding the value. Its inverse, pivot, goes back.

The cell below builds a deliberately untidy miniature of our real problem — four compounds, three measurement types, values in the cells — and tidies it.

2. The raw table, exactly as it arrived

The running dataset of this course is a frozen snapshot of ChEMBL release 37 (released 2026-05-01, downloaded 2026-10-18) holding every IC50, Ki and Kd measurement for the human protein ROCK1, target CHEMBL3231.[3][4] It is frozen deliberately: a live API returns a different table every month, and a notebook whose numbers change is not reproducible.[5] Nothing was cleaned before freezing — the defects are the lesson.

Domain context — what a bioactivity measurement is

A protein kinase is an enzyme that attaches a phosphate group to other proteins, switching them on or off; ROCK1 (Rho-associated protein kinase 1) is one such enzyme and one of this laboratory's targets. A drug-discovery project looks for small molecules — compounds, a few dozen atoms each — that block it, and "blocking" is quantified by a concentration: the IC50 halves the enzyme's activity in a given experiment, the Ki is the thermodynamic binding constant, the Kd its equivalent for binding without catalysis. All three are concentrations, usually in nanomolar (nM, 10−9 mol/L), and all three are smaller is better: IC50 = 5 nM is a hundred times more potent than 500 nM. An assay is one experimental protocol (enzyme preparation, substrate, detection chemistry); the same compound in two assays can differ by an order of magnitude, which is why the assay identifier travels with every measurement. ChEMBL is the public database, curated at the European Bioinformatics Institute, that extracts such measurements from the medicinal-chemistry literature and patents, and increasingly accepts direct deposition.[3][32] IC50 and Ki are related by the Cheng–Prusoff equation when the assay conditions are known,[6] which they usually are not.

3. Grain: the question decides the row

Before any cleaning, decide the grain — what one row stands for. The raw file's grain is one measurement; the modelling table's is one compound. The conversion between them is irreversible: a compound measured five times becomes one row and the five are reduced to summary statistics. Confusing the two is the commonest error in bioactivity modelling, and its symptom is a model that scores beautifully because the same compound sits in both the training and the test set under two activity records.

4. Reshaping the real table

melt has three partners you will reach for constantly.

  • pivot_table runs melt backwards with an aggregation step, spreading one column into many and applying a function (size, mean, median) wherever several rows land in one cell. Plain pivot refuses to aggregate and raises if the index/column pair is not unique — a useful assertion when you believe it is.
  • stack/unstack move the innermost level of a MultiIndex between rows and columns: the index-level equivalents of melt/pivot, and what you use after a groupby on two keys.
  • explode turns a column of list cells into one row per element — the standard way to normalise a packed collection into tidy form.

The cell below applies all four to the real table.

The assert is the point of the cell, not the printout. Every reshaping operation has an invariant — here, "exploded rows = distinct compound–assay pairs" — and writing it down converts a silent corruption into a loud failure. Make it a habit: one assert under every reshape, join and aggregation.[7]

Domain context — what an assay identifier buys you

In ChEMBL an assay is one protocol from one paper: a specific enzyme construct, substrate, ATP concentration and detection method. Two assays for the same protein routinely give IC50 values differing fivefold, because IC50 depends on the ATP concentration (competitive inhibitors look weaker when ATP is abundant), the enzyme preparation and the readout chemistry. The assay identifier is therefore provenance, and "which assay" a legitimate grouping variable. The assay_type letter distinguishes B (binding: does the molecule stick?) from F (functional: does it stop the protein working?) and A (ADMET).

5. Joining tables safely

Rule 3 says the assays belong in their own table. Let us build it, join it back, and then break the join on purpose.

A join is the relational operation Codd formalised in 1970,[28] and three things go wrong with it: (i) the key does not mean the same thing on both sides; (ii) the cardinality is not what you assumed, so rows multiply; (iii) some keys do not match and you never notice. pandas has one argument for each — validate= declares the cardinality and raises if violated, indicator=True records where each row came from, how= decides the fate of non-matching keys. Use all three, always.

# The assay companion table: one row per assay (rule 3 of tidy data).

            .agg(assay_type=("assay_type", "first"),

6. Split–apply–combine: groupby, agg, transform, windows

Split–apply–combine[8] names the pattern behind nearly every aggregation: split the table into groups, apply a function per group, combine the results. pandas exposes three combination semantics, and choosing the wrong one is a frequent bug.

  • agg returns one row per group — a summary table.
  • transform returns one row per original row, broadcasting the group result back — for normalising within a group or adding a group statistic as a column.
  • filter returns the original rows of the groups satisfying a predicate — for dropping small groups.

Before aggregating we need a comparable number. Potency spans six orders of magnitude, so the field works on a logarithmic scale, pActivity = −log10(activity in mol/L), which for a value in nanomolar is

$$\mathrm{pActivity} \;=\; -\log_{10}\!\left(\text{value}_{\mathrm{nM}} \times 10^{-9}\right) \;=\; 9 - \log_{10}\left(\text{value}_{\mathrm{nM}}\right).$$

IC50 = 1 nM gives pActivity 9, 1 µM gives 6, 10 µM gives 5: bigger is better and one unit is a tenfold change. ChEMBL's standard_value is already in nM, so we can compute the scale now; Section 9 rebuilds it from the reported column without ChEMBL's help and compares.

7. Missing data: three mechanisms, three responses

NaN is not a value but the absence of one, and the right response depends on why it is absent. Rubin's three cases[9] are worth memorising.

  • MCAR (missing completely at random): the probability of being missing depends on nothing — a tube was dropped. Dropping incomplete rows loses precision but introduces no bias.
  • MAR (missing at random, a badly chosen name): the probability depends on observed variables but not on the missing value itself. Dropping rows biases the result; conditioning on those variables — stratification, regression, multiple imputation — recovers it.[10]
  • MNAR (missing not at random): the probability depends on the unobserved value — a measurement is absent because it was too small to measure. No imputation fixes this from the data alone; the mechanism must be modelled or the missingness carried through as information.

The three cannot be told apart by looking at the missing cells — they are, after all, missing — only by understanding the data-generating process. Our table contains a textbook MNAR column, and one crosstab exposes it.

rows = []
for name, p in [("MCAR", p_mcar), ("MAR", p_mar), ("MNAR", p_mnar)]:
    ests = []
    for rep in range(200):

    rows.append(dict(mechanism=name, mean_fraction_deleted=float(p.mean()),
                     complete_case_mean=ests.mean(), bias=ests.mean() - truth, sd_of_estimate=ests.std()))

8. Duplicates: by key, by flag, by structure

"Remove duplicates" is three operations that disagree with each other. Answer first: duplicate with respect to what?

  1. Exact-key duplicates — rows agreeing on the columns that identify a measurement (same compound, quantity, number, assay), almost always an extraction artefact.
  2. Flagged duplicates — ChEMBL's potential_duplicate column marks rows it suspects of being re-reports of one experiment, typically across documents.
  3. Structural duplicates — different identifiers, same molecule: a free base and its hydrochloride salt, or two deposits of one structure. Invisible to any identifier-based test; they require chemistry.

Domain context — SMILES, standardisation and the InChIKey

A SMILES string is a molecule written as text: atoms are letters, bonds are implicit or written as symbols, rings are opened and closed by matching digits.[11] The same molecule can be written as many different valid SMILES, so software canonicalises them. A salt carries a counter-ion so the drug dissolves better; the dot in ...CC1.Cl separates the two fragments. Standardisation strips the counter-ion, neutralises charges and normalises functional-group drawings; RDKit implements it in a few lines.[12] The InChIKey is a 27-character hash of a standard structure description[13] — two molecules with the same InChIKey are the same molecule, which makes it the natural key for de-duplicating chemical tables.

# (3) structural duplicates: standardise every distinct SMILES once, key on the InChIKey.
from rdkit import Chem, RDLogger
from rdkit.Chem.MolStandardize import rdMolStandardize
RDLogger.DisableLog("rdApp.*")
normaliser, uncharger = rdMolStandardize.Normalizer(), rdMolStandardize.Uncharger()
largest = rdMolStandardize.LargestFragmentChooser()

def standardise(smiles):
    '''Return (canonical parent SMILES, InChIKey) or (None, None) if RDKit cannot parse it.'''
    mol = Chem.MolFromSmiles(smiles)
    if mol is None:
        return None, None
    try:
        mol = largest.choose(normaliser.normalize(mol))
        mol = uncharger.uncharge(mol)
        Chem.SanitizeMol(mol)
    except Exception:
        return None, None
    return Chem.MolToSmiles(mol), Chem.MolToInchiKey(mol)

Two of the seven — pKi and pIC50 — are already logarithms, which is why their rows carry no unit: a logarithm is dimensionless. Kd apparent and Kd(app) are two spellings of one thing.

Write the mapping as data, not code: a dictionary from unit string to a multiplicative factor into a base unit, plus an explicit list of already-logarithmic types. Anything absent from the table stays NaN and is counted, never silently coerced. The single regular expression we allow ourselves, for the scientific-notation strings, is written to fail loudly.

Worked example by hand

Take row 2670 of the file, which reports type = IC50, value = 7.99, units = 10'-10M. The header means "the number in this column is in units of 10−10 mol/L". Therefore

step expression result
1. read the exponent out of the unit string 10'-10M → exponent −10 −10
2. convert the unit to nanomolar 10−10 mol/L = 10−10 × 109 nM = 10−1 nM factor 0.1
3. apply the factor 7.99 × 0.1 0.799 nM
4. convert to molar 0.799 × 10−9 7.99 × 10−10 M
5. take the negative logarithm −log10(7.99 × 10−10) = 9 − log10(0.799) 9.097

Our conversion table should therefore turn this row into 0.799 nM, and ChEMBL's own standard_value for the row should agree. The next cell builds the table and checks all 5,063 convertible rows at once.

# Unit harmonisation written as data: a conversion table, an explicit log-type list, one regex.
TO_NM = {"nM": 1.0, "uM": 1e3, "µM": 1e3, "mM": 1e6, "M": 1e9,
         "pM": 1e-3, "fM": 1e-6, "nmol/L": 1.0, "umol/L": 1e3, "mol/L": 1e9}
LOG_TYPES = {"pKi", "pIC50", "pKd"}          # value is already -log10(molar): dimensionless
SCI = re.compile(r"^10[\^'](-?\d+)\s*M$")    # '10^-9M', "10'-8M"  ->  exponent

def unit_factor(u):
    '''Multiplier that converts a value expressed in unit u into nanomolar; NaN if unknown.'''
    if not isinstance(u, str):
        return np.nan
    u = u.strip()
    if u in TO_NM:
        return TO_NM[u]
    m = SCI.match(u)
    return 10.0 ** (int(m.group(1)) + 9) if m else np.nan

raw["unit_factor"] = raw["units"].map(unit_factor)
is_log = raw["type"].isin(LOG_TYPES)
raw["mine_nM"] = np.where(is_log, 10.0 ** (9.0 - raw["value"]), raw["unit_factor"] * raw["value"])

10. Censored measurements and the relation that flips

A censored measurement is one the experiment could not pin down: the compound was inactive at the highest concentration tested, so all we know is that its IC50 is greater than that concentration. The information is real but it is an inequality, not a number. Statistics has a mature theory for this — Tobin's censored regression,[18] survival analysis, and an environmental-chemistry literature on the damage done by substituting values for non-detects[19] — and in this unit our job is simply never to lose the flag.

One subtlety catches everyone once: on a logarithmic scale the inequality reverses, because −log10 is decreasing. "pKi < 5" means "Ki > 10,000 nM". ChEMBL performs the flip when it standardises; the crosstab shows it happening.

# Convert value and relation together, never separately.
FLIP = {"<": ">", ">": "<", "<=": ">=", ">=": "<=", "=": "="}

def to_nanomolar(value, units, mtype, relation):
    '''Return (value in nM, relation on the concentration scale). Relation flips for log-scale types.'''
    rel = relation if isinstance(relation, str) else "="
    if mtype in LOG_TYPES:
        return 10.0 ** (9.0 - value), FLIP[rel]
    f = unit_factor(units)
    return (f * value if f == f else np.nan), rel

12. Writing it as a chain

Wrangling written as a sequence of reassignments (df = df[...], df["x"] = ...) is hard to read and harder to debug: df means something different on every line and nothing can be inspected without re-running from the top. Method chaining — one expression, one operation per line — gives each step a visible input and output and avoids the chained-assignment warnings pandas 3 raises when you write into a slice. Four verbs make it possible: assign (add a column from a lambda receiving the current frame), query (filter with an expression), pipe (insert any function) and loc with a boolean callable.

# The same cleaning, written once as a chain. Each line is one verb; the input of each is the output above it.
def add_pactivity(df):
    '''Convert the reported measurement to nM and to pActivity, carrying the relation along.'''
    pairs = [to_nanomolar(v, u, t, r) for v, u, t, r in
             zip(df["value"], df["units"], df["type"], df["relation"])]
    return df.assign(value_nM=[p[0] for p in pairs], rel=[p[1] for p in pairs])

    '''Median wall-clock time of fn over a few repeats, in milliseconds.'''
    ts = []

14. The deliverable: from measurements to compounds

Everything so far prepares one transformation. The modelling table of Units 11–17 has one row per compound, and four decisions get us there, each written down rather than defaulted into:

  1. What is a compound? The standardised parent structure keyed by InChIKey (Section 8), not the ChEMBL identifier.
  2. How are replicates combined? The median of the exact values, insensitive to the single wild value a literature dataset always contains; the mean would be dragged by it.
  3. What about compounds with only censored measurements? Kept, with the median of their censored values and a censored = True flag. Dropping them would delete 1,516 mostly inactive compounds — the MNAR trap of Section 7.
  4. What provenance survives? The number of measurements, assays and documents behind each row, and the replicate spread, which Unit 6 turns into an experimental noise floor.

Worked example by hand: fasudil

15. Summary

  • Tidy data is a contract: one variable per column, one observation per row, one observational unit per table. melt, pivot_table, stack/unstack and explode move between shapes; every reshape has an invariant, so write it as an assert.
  • Decide the grain before cleaning. The raw file has one row per measurement (5,089 rows, 4,312 compounds), the modelling table one per compound (4,280). Conflating them is the commonest source of leakage in bioactivity modelling.
  • Joins fail three ways: wrong cardinality (validate=), silent non-matching (indicator=), undeclared row survival (how=). A careless merge against a non-unique lookup turned 5,089 rows into 1.6 million.
  • agg, transform and filter are different semantics. Centring with transform showed 54% of the variance in potency is attributable to which assay measured it.
  • Missingness has a mechanism. pchembl_value is missing in exactly the censored rows (MNAR); complete-case analysis was biased by roughly half a log unit in our simulation.
  • "Duplicate" needs a definition: exact-key (78 redundant rows), the database flag (279 rows, 4 shared) and structural (9 InChIKeys covering 19 identifiers, including three deposits of ripasudil).
  • Harmonise units with a table, not cleverness, convert value and relation in one function (550 relations flip when a pKi becomes a Ki), and check against an independent harmonisation — ours matched ChEMBL's to 10−15 log units on 5,062 rows.
  • Keep censored measurements and flag them. 1,727 of 5,089 are censored and 1,516 compounds have nothing else; they pile up at pActivity 5.00 (10 µM), so treating the spikes as potencies invents compounds.
  • Chain the pipeline, let NumPy loop, finish with asserts. Ten invariants plus a comparison with the reference protocol are what let us claim the table is correct.

16. Self-assessment

1. A collaborator sends a spreadsheet with columns compound, day1, day2, day3. Which tidy rule is broken and what is the one-line fix?

Rule 1: the headers day1…day3 are values of a variable that has no column. Fix: df.melt(id_vars="compound", var_name="day", value_name="value"), after which "day" can be grouped, filtered and plotted like any other variable.

2. You merge a 10,000-row measurement table with a 500-row "compound properties" table on compound_id and get 10,240 rows. What happened, and which single keyword would have prevented it?

The right-hand table is not unique on compound_id, so those measurements were duplicated. validate="many_to_one" raises a MergeError instead of silently producing the extra 240 rows.

3. What is the difference between groupby(...).agg("median") and groupby(...).transform("median")?

agg returns one row per group; transform returns one value per original row — the group median broadcast back — so it can be assigned as a column of the same frame. Summarise with agg, normalise within groups with transform.

4. In our table, pchembl_value is missing in 1,759 rows. Classify the mechanism and justify it from the data.

MNAR. The crosstab against standard_relation shows missingness is total for every censored row (1,410 >, 312 <, 5 <=, 26 with no relation) and nearly absent for exact rows: the value is missing because the compound was too weak to measure, so complete-case analysis discards the inactives and biases mean potency upward.

5. Why does df.drop_duplicates("molecule_chembl_id") not de-duplicate a chemical dataset?

Because one molecule can carry several identifiers — free base, hydrochloride, hydrate, independent deposits. Ripasudil appears under three ChEMBL ids here. De-duplicate on a canonical identifier of the standardised parent, such as the InChIKey, which collapses the three into one row.

6. A paper reports "pIC50 < 5". What is the IC50, and what is the relation symbol on the concentration scale?

IC50 = 10−5 M = 10,000 nM, and the relation becomes >: the compound is weaker than 10 µM. The minus sign in −log10 reverses the inequality. In our table 550 rows (518 pKi, 32 pIC50) undergo this flip.

7. Why does this unit keep censored measurements instead of dropping them, and what would dropping them do to a regression model?

They carry real information and amount to 1,727 of 5,089 measurements, including everything known about 1,516 compounds. Dropping them leaves actives only: the model never learns what an inactive looks like, apparent accuracy rises because the target range was truncated, and prospective performance falls. Model them as censored, or carry the flag and exclude them explicitly from the regression target — and say so.

8. What invariant would have caught a many-to-many join in the aggregation pipeline?

assert compounds["n_measurements"].sum() == len(measurements) — the conservation law. A duplicated join inflates the per-group counts, so the sum exceeds the number of measurements that actually exist.

9. When is the category dtype a win, and what is the trap when grouping on it?

A win for low-cardinality text columns in long tables — the four identifier columns here shrink about ninefold — and faster to group and join. The trap is observed=False: grouping two categorical columns then returns every combination, including those that never occur, filling the result with structural zeros.

10. You must aggregate replicate measurements of a compound. Why the median rather than the mean, and when would you prefer the mean?

The median resists the single wild replicate literature data reliably contain: a mis-transcribed exponent moves a mean by a log unit and a median not at all. Prefer the mean for technical repeats of one experiment with symmetric error on the log scale, and report the standard deviation with it.

11. Your cleaning script runs in 40 minutes on 2 million rows. Name the three things to check first.

(i) A row-wise apply or iterrows that should be vectorised NumPy — in the timing above, a factor of fifty to over a thousand; (ii) pd.concat inside a loop, which is quadratic; (iii) reading every column of a wide file when usecols= and an explicit dtype= map would read a tenth of the bytes. Profile first.

12. Why does the final cell compare our table with rock1_chembl_curated.csv, and what would a mismatch of two compounds mean?

It is an independent-implementation check: two people wrote the same protocol, so structures, censoring flags and potencies must agree. A two-compound mismatch would mean one pipeline applied a filter the other did not — most likely the heavy-atom window or the standardisation step — and must be traced before either table is used, not averaged away.

Glossary

Anti-join
The rows of A whose key has no match in B; in pandas, a left merge with indicator=True filtered on _merge == "left_only".
Assay
One experimental protocol — enzyme preparation, substrate, detection method — with its own ChEMBL accession. One compound measured in two assays can differ by an order of magnitude.
Cardinality (of a join)
How many rows a key may match on each side; declared with validate=.
Censored measurement
A measurement reported as an inequality (> 10 µM) because the experiment could not determine the value. Carries real information about inactivity.
Grain
What one row stands for: one measurement in the raw file, one compound in the modelling table.
IC50 / Ki / Kd
Concentrations summarising inhibition or binding: the concentration halving activity, the inhibition constant, the dissociation constant. Smaller is more potent.
InChIKey
A 27-character hash of a standardised structure; the practical key for de-duplicating chemical tables.
MCAR / MAR / MNAR
Rubin's missingness mechanisms: completely at random (deletion unbiased), at random given observed variables (correctable), not at random (depends on the unobserved value; not correctable from the data alone).
Method chaining
One expression of successive method calls (assign, query, pipe, loc), so each step has a visible input and output.
pActivity
−log10 of the activity in mol/L, i.e. 9 − log10(value in nM). One unit is tenfold.
Split–apply–combine
The pattern behind groupby: partition, apply per group, combine — as a summary (agg), a broadcast column (transform) or a row filter (filter).
Tidy data
Each variable a column, each observation a row, each observational unit its own table.
Window function
Computed over a group but returning one value per row — rank, cumcount, shift, rolling. Order-dependent, so sort explicitly.
