A model is a claim about data it has never seen, and validation is the experiment that tests the claim. Almost every inflated result in the literature of machine learning for chemistry and medicine comes from an experiment whose design did not match the claim: a random split used to support a statement about new chemotypes, a hyperparameter chosen on the same data used to report the score, a metric that flatters an imbalanced problem, or two models declared different on the strength of a paired test whose assumptions do not hold. This unit builds the validation apparatus of the laboratory: the taxonomy of splits and what each one simulates, cross-validation with model selection separated from model assessment, every metric you will be asked for derived from its definition, honest statistical comparison of two models, and y-randomisation. Its central experiment is the one every QSAR referee asks for — the same model under a random split and under a scaffold split — and the gap between the two numbers is the lesson.

Learning objectives

  • Choose the split that matches the deployment question — random, stratified, group, scaffold/cluster, temporal — and explain why random splits overestimate performance for molecules and for patients.
  • Implement each split in code and diagnose it with the distance from each test point to the training set.
  • Use k-fold, repeated, stratified and nested cross-validation correctly, and keep model selection separate from model assessment.
  • Derive and interpret regression metrics (RMSE, MAE, R², Spearman) and classification metrics (confusion matrix, precision, recall, F1, MCC, ROC-AUC, PR-AUC, calibration, Brier), including under class imbalance.
  • Compare two models statistically with paired cross-validation differences, a bootstrap confidence interval and the Nadeau–Bengio corrected t-test.
  • Run y-randomisation and place the real score inside a null distribution of 100 scrambles.
  • Write the validation paragraph of a paper so that a reader can reconstruct exactly what was measured.

1. Validation answers a question, and the question is about deployment

Every validation protocol is a simulation of the future. Writing it down forces you to say what the future is:

claim you want to make future being simulated split that simulates it
"this model predicts the potency of further analogues of our series" more compounds from the same chemotypes random split
"this model finds potent compounds in chemotypes we have not worked on" a new series, a new vendor catalogue scaffold or cluster split
"this model would have predicted next year's results from last year's data" the laboratory's own future time split
"this model transfers to another site/cohort" another laboratory, another hospital external validation

The numbers these protocols produce are different numbers about different questions, not better and worse estimates of one quantity. The error of the field is to compute the easiest one and write the sentence that belongs to the hardest. Wallach and Heifets showed that most ligand-based classification benchmarks reward memorisation of series rather than generalisation[3]; Sheridan showed on industrial datasets that time-split validation is systematically more pessimistic — and more honest — than random cross-validation[2]; the same complaint runs through the clinical-prediction literature, where TRIPOD+AI now requires the validation design to be stated explicitly[8].

Domain context — series, scaffolds and chemotypes

Medicinal chemists do not make molecules one at a time. They make a series: one core structure decorated with dozens of variations, so that a project's data contain large families of near-identical compounds. The computable proxy for "the core" is the Bemis–Murcko scaffold[11] — the molecule stripped of its side chains, leaving ring systems and the linkers between them. A chemotype is a looser notion: a family of structures a chemist would recognise as the same idea. Our 2,764 compounds, curated from the public ChEMBL database[32], contain 1,218 distinct scaffolds, of which 858 occur once and the largest covers more than a hundred compounds. A random split therefore puts near-twins of most test compounds into the training set, which is exactly what makes it optimistic — and exactly what a scaffold split, which keeps a whole family on one side of the split, is designed to prevent. The clinical analogue is a dataset with several tissue sites per patient, where the family is the patient; the equivalent of the scaffold split is splitting by patient_id.

2. The split taxonomy, in code

2.1 Random and stratified splits

A random split assigns rows to folds independently. A stratified split does the same while preserving the proportion of each class in every fold, which matters when the positive class is small: with 5 folds and 90 positives, a random split can produce a fold with 12 positives and one with 24, and the fold-to-fold variance of any metric inflates accordingly.

# Class balance per fold: random vs stratified.
from sklearn.model_selection import KFold, StratifiedKFold, GroupKFold

k_strat = StratifiedKFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=0)

2.2 Group splits: keeping a family together

A group split guarantees that all rows sharing a group label land in the same fold. GroupKFold takes a groups array — scaffold for molecules, patient identifier for tissue samples, trajectory for simulation frames — and builds folds that never split a group. This is the mechanism behind both the scaffold split and the cluster split; the only difference between them is how the group label is defined.

2.3 The Bemis–Murcko scaffold split

The convention in cheminformatics is deterministic: sort scaffold families by size, largest first, and fill the training set until it reaches the target fraction; everything after that goes to the test set. Large, well-populated series therefore go to training and the rarest, most isolated structures to test — a deliberately pessimistic design that answers "can this model say anything about a chemotype it has barely seen?"

# A deterministic Bemis-Murcko scaffold split: largest families to training, the tail to test.
from collections import defaultdict
def scaffold_split(scaffolds, test_size=0.2):
    groups = defaultdict(list)
    for i, s in enumerate(scaffolds):
        groups[s].append(i)
    families = sorted(groups.values(), key=lambda g: (-len(g), g[0]))   # deterministic: size, then first index
    n_train = int(round((1 - test_size) * len(scaffolds)))
    train, test = [], []
    for fam in families:
        (train if len(train) < n_train else test).extend(fam)
    return np.array(train), np.array(test)

tr_scaf, te_scaf = scaffold_split(scaffold, 0.2)
print("scaffold split: train %d, test %d compounds" % (len(tr_scaf), len(te_scaf)))
print("scaffolds shared between the two sides:", len(set(scaffold[tr_scaf]) & set(scaffold[te_scaf])))
print("active rate  train %.3f | test %.3f   (overall %.3f)"

2.4 The cluster split

Scaffolds are a crude definition of "family": two compounds with different ring systems can still be obvious analogues, and a scaffold with a single compound does not mean the compound is isolated. The alternative is to cluster the compounds by fingerprint similarity and use the cluster as the group — in cheminformatics the standard algorithm is Butina's[12], a single-pass neighbour-count clustering at a fixed Tanimoto cut-off, and the resulting leave-cluster-out protocol is the one recommended when the question is prospective performance[13].

# Butina clustering at Tanimoto distance 0.4, then clusters as cross-validation groups.
dists = []
for i in range(1, len(fps)):
    dists.extend([1.0 - s for s in DataStructs.BulkTanimotoSimilarity(fps[i], fps[:i])])
clusters = Butina.ClusterData(dists, len(fps), 0.4, isDistData=True)
cluster_id = np.empty(len(fps), dtype=int)
for k, c in enumerate(clusters):
    for i in c:
        cluster_id[i] = k
sizes = np.array([len(c) for c in clusters])
print("Butina at distance 0.4: %d clusters | largest %d | singletons %d | median size %.0f"
      % (len(clusters), sizes.max(), int((sizes == 1).sum()), np.median(sizes)))
print("compounds in the 10 largest clusters: %d (%.0f%% of the set)"
      % (sizes[np.argsort(-sizes)[:10]].sum(), 100 * sizes[np.argsort(-sizes)[:10]].sum() / len(fps)))

2.5 A simulated time split

A real time split needs dates. The curated table does not carry them, but ChEMBL molecule identifiers are assigned in roughly chronological order of deposition, so the smallest identifier of a compound is a usable proxy for "when this structure entered the public record". Sorting by it and cutting at 80% simulates training on the past and testing on the future — the protocol Sheridan advocated[2]. This is a proxy, and the notebook says so: identifier order is not publication date, and a compound re-measured years later keeps its old identifier.

# Simulated time split: order compounds by their lowest ChEMBL molecule id (a deposition-order proxy).

order = np.argsort(first_id.to_numpy(), kind="stable")
cut = int(0.8 * len(order))
tr_time, te_time = order[:cut], order[cut:]

2.6 Distance to the training set: the diagnostic that explains every split

Rather than trusting a split's name, measure what it did. For each test compound, compute the Tanimoto similarity to its nearest training compound. The distribution of that quantity is the difficulty of the split, and it is the same quantity that Unit 17 turns into an applicability domain[26].

# How far is each test compound from the training set, under three different splits?
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
tr_rand, te_rand = train_test_split(np.arange(len(y)), test_size=0.2, random_state=0)
def nn_similarity(train_idx, test_idx):
    ref = [fps[i] for i in train_idx]
    return np.array([max(DataStructs.BulkTanimotoSimilarity(fps[i], ref)) for i in test_idx])

3. The canonical experiment: one model, five protocols

Now the experiment that the rest of this block depends on — the same comparison that benchmark studies of fingerprints[28] and of structure-based screening sets[27] report when they control for dataset bias. The model is fixed — a random forest of 200 trees on the 2,048-bit fingerprints, no tuning — and only the validation protocol changes. Two metrics are reported: ROC-AUC, the probability that a random active is ranked above a random inactive, and average precision (PR-AUC), the area under the precision–recall curve, which is sensitive to how many actives there are.

# The same model under five validation protocols. (~30 s)
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier, RandomForestRegressor
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score
from sklearn.metrics import roc_auc_score, average_precision_score
def forest():
    return RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=200, random_state=0, n_jobs=-1)
def holdout(train_idx, test_idx):
    p = forest().fit(X[train_idx], y_cls[train_idx]).predict_proba(X[test_idx])[:, 1]
    return roc_auc_score(y_cls[test_idx], p), average_precision_score(y_cls[test_idx], p)
rows = [{"protocol": "random hold-out (20%)", **dict(zip(["ROC_AUC", "PR_AUC"], holdout(tr_rand, te_rand)))},
        {"protocol": "scaffold hold-out (20%)", **dict(zip(["ROC_AUC", "PR_AUC"], holdout(tr_scaf, te_scaf)))},
        {"protocol": "time hold-out (proxy, last 20%)", **dict(zip(["ROC_AUC", "PR_AUC"], holdout(tr_time, te_time)))}]
for name, cv, grp in [("5-fold stratified CV", StratifiedKFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=0), None),
                      ("5-fold GroupKFold by scaffold", GroupKFold(5), scaffold),
                      ("5-fold GroupKFold by cluster", GroupKFold(5), cluster_id)]:
    auc = cross_val_score(forest(), X, y_cls, cv=cv, groups=grp, scoring="roc_auc")
    ap = cross_val_score(forest(), X, y_cls, cv=cv, groups=grp, scoring="average_precision")
    rows.append({"protocol": name, "ROC_AUC": auc.mean(), "PR_AUC": ap.mean(),
                 "ROC_sd": auc.std(), "PR_sd": ap.std()})
split_table = pd.DataFrame(rows).set_index("protocol").round(3)

Key idea

A split is a hypothesis about the test set's relationship to the training set. Report the protocol together with the number, give both the optimistic and the pessimistic figure when you have them, and let the reader see the gap. A referee who sees only one number assumes the easiest protocol was used — usually correctly.

4. Cross-validation: selection versus assessment

4.1 k-fold, repeated and the variance of a single estimate

A single 5-fold cross-validation gives one number per fold and one average. That average is itself a random variable: change the seed and it moves. Repeated cross-validation (several complete k-fold runs with different partitions) exposes the size of that movement, which is the only honest basis for comparing two scores[25].

4.2 Selection is not assessment

Choosing a hyperparameter by cross-validation and then quoting the best cross-validated score as the model's performance uses the same data twice: once to choose, once to judge. The result is optimistic, because the maximum of several noisy estimates is biased upwards — the winner's curse. The remedy is nested cross-validation: an inner loop selects, an outer loop assesses, and the outer loop never sees the selection[1].

# Nested vs non-nested cross-validation on the real dataset (small grid, large n).
from sklearn.model_selection import GridSearchCV, cross_validate

nested = cross_validate(GridSearchCV(Ridge(), grid, cv=inner, scoring="neg_root_mean_squared_error"),
                        X, y, cv=outer, scoring="neg_root_mean_squared_error", return_estimator=True)

print("optimism of the non-nested protocol here: %.4f RMSE units" % (-flat.best_score_ + nested["test_score"].mean()))

Common misconception

"I used cross-validation, so my result is validated." Cross-validation protects against a bad train/test draw; it does nothing about a split that does not match the question, about preprocessing fitted outside the folds (Unit 11), or about selecting among candidates on the same folds you then report. Each of those needs its own guard: the right grouping, a Pipeline, and nesting.

5.2 The classification metrics, traced by hand

Take the first ten held-out compounds, the forest's predicted probability for each, and the default decision threshold 0.5. The confusion matrix counts four kinds of outcome, and every classification metric in common use is an arithmetic function of those four numbers.

predicted active predicted inactive
truly active TP (true positives) FN (false negatives)
truly inactive FP (false positives) TN (true negatives)

With the ten compounds printed by the next cell the counts are TP = 2, FP = 1, FN = 1, TN = 6, from which, by hand:

$$\text{precision} = \frac{TP}{TP+FP} = \frac{2}{3} = 0.667, \qquad \text{recall} = \frac{TP}{TP+FN} = \frac{2}{3} = 0.667, \qquad F_1 = \frac{2 \cdot 0.667 \cdot 0.667}{0.667+0.667} = 0.667$$

$$\mathrm{MCC} = \frac{TP \cdot TN - FP \cdot FN}{\sqrt{(TP+FP)(TP+FN)(TN+FP)(TN+FN)}} = \frac{2 \cdot 6 - 1 \cdot 1}{\sqrt{3 \cdot 3 \cdot 7 \cdot 7}} = \frac{11}{21} = 0.524$$

The Matthews correlation coefficient[18] uses all four cells, which is why it is the recommended single-number summary for imbalanced problems where F₁ ignores the true negatives entirely[6].

5.3 ROC and precision–recall under imbalance

ROC-AUC is the probability that a random active scores above a random inactive[17]. It is invariant to prevalence, which is both its strength and its trap: in a screening campaign where 1 compound in 1,000 is active, an AUC of 0.9 can still mean that almost every predicted hit is a false positive. The precision–recall curve answers the question the experimentalist asks — "of the compounds I buy, how many will work?" — and its area collapses when prevalence falls[5][19]. The experiment: keep the same model and the same features, and only change the proportion of actives by sub-sampling them.

5.4 Calibration: are the probabilities probabilities?

A model is calibrated when, among the cases it assigns probability 0.7, about 70% are truly positive. Ranking metrics are indifferent to calibration — ROC-AUC is unchanged by any monotone transformation of the scores — but every decision made with a probability needs it[22][23]. The Brier score is the mean squared error of the predicted probability[21], and it decomposes into calibration and resolution.

6. Comparing two models without fooling yourself

Two models, one dataset, a difference of 0.009 in mean AUC. Is it real? The naive answer applies a paired t-test to the per-fold differences. That test is invalid here, and the reason is worth understanding: cross-validation folds are not independent, because every pair of training sets overlaps in roughly (k−2)/(k−1) of its rows. The variance of the mean difference is therefore underestimated, the t statistic is inflated, and the p-value is too small — often by an order of magnitude[14][15].

Nadeau and Bengio derived the correction[4]: for $k$ resampled differences $d_i$ with $n_{\text{test}}$ test rows and $n_{\text{train}}$ training rows per split,

$$t_{\text{corrected}} = \frac{\bar{d}}{\sqrt{\hat{\sigma}^2\left(\frac{1}{k} + \frac{n_{\text{test}}}{n_{\text{train}}}\right)}}$$

where the extra term $n_{\text{test}}/n_{\text{train}}$ inflates the standard error to account for the overlap. With 5-fold cross-validation that term is 0.25, which roughly doubles the standard error. A bootstrap confidence interval over the fold differences is the non-parametric companion[16].

    m2 = LogisticRegression(max_iter=3000).fit(X[tr], y_cls[tr])
    auc_rf.append(roc_auc_score(y_cls[te], m1.predict_proba(X[te])[:, 1]))
    auc_lr.append(roc_auc_score(y_cls[te], m2.predict_proba(X[te])[:, 1]))
auc_rf, auc_lr = np.array(auc_rf), np.array(auc_lr)
d = auc_rf - auc_lr
k, n_te, n_tr = len(d), len(splits[0][1]), len(splits[0][0])
t_naive = d.mean() / (d.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(k))
t_corr = d.mean() / np.sqrt(d.var(ddof=1) * (1 / k + n_te / n_tr))
boot = np.array([np.random.RandomState(s).choice(d, k, replace=True).mean() for s in range(2000)])
print("forest  AUC %.4f (sd %.4f) | logistic AUC %.4f (sd %.4f)" % (auc_rf.mean(), auc_rf.std(), auc_lr.mean(), auc_lr.std()))
print("mean paired difference: %+.4f over %d resamples" % (d.mean(), k))
print("naive paired t-test        : t = %.2f, p = %.4f" % (t_naive, 2 * stats.t.sf(abs(t_naive), k - 1)))
print("Nadeau-Bengio corrected    : t = %.2f, p = %.4f  (correction factor n_test/n_train = %.2f)"
      % (t_corr, 2 * stats.t.sf(abs(t_corr), k - 1), n_te / n_tr))
print("bootstrap 95%% CI of the difference: [%+.4f, %+.4f]" % tuple(np.percentile(boot, [2.5, 97.5])))

7. y-randomisation: is the model learning the target at all?

The oldest sanity check in QSAR[7][10]: permute the target, re-run the entire protocol, and repeat many times. The distribution of scores obtained with scrambled targets is the null distribution of your pipeline — it absorbs every optimism the protocol contains, including the residual leakage that the reproducibility literature finds so often[31], because a leaky protocol scores above zero even on scrambled targets. The real model's score must sit far outside it.

# 100 scrambles of the target, the same ridge pipeline each time, 3-fold CV for speed. (~45 s)

Figure 10. The null distribution of 100 y-scrambles against the real score. Scrambled targets give a cross-validated R² of about −0.21 on average (negative, as they must: a model fitted to noise predicts held-out data worse than the mean) with a standard deviation of about 0.02 and a maximum below −0.16; the real target gives about 0.50, some thirty standard deviations away, and the permutation p-value is at its floor of 1/101 ≈ 0.01. The test cannot "prove" a model is good — it only rules out the hypothesis that the apparent performance comes from the protocol rather than from the data. Notice that the null is centred below zero: any pipeline whose scrambled null sits noticeably above zero has a leak, and this is the cheapest way to find one.

What y-randomisation does and does not test

It tests the protocol end to end, so it catches leakage, bugs in the fold assignment and target/feature misalignment. It does not test whether the split matches your claim: a leaky-free, perfectly scrambled-null protocol on a random split still answers the random-split question. Both checks are needed, and they are independent.

10. Self-assessment

1. Why does a random split overestimate prospective performance for molecules?Because public bioactivity data are organised into series of near-identical analogues. A random split puts close relatives of most test compounds into training, so the test score mostly measures interpolation within known series. Here the nearest-neighbour similarity of the test set is about 0.75 under a random split and 0.62 under a scaffold split.
2. You must predict potency for a vendor catalogue your laboratory has never worked with. Which protocol estimates your performance?A scaffold or cluster split, or better an external set from that catalogue. The random-split number answers a different question. Expect the scaffold-split figure (PR-AUC about 0.65 here) rather than the random-split one (about 0.84).
3. What exactly does GroupKFold guarantee, and what does it not?It guarantees that all rows sharing a group label fall in the same fold. It does not guarantee balanced classes, equal fold sizes, or that groups are chemically distinct — two different scaffolds can still be close analogues, which is why the cluster split and the similarity diagnostic exist.
4. Your nested and non-nested cross-validation agree to three decimals. Did you waste your time?No — you measured that the selection bias is negligible for this grid and this sample size, which is itself a reportable result. The same comparison with a large grid and 150 samples can differ by 0.15 AUC units, as the noise experiment shows.
5. ROC-AUC 0.90 and PR-AUC 0.18 on the same predictions. Contradiction?No. The dataset is 5% active, so the no-skill PR baseline is 0.05 and 0.18 is a 3.6-fold enrichment, while ROC-AUC is nearly prevalence-independent. Report the prevalence with any precision-based metric, and prefer PR-AUC when retrieval of a rare class is the point.
6. Two models have identical ROC-AUC but different Brier scores. Which do you deploy?If the output is a ranking, either; if the output is a probability used in a decision, the better-calibrated one. ROC-AUC is invariant to any monotone transformation of the scores, so it cannot see calibration at all.
7. Why is the naive paired t-test over cross-validation folds anti-conservative?Because the folds share training data — any two training sets overlap in most of their rows — so the differences are positively correlated and their sample variance underestimates the true variance of the mean. Nadeau and Bengio add n_test/n_train to the 1/k term, which for 5-fold cross-validation roughly doubles the standard error.
8. Your y-randomisation null is centred at R² = +0.15. What happened?Something in the protocol gives the model information about the target even when the target is scrambled — preprocessing fitted outside the folds, duplicated rows across folds, or a feature derived from the target. Find it before reading any real score.
9. Why not just report the best of your five protocols?Because the protocols answer different questions and the best one is the one that answers the easiest. Reporting the easiest number with the hardest claim is the single most common validation error in the literature, and a reader who sees one number assumes the easiest protocol.
10. How would you validate a model that will be used prospectively next year?Simulate it: train on data up to a cut-off date and test on everything after it, which is the time split (here approximated by identifier order). If possible, run the real prospective experiment — predict, then measure — which is the only validation nobody can argue with.
11. What does the standard deviation across cross-validation folds tell you?How much the estimate depends on which rows happened to be held out — about 0.035 RMSE units for the random split and 0.048 for the scaffold split here. It is a lower bound on the uncertainty of the point estimate, not a confidence interval for the model's performance on new data, which is wider.
12. Your scaffold-split test set has a much lower active rate than the training set. Is the split broken?No, it is informative: potent compounds accumulate in the large optimised series that a size-ordered scaffold split sends to training. It does mean that precision-based metrics are not comparable between the two sides, and that the prevalence must be quoted alongside them.

Glossary

Average precision (PR-AUC)
Area under the precision–recall curve; its no-skill baseline is the prevalence of the positive class.
Brier score
Mean squared error of predicted probabilities; lower is better, and its value depends on prevalence.
Butina clustering
Single-pass fingerprint clustering at a fixed Tanimoto cut-off, used in cheminformatics to define compound families.
Calibration
Agreement between predicted probabilities and observed frequencies; assessed with a reliability curve and the Brier score.
Cluster / leave-cluster-out split
A group split whose groups are similarity clusters rather than scaffolds.
Group split
A split in which all rows sharing a group label (scaffold, patient, trajectory) stay in the same fold.
MCC
Matthews correlation coefficient; a correlation between predicted and true labels that uses all four confusion-matrix cells.
Nadeau–Bengio correction
An inflation of the standard error of resampled performance differences by the factor 1/k + n_test/n_train, correcting for overlapping training sets.
Nested cross-validation
An inner loop that selects hyperparameters and an outer loop that assesses the whole selection procedure.
Permutation (y-randomisation) test
Re-running the complete protocol on scrambled targets to obtain the null distribution of its score.
Prevalence
The fraction of positives in a dataset; sets the baseline of every precision-based metric.
ROC-AUC
Probability that a random positive is scored above a random negative; nearly independent of prevalence.
Scaffold split
A deterministic group split by Bemis–Murcko scaffold, largest families to training, rare ones to test.
Selection vs assessment
Choosing a model configuration versus estimating the performance of the chosen procedure; using the same data for both is optimistic.
Stratified split
A split that preserves class proportions in every fold.
Time split
Training on data up to a cut-off and testing on later data, simulating prospective use.
Winner's curse
The upward bias of the maximum of several noisy estimates, which makes the best cross-validated candidate look better than it is.
