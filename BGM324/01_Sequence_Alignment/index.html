Section 1Why align sequences?

Sequence alignment is the operation on which almost everything else in bioinformatics rests. A sequence of nucleotides or amino acids, considered on its own, is a string with no meaning; it acquires meaning only when it is compared with other sequences whose properties are already known. The comparison is performed through an alignment: an arrangement of two or more sequences that places, one above the other, the characters inferred to descend from the same ancestral position.

The reason this works is evolutionary. Related sequences are related because they were copied, with errors, from a common ancestor; the errors accumulate slowly compared with the rate at which function is lost, so a protein that still performs the ancestral function tends to retain the ancestral sequence at the positions that matter. Alignment therefore allows us to transfer information — function, structure, the location of a catalytic residue, a disease-associated mutation — from a characterised sequence to an uncharacterised one. Section 2Evolutionary foundations

2.1 Homology, orthology and paralogy

Two sequences are homologous if they descend from a common ancestral sequence. Homology is a statement about history, and it is therefore binary: two genes either share an ancestor or they do not. Expressions such as "70 % homologous" are incorrect; the quantity that can be measured and expressed as a percentage is identity or similarity, which are properties of an alignment (Section 2.2).[13],[14]

Walter Fitch introduced in 1970 the distinction that is still used to classify homologs according to the evolutionary event that separated them.[14]

  • Orthologs are homologs in different species that diverged by a speciation event. Human haemoglobin β and horse haemoglobin β are orthologs. Orthologs usually, though not invariably, retain the ancestral function, which is why orthology is the preferred basis for transferring functional annotation between organisms.
  • Paralogs are homologs that diverged by a gene duplication event. They may reside in the same genome (human haemoglobin α and haemoglobin β) or, after a subsequent speciation, in different genomes. After duplication one copy is released from selective pressure and may acquire a new function (neofunctionalisation), partition the ancestral function with its partner (subfunctionalisation) or degenerate into a pseudogene.
  • Xenologs are homologs whose history includes horizontal gene transfer; they are common in bacteria and relevant, for instance, to the spread of antibiotic-resistance genes.

The haemoglobin example is worth keeping in mind for the rest of the unit: the globin family (haemoglobin α and β chains, myoglobin, plant leghaemoglobins) descends from a single ancestral oxygen-binding protein, its members share the same three-dimensional fold, and yet some pairs retain less than 20 % sequence identity. It is the canonical case in which sequence comparison alone struggles while structural comparison succeeds, and we shall return to it in Sections 8 and 9.

2.2 Similarity is evidence for homology, not homology itself

The lecture slides illustrate the concept of homology with the pentadactyl limb: the wing of a bat, the flipper of a dolphin and the human hand share the same skeletal plan because they inherited it from a common tetrapod ancestor, even though they now serve flight, swimming and grasping. Comparative anatomists reached this conclusion from structural correspondence, and sequence analysis reasons the same way: we observe similarity and we infer homology when the similarity is too great to have arisen by chance.

Three terms must be kept separate:

  • Identity: the percentage of aligned positions at which the two residues are the same.
  • Similarity: the percentage of aligned positions at which the residues are the same or have a positive substitution score (e.g. isoleucine aligned to leucine). Similarity is always at least as large as identity and depends on the matrix used.
  • Homology: the inference of common ancestry. It is justified when the alignment score is statistically significant (Section 7.3), and it is strengthened by shared structure, shared function and consistent phylogenetic placement.

The converse also matters. Sequences can be similar without being homologous — short low-complexity segments, transmembrane helices rich in leucine, or coiled coils all generate chance similarity — and homologous sequences can be dissimilar when they have diverged for long enough. Pearson's review on similarity searching is the clearest short treatment of these distinctions and of the practical thresholds that follow from them.[13]

2.3 How sequences diverge

An alignment is a hypothesis about the mutational events that transformed an ancestral sequence into the sequences we observe today. Three classes of event are represented explicitly.

EventEffect on the DNARepresentation in the alignmentExample
Substitution (point mutation)One nucleotide is replaced by anotherA mismatch between two aligned charactersATA → ATG
InsertionOne or more nucleotides are addedA gap ("–") in the sequence that lacks the materialAA → AGA
DeletionOne or more nucleotides are removedA gap in the sequence that lost the materialACTG → AC–G

Because a pairwise alignment cannot tell whether a gap reflects an insertion in one lineage or a deletion in the other, the two are grouped under the term indel. Only an outgroup — a third sequence that diverged earlier — can polarise the event, which is one of the reasons multiple alignments carry more information than pairwise ones.

At the protein level the consequence of a nucleotide substitution depends on the genetic code. A synonymous substitution changes the codon but not the encoded amino acid (UUA → UUG, both leucine); it is largely invisible to selection on the protein and accumulates at approximately the neutral rate. A non-synonymous substitution changes the amino acid (UUA → UUC, leucine → phenylalanine) or creates a stop codon (UGG → UGA, tryptophan → stop, a nonsense mutation that truncates the protein). Non-synonymous changes are filtered by selection: substitutions between physicochemically similar residues (leucine ↔ isoleucine, aspartate ↔ glutamate) are tolerated far more often than radical ones (tryptophan ↔ glycine). The substitution matrices of Section 4 are, in essence, quantitative tables of this tolerance.

Two consequences for nucleotide-level comparison follow from chemistry. Purines (A, G) are two-ring bases and pyrimidines (C, T) are one-ring bases; a substitution that preserves the ring class is a transition (A↔G, C↔T) and one that changes it is a transversion. Although there are twice as many possible transversions as transitions, transitions are observed roughly twice as often in most genomes, in part because they are more easily generated by the common deamination and tautomerisation errors and more easily tolerated by the structure of the double helix (Figure 3, Section 4.1). Finally, in protein-coding DNA the three codon positions evolve at different rates: third positions, where most synonymous changes occur, are the fastest, and this is why protein alignments are more sensitive than DNA alignments for detecting distant relationships.

Key points — Section 2

  • Homology is binary and historical; identity and similarity are measured on an alignment.
  • Orthologs are separated by speciation, paralogs by duplication; orthologs are the safer basis for functional transfer.
  • Alignments model substitutions as mismatches and indels as gaps; pairwise data cannot distinguish an insertion from a deletion.
  • The genetic code and physicochemistry make some substitutions far more frequent than others; this is what scoring matrices encode.

Section 3What an alignment is

Formally, an alignment of two sequences s and t is obtained by inserting gap characters ("–") into either sequence so that the two padded strings have the same length and no column contains two gaps. Each column is then one of three things: a match (identical characters), a mismatch (different characters) or an indel (a character paired with a gap). The slide example for the toy sequences s = VIVALASVEGAS and t = VIVADAVIS is one such arrangement:

V I V A L A S V E G A S | | | | | | | V I V A D A - V - - I S

The vertical bars mark identities (7 of 12 columns); the remaining columns are two mismatches (L/D, E/I) and three gaps. The question posed in the lecture — which of two alignments is better? — has no answer until we decide how to score columns. An alignment with more identities is not automatically better if it achieves them by opening many gaps, because each gap postulates an additional mutational event. Section 4 develops the scoring system; Sections 5 and 6 then show how to find the arrangement that maximises the score.

Two further quantities are reported routinely and will be needed when interpreting BLAST output. Coverage is the fraction of the query that participates in the alignment; a 98 %-identical alignment covering 20 residues of a 500-residue protein tells us something about a shared motif, not about the proteins as wholes. Alignment length is the number of columns, including gaps, and it is the denominator conventionally used for percent identity, although different programs make different choices (identity over the shorter sequence, over the alignment length, or over aligned columns only), so the convention should always be checked before two numbers are compared.

A note on the slide's statement that global alignment has been "discarded"

It is true that for database searching global alignment was superseded by local methods, because a query domain rarely corresponds to the full length of every database entry. Global alignment remains the method of choice, however, whenever two sequences are known to be homologous along their whole length: comparing orthologous proteins, aligning a target to its template for homology modelling, computing percent identity for a family, and — as the core operation inside every progressive multiple-alignment program — aligning profiles to profiles. Treat the two approaches as tools for different questions rather than as old and new.

Section 4Scoring systems

A scoring system assigns a number to every column of an alignment; the score of the alignment is the sum over columns. It therefore has two components: a substitution score σ(a, b) for every pair of residues, and a gap penalty for columns that contain a gap.

4.1 Nucleotide scoring

The simplest scheme used in the lecture is the unit or edit scheme: σ(a,a) = +1, σ(a,b) = −1 for a ≠ b, and −1 per gap position. It is adequate for teaching the algorithms and for very similar sequences, and under it the maximum score is related to the edit distance (the minimum number of substitutions and indels that converts one string into the other). For real data a 4 × 4 matrix is preferred because, as discussed in Section 2.3, transitions are more frequent than transversions and should therefore be penalised less. The lecture uses the following illustrative matrix:

Note the structure: the two transitions, A/G (−1) and C/T (0), score higher than the four transversions (−3 to −5). The default matrices of BLASTn (match +2, mismatch −3) and megaBLAST (match +1, mismatch −2) are simpler; they are tuned, respectively, to sequences of roughly 85–90 % and >95 % identity, which is one reason the choice of program matters.

4.2 Log-odds substitution matrices for proteins

With twenty amino acids of very different size, charge and hydrophobicity, a protein matrix cannot be written down from first principles; it must be estimated from alignments of proteins already known to be related. Both families of matrices in common use are log-odds matrices, which means that each entry compares two hypotheses about a column containing residues i and j:

sij = (1/λ) · log ( pij / qi qj ) pij: observed probability that i and j are aligned in homologous proteins (the "target frequency"); qi, qj: background frequencies of each residue, so that qiqj is the probability of the pair under the hypothesis of no relationship; λ: a scale factor that sets the units.

A positive score means the pair is observed more often in homologs than chance predicts; a negative score means it is observed less often. A score of zero is a pair that is uninformative. The logarithm makes scores additive, so that summing over the columns of an alignment yields the log-odds ratio of the whole alignment — the alignment score is the logarithm of how much more likely the alignment is under homology than under chance. Altschul's analysis showed that every sensible scoring matrix is implicitly a log-odds matrix for some set of target frequencies, so the real question is not whether to use log-odds but which target frequencies — which evolutionary distance — the matrix should represent.[32],[6] When λ is chosen as ln 2 the scores are in bits; BLOSUM62 is tabulated in half-bits (λ = ln 2 / 2) and rounded to integers.

4.3 PAM matrices (Dayhoff, 1978)

Margaret Dayhoff and colleagues built the first systematic substitution matrices from 71 families of closely related proteins (≥ 85 % identity), in which the alignment was unambiguous and each difference could reasonably be attributed to a single accepted mutation.[4] The procedure, summarised on the slides, is as follows.

  1. Within each family, reconstruct a phylogenetic tree and count every amino-acid exchange i → j along its branches. These are accepted point mutations: substitutions that passed through natural selection.
  2. Normalise the counts by the mutability of each residue (how often it changes at all) and by its frequency, to obtain a matrix of substitution probabilities Mij for an evolutionary distance of 1 PAM — defined as the amount of change in which 1 % of positions have undergone an accepted mutation.
  3. Extrapolate to larger distances by matrix multiplication: M(n) = (M(1))n. PAM250 corresponds to 250 accepted mutations per 100 residues; because many positions mutate more than once, and some revert, this leaves roughly 20 % of positions identical.
  4. Convert each probability matrix to log-odds scores by dividing by the background frequencies and taking the logarithm.

The PAM model therefore assumes that evolution is a Markov process with the same substitution probabilities at every position and every time, and that the exchanges observed between near-identical proteins can be extrapolated to distant ones. Both assumptions are approximations: conserved active-site positions and surface positions evolve at very different rates, and the substitutions that are accepted between 85 %-identical proteins are not the same as those tolerated after hundreds of millions of years. The numbering convention follows the extrapolation: a larger PAM number means a greater evolutionary distance (PAM30 for very similar sequences, PAM250 for distant ones).

4.4 BLOSUM matrices (Henikoff & Henikoff, 1992)

Steven and Jorja Henikoff took the opposite approach: instead of extrapolating from close relatives, measure the substitutions directly in the type of alignment one wishes to score — conserved, ungapped blocks from alignments of distantly related proteins (the BLOCKS database, ~2,000 blocks from ~500 families at the time).[5]

  1. In every column of every block, count all pairs of residues (i, j). A column with residues A, A, A, S contributes three A–A pairs and three A–S pairs.
  2. To prevent large groups of near-identical sequences from dominating the counts, cluster the sequences in each block at a chosen identity threshold and treat each cluster as a single weighted sequence. The threshold gives the matrix its number: for BLOSUM62, sequences more than 62 % identical were merged.
  3. Compute target frequencies pij and background frequencies qi from the weighted counts; convert to log-odds in half-bits (the formula of Section 4.2).

Because clustering at a lower identity merges more sequences and leaves only the more divergent pairs to be counted, the numbering runs in the opposite direction to PAM: a smaller BLOSUM number corresponds to a greater evolutionary distance (BLOSUM80 for close relatives, BLOSUM45 for remote ones). BLOSUM62 is the default of BLASTp and of most protein alignment tools because it performed best in the original benchmarks across a wide range of distances. A curious historical footnote is that the BLOSUM62 actually distributed and used everywhere differs slightly from the one the published algorithm produces, owing to a programming error in the original computation; the "wrong" matrix turns out to perform marginally better in homology searches, a reminder that matrix construction is as much empirical as theoretical.[6],[7]

4.5 Choosing a matrix

The slide summarising "PAM versus BLOSUM differences" compresses several ideas and deserves an expansion. The two families differ in how they were built, not in what they represent: both are log-odds matrices, both have positive diagonals and mostly negative off-diagonals, and both exist in series spanning close to distant relationships. The practically useful statements are these.

Two rules of thumb follow. First, use the matrix that matches the relationship you expect: a matrix tuned for 20 % identity will over-reward conservative substitutions between 90 %-identical sequences, and a matrix tuned for 90 % identity will fail to see that isoleucine and valine are nearly interchangeable in a remote homolog. Second, when a search fails with BLOSUM62, repeat it with BLOSUM45 and a lower gap-open penalty before concluding that no homolog exists; and when it succeeds with hundreds of near-identical hits, BLOSUM80 or PAM30 will sharpen the ranking among them.

4.6 Gap penalties

A gap of length k represents an indel of k residues. Under a linear penalty each gap position costs the same amount, d, so a gap of length k costs k·d. This is simple but biologically inappropriate: a single insertion of five residues is a single mutational event and is far more probable than five independent single-residue insertions, yet a linear scheme charges both the same. The standard remedy is the affine penalty introduced into dynamic programming by Gotoh:[3]

γ(k) = −o − (k − 1)·e o: gap-opening penalty (large); e: gap-extension penalty per additional position (small). BLASTp defaults with BLOSUM62: o = 11, e = 1. EMBOSS Needle/Water defaults: o = 10, e = 0.5.

With affine penalties the algorithm prefers one long gap to several short ones, which is what the biology suggests. The values of o and e are not independent of the matrix: a matrix in half-bits requires larger penalties than one in bits, and NCBI publishes, for each matrix, the gap pairs for which Karlin–Altschul parameters (Section 7.3) have been estimated. Two additional refinements are common. End-gap (terminal) penalties are often waived in semi-global alignment, which is the right mode for aligning a fragment to a full-length sequence or a target to a longer template. Position-specific gap penalties, which make gaps cheaper in loops than in secondary-structure elements, are used by ClustalW and by most structure-aware aligners (Section 8).

Key points — Section 4

  • Substitution scores are log-odds ratios: positive when a pair is over-represented in homologs, negative when under-represented; summing them over an alignment gives the log-odds of the alignment.
  • PAM extrapolates from close relatives (higher number = more distant); BLOSUM measures blocks directly (lower number = more distant). PAM160 ≈ BLOSUM62.
  • Affine gap penalties (open + extend) model a long indel as one event; default BLASTp scheme is BLOSUM62 with 11/1.
  • A raw score is only interpretable together with the matrix and gap scheme used.

Section 5Global alignment: the Needleman–Wunsch algorithm

5.1 Why enumeration is impossible

Given a scoring system, the best alignment is simply the one with the highest score. The naive approach — enumerate every alignment and keep the best — fails because the number of alignments grows explosively. 5.2 The dynamic-programming principle

Needleman and Wunsch (1970) observed that the optimal alignment of two sequences can be assembled from optimal alignments of their prefixes.[1] Consider the best alignment of s1..i with t1..j. Its last column must be one of three things: si aligned to tj, si aligned to a gap, or tj aligned to a gap. Whichever it is, the columns that precede it must form an optimal alignment of the corresponding shorter prefixes — otherwise we could improve the whole by improving the part. Writing F(i, j) for the best score of aligning the two prefixes, this gives the recurrence:

F(i, j) = max { F(i−1, j−1) + σ(si, tj) ;  F(i−1, j) + d ;  F(i, j−1) + d } with boundary conditions F(0, 0) = 0, F(i, 0) = i·d, F(0, j) = j·d, where d < 0 is the (linear) gap penalty. The three terms correspond to a diagonal move (match/mismatch), a vertical move (gap in t) and a horizontal move (gap in s).

The algorithm has three steps:

  1. Initialisation. Fill the first row and column with cumulative gap penalties.
  2. Matrix fill. Compute every cell from its three neighbours (upper-left, upper, left), row by row. Record for each cell which neighbour produced the maximum (the pointer).
  3. Traceback. The score of the optimal global alignment is F(n, m), in the bottom-right cell. Follow the pointers back to F(0, 0); a diagonal step emits a column of two residues, a vertical step emits si over a gap, a horizontal step emits a gap over tj.

Time and memory are both proportional to n·m — O(nm) — because each cell is computed once from a constant number of neighbours. With affine gap penalties, three matrices are needed instead of one (one for each type of move ending the alignment), but the complexity remains quadratic.[3]

Key points — Section 5

  • The number of possible alignments is astronomically large; dynamic programming finds the optimum in O(nm) by solving prefixes first.
  • Three steps: initialise the borders, fill each cell from three neighbours, trace back from the bottom-right corner.
  • Global alignment aligns every residue of both sequences; it is the right tool for full-length homologs and for target–template alignment.

Section 6Local alignment: the Smith–Waterman algorithm

Proteins are modular. A kinase domain may be fused to very different regulatory domains in different proteins; a transcription factor may share a DNA-binding domain with hundreds of proteins and nothing else. A global alignment of two multidomain proteins that share one domain would be forced to align the unrelated regions as well, accumulating mismatch and gap penalties until the shared domain's contribution is swamped. Local alignment addresses this by seeking the highest-scoring pair of subsequences, with no requirement that the flanks be aligned at all.

Smith and Waterman (1981) showed that two small modifications to the Needleman–Wunsch recurrence achieve this:[2]

H(i, j) = max { 0 ;  H(i−1, j−1) + σ(si, tj) ;  H(i−1, j) + d ;  H(i, j−1) + d } with H(i, 0) = H(0, j) = 0. The zero allows an alignment to start anywhere; the traceback begins at the highest cell in the matrix rather than at the corner and stops at the first zero, allowing the alignment to end anywhere.

For the zero to have its intended effect the scoring system must have a negative expected score for random pairs — otherwise long random alignments would accumulate positive scores and the "local" alignment would simply grow to cover everything. All log-odds matrices built from realistic target frequencies satisfy this condition, and it is also a requirement of the statistical theory in Section 7.3.

Note how the zeros in the first rows and columns and scattered throughout the matrix reset the score whenever the running total would become negative; each non-zero region of the matrix is a candidate local alignment, and the algorithm can report several non-overlapping ones (a feature exploited by BLAST, whose HSPs are precisely such regions).

Both Needleman–Wunsch and Smith–Waterman are optimal: they are guaranteed to return the highest-scoring alignment under the given scoring system. Their cost is O(nm) time per pair, which is negligible for two sequences but prohibitive for searching a query of 300 residues against the ~250 million sequences of UniProtKB or the hundreds of billions of nucleotides in GenBank. This is the problem that BLAST solves — at the price of optimality.

Section 7Database searching with BLAST

7.1 History and the family of programs

The Basic Local Alignment Search Tool was published by Altschul, Gish, Miller, Myers and Lipman in 1990, building on the earlier FASTA heuristic of Pearson and Lipman.[8],[12] The original program computed ungapped local alignments and, crucially, reported for each a p-value derived from the Karlin–Altschul theory published the same year.[11] Three milestones followed.

  • 1990 — BLAST. Ungapped alignments; statistical significance of each hit. The paper is among the most cited in all of science.[8]
  • 1997 — Gapped BLAST and PSI-BLAST. Gapped extension of hits, the two-hit seeding heuristic (roughly three times faster), and Position-Specific Iterated BLAST, which builds a profile from the first search and re-searches with it.[9]
  • 2009 — BLAST+. A complete rewrite in C++ with a modular architecture, faster database handling and the command-line tools (blastp, blastn, makeblastdb, …) used today.[10]

The program to use depends on the type of query and of database. Translated searches use the genetic code to compare nucleotides with proteins in all six reading frames (three on each strand), which is how one finds a protein-coding gene in an unannotated genome or a protein homolog in a transcriptome assembly.

7.2 The algorithm

BLAST rests on one empirical observation: two related sequences almost always share at least one short stretch of high-scoring identity or near-identity. If so, one can first look for such short "words" — a fast operation that can be done with a lookup table — and run the expensive alignment only where a word hit exists. The procedure, for BLASTp with default parameters, is as follows (Figure 5).

  1. Seeding. The query is decomposed into all overlapping words of length k (k = 3 for proteins, 11 for BLASTn, 28 for megaBLAST). For each query word, BLAST also generates the neighbourhood: every 3-letter word whose BLOSUM62 score against the query word is at least a threshold T (default 11). The protein neighbourhood is what gives BLASTp its sensitivity to conservative substitutions; nucleotide BLAST uses exact words only.
  2. Hit lookup. The database is scanned for exact occurrences of any neighbourhood word. Since 1997, an extension is triggered only when two non-overlapping hits occur on the same diagonal within a short distance (the two-hit method), which eliminates most spurious single-word hits.[9]
  3. Ungapped extension. Each triggering hit is extended in both directions, one residue at a time, as long as the cumulative score does not fall more than X below the best score seen so far. The result is a high-scoring segment pair (HSP): a maximal ungapped local alignment.
  4. Gapped extension. HSPs whose score exceeds a second threshold are subjected to a Smith–Waterman-type gapped extension, restricted to a band around the HSP, with affine penalties (11/1 by default).
  5. Statistics. Each resulting alignment is assigned a bit score and an E-value (Section 7.3); hits are ranked by E-value and those above the user-defined cut-off (default 10) are discarded.

7.3 Statistics: raw score, bit score and E-value

Consider the lecture's thought experiment. The 12-residue query LIAWHCMPNAAA has 12! ≈ 4.8 × 108 distinct permutations. If we shuffled it, aligned each shuffled version against the same database sequence and recorded the best local score, we would obtain the distribution of scores expected by chance for a sequence of this length and composition. The score of the real query can then be judged against that distribution. Figure 6 performs this experiment with real proteins.

Karlin and Altschul proved that, for ungapped local alignments of random sequences under a scoring system with negative expected score, the maximal score S follows an extreme-value distribution, and they derived its parameters analytically.[11] The practical consequence is a closed-form expression for the expected number of alignments with score ≥ S that would occur by chance:

E = K · m · n · e−λS m: length of the query; n: total length of the database (sum of all sequence lengths); K and λ: constants that depend on the scoring matrix, gap penalties and residue composition (for BLOSUM62 ungapped, λ ≈ 0.318 and K ≈ 0.134; the gapped values are estimated by simulation and tabulated by NCBI).

Three features of this formula are worth internalising. The E-value grows linearly with the query length and with the database size — the same alignment found in a database ten times larger is ten times more likely to be a chance event, which is why a hit's E-value changes when the database is updated. It decays exponentially with the score — a few extra points of score change the E-value by orders of magnitude. And the raw score S enters only through the product λS, which suggests normalising it. The bit score is this normalisation:

S′ = ( λS − ln K ) / ln 2    so that    E = m · n · 2−S′

Bit scores are comparable across searches that used different matrices and gap penalties, because λ and K have been absorbed. The formula also gives an intuition for their size: a bit score of S′ is significant when 2S′ is comfortably larger than m·n. For a 300-residue query against a database of 1011 residues, m·n ≈ 3 × 1013 ≈ 245, so bit scores above roughly 50 begin to be significant and above 100 are overwhelmingly so.

Finally, the relationship between E-value and p-value (the probability of at least one chance alignment with score ≥ S) follows from the Poisson distribution: p = 1 − e−E. For small values the two are numerically identical (E = 0.001 ⇒ p = 0.000999), which is why BLAST reports only E; for large values they diverge (E = 5 is possible, p = 5 is not). The lecture's shorthand that "an E-value of 0.05 means a 5 % chance of a random match" is therefore accurate to within 2.5 %.

Caveats that the formula does not capture

  • Low-complexity regions (poly-Q tracts, proline-rich linkers, transmembrane segments) violate the random-composition assumption and generate spurious significant hits. BLAST masks them by default (SEG for proteins, DUST for nucleotides); do not switch this off without reason.
  • Short queries cannot reach significant scores against large databases regardless of identity — a 15-residue peptide with a perfect match has a bit score of about 30. Use a smaller database, PAM30, and the short-query settings.
  • Composition bias (e.g. very acidic or very hydrophobic proteins) is partly corrected by the compositional adjustment that BLASTp applies by default since 2005.
  • A significant E-value establishes homology of the aligned region, not of the whole protein, and not identity of function. Always inspect coverage and the alignment.

7.4 Sensitivity, specificity and the trade-off in heuristics

Every search has a decision threshold (the E-value cut-off), and every threshold produces four kinds of outcome when compared with the truth about homology.

Truly homologousTruly unrelated
Reported as a hitTrue positive (TP)False positive (FP) — type I error
Not reportedFalse negative (FN) — type II errorTrue negative (TN)

Sensitivity (recall), TP / (TP + FN), is the fraction of true homologs that the search finds; specificity, TN / (TN + FP), is the fraction of unrelated sequences correctly excluded; precision, TP / (TP + FP), is the fraction of reported hits that are real. Lowering the E-value cut-off increases specificity and precision at the cost of sensitivity; raising it does the reverse. The heuristics of BLAST introduce an additional, independent loss of sensitivity: a true homolog that happens to share no word of score ≥ T with the query will never be extended and will be missed no matter how permissive the cut-off. This is why Smith–Waterman (e.g. SSEARCH in the FASTA package) finds a few per cent more remote homologs than BLASTp in benchmarks, and why profile methods (Section 7.5) find many more still. In practice, a false negative in a database search is far more common than a false positive with E < 10−3; the usual error of a beginner is to trust an absence of hits.

7.5 Position-specific scoring matrices and PSI-BLAST

A substitution matrix applies the same scores to every position of a protein. But a protein family does not behave this way: a catalytic aspartate is invariant, a buried leucine tolerates isoleucine and valine, and a surface loop tolerates almost anything. A position-specific scoring matrix (PSSM, or profile) captures this by storing, for every column of a multiple alignment, a score for each of the twenty amino acids.[31]

Worked example from the lecture

Consider five aligned 7-residue sequences:

D R S W L E I D K S W I D V E K T W L D I D K S W V E L E E S W L E I

The simplest profile records the frequency of each residue in each column: position 1 is D in 3/5 (0.6) and E in 2/5 (0.4); position 2 is K 0.6, R 0.2, E 0.2; position 3 is S 0.8, T 0.2; position 4 is W 1.0; position 5 is L 0.6, I 0.2, V 0.2; position 6 is E 0.6, D 0.4; position 7 is I 0.6, V 0.2, L 0.2. A new candidate sequence E R S W V E I scores, column by column, 0.4 + 0.2 + 0.8 + 1.0 + 0.2 + 0.6 + 0.6 = 3.8 out of a maximum of 7 (0.54 per position). Is this good? The sequence matches the invariant tryptophan, the near-invariant serine and the acidic character of positions 1 and 6, and it uses residues seen in the family at every position; a random 7-mer would score close to zero, because most of its residues would never have been observed in the corresponding column. The sequence is therefore a plausible family member, even though it is identical to none of the five.

Real PSSMs improve on raw frequencies in two ways. They are converted to log-odds against background frequencies (so that a tryptophan match, rare by chance, is rewarded more than an alanine match), and they add pseudocounts derived from the substitution matrix, so that a residue never observed in a column of five sequences receives a small, non-zero probability instead of an infinite penalty. The resulting matrix can be used in exactly the same way as BLOSUM62 — the recurrence of Section 6 simply looks up σ(position, residue) instead of σ(residue, residue).

PSI-BLAST

Position-Specific Iterated BLAST automates the construction and use of a PSSM:[9]

  1. Run a standard BLASTp with the query.
  2. Take all hits below an inclusion threshold (default E < 0.005), build a multiple alignment of them anchored on the query, and compute a PSSM of the query's length.
  3. Search the database again with the PSSM instead of the query sequence.
  4. Add the new significant hits to the alignment, rebuild the PSSM, and repeat until no new sequences are found (convergence) or a fixed number of iterations is reached.

Each iteration incorporates the variability of the family, so that the profile learns which positions may vary and which may not. PSI-BLAST detects roughly three times as many remote homologs as BLASTp at the same error rate, and it is the method with which, for example, most of the distant members of the GPCR and kinase superfamilies were first recognised. Its danger is profile drift: a single false positive included at iteration 2 will pull the profile toward an unrelated family and recruit its members at iteration 3. Inspect the hits added at each round, lower the inclusion threshold when the query contains repetitive or low-complexity segments, and treat a hit that appears only at iteration 5 with appropriate suspicion.

RPS-BLAST (Reverse Position-Specific BLAST) inverts the search: a single query sequence is compared against a database of precomputed PSSMs, one for each known domain family. This is the engine behind NCBI's Conserved Domain Database and the CD-Search service, which annotates the domain architecture of a query in seconds; it integrates Pfam, SMART, COG and NCBI-curated models.[25] Running CD-Search on a protein of unknown function is normally the first step after BLASTp.

Key points — Section 7

  • BLAST is a heuristic: word seeding → two-hit trigger → ungapped extension (HSP) → gapped extension → statistics. It is fast because most of the database is never aligned, and it may miss homologs for the same reason.
  • E = K m n e−λS: expected number of chance hits with at least this score; linear in query and database size, exponential in score.
  • Bit scores are normalised and comparable across scoring systems; E = m n 2−S′.
  • E < 10−3 is a reasonable single-search threshold for homology; always check coverage and low-complexity masking.
  • PSSMs score each position differently; PSI-BLAST builds them iteratively and detects remote homologs at the risk of profile drift.

Section 8Multiple sequence alignment

8.1 Purpose

A multiple sequence alignment (MSA) arranges three or more sequences so that homologous positions share a column. Compared with a collection of pairwise alignments it offers qualitatively new information: a position conserved across fifty sequences spanning a billion years of evolution is almost certainly structurally or functionally essential, whereas conservation between two sequences may be chance; a gap present in one lineage but absent in all others can be polarised as an insertion; and covariation between two columns suggests that the residues are in physical contact. MSAs are the input for phylogenetic reconstruction, for building PSSMs and hidden Markov models (Pfam), for identifying catalytic and binding residues, for distinguishing orthologs from paralogs within a family, and — since 2020 — for the coevolution-based structure prediction of AlphaFold and related methods.[30]

The cost is therefore proportional to LN cells, each requiring 2N operations, where L is the sequence length. Memory grows at the same rate. Exact MSA is thus feasible for three sequences, marginal for four or five and impossible beyond; the problem is NP-hard under the sum-of-pairs score. All practical methods are heuristics, and the dominant one since 1987 is progressive alignment, introduced by Feng and Doolittle and popularised by the Clustal programs.[16],[17]

  1. Pairwise distances. All N(N−1)/2 pairs are aligned (quickly, often with a k-mer heuristic rather than full dynamic programming) and converted to a distance.
  2. Guide tree. A tree is built from the distance matrix. It is not a phylogeny — it is a schedule — but its topology determines the result.
  3. Progressive alignment. Following the tree from the leaves to the root, sequences are aligned to sequences, sequences to profiles, and profiles to profiles, using a global dynamic-programming alignment in which the score of a column is averaged over all pairs of residues it contains (sum of pairs). Once two sequences have been aligned, their relative gaps are frozen: once a gap, always a gap.

The frozen-gap rule is the heuristic's strength — it makes the problem polynomial — and its weakness: an error made when aligning the first two sequences, which are aligned with the least information, can never be corrected. The modern programs differ mainly in how they mitigate this.

Practical cautions

  • An MSA is a hypothesis. Columns in highly variable regions and near gaps are frequently wrong; downstream analyses (phylogeny, conservation scores) should either trim those columns (trimAl, Gblocks) or weight them down.
  • The composition of the sequence set matters as much as the program. Fifty near-identical mammalian orthologs contribute less information than ten sequences spanning vertebrates, invertebrates and plants; sequence redundancy is normally reduced (CD-HIT, UniRef) before alignment.
  • Always inspect the alignment in a viewer (Jalview, AliView). A one-residue shift of a secondary-structure element is immediately visible to the eye and invisible in a score.

Section 9From alignment to structure: what the next lectures will assume

The lectures that follow — protein structure prediction, docking, drug design and molecular dynamics — all begin from sequence comparisons made with the tools of this unit. Three empirical relationships justify that dependence.

9.1 Sequence identity predicts structural similarity

Chothia and Lesk compared pairs of homologous proteins of known structure and found that the root-mean-square deviation of their common core increases smoothly, approximately exponentially, with sequence divergence: pairs above 50 % identity superimpose to within about 1 Å, while pairs near 20 % identity diverge to 2–2.5 Å and share a shrinking core.[22] This relationship is the theoretical basis of homology (comparative) modelling: if a template of known structure can be found with sufficient identity to the target, the target's backbone can be built on the template's coordinates, and the expected accuracy of the model can be estimated from the identity alone. The quality of the alignment between target and template sets a hard ceiling on the quality of the model, because a residue aligned to the wrong template position will be placed in the wrong location in space no matter how well the side chains are optimised. Target–template alignments are therefore built with the most information available — PSI-BLAST or HMM profiles, secondary-structure prediction, and manual inspection — rather than with a single pairwise alignment.

9.2 The twilight zone

Sander and Schneider, and later Rost, quantified the identity below which sequence alignment can no longer be trusted to indicate common structure.[23],[24] The threshold depends on alignment length: for alignments of more than about 100 residues, above ~30–35 % identity nearly all pairs share the same fold; between ~20 % and ~35 % — the twilight zone — true homologs and chance alignments overlap and sequence identity alone cannot discriminate them; below ~20 % (the "midnight zone") pairwise sequence methods are essentially uninformative even though many such pairs are genuinely homologous, as the globin family demonstrates. The practical consequences are that identity thresholds must always be read together with alignment length, that a BLAST E-value is a far better guide than a percentage, and that in the twilight zone one should move to profile- and HMM-based methods (PSI-BLAST, HHpred, HMMER) or to structure-based comparison. The success of AlphaFold rests on the same principle taken to its conclusion: a deep MSA of a protein family, built with the methods of Section 8, contains enough covariation signal to infer the residue contacts that define the fold.[30]

9.3 Conservation locates function

Residues that bind a ligand, catalyse a reaction or stabilise the fold are under purifying selection and appear as conserved columns in an MSA. Mapping column conservation (as computed, for instance, by ConSurf or by the Shannon entropy of each column) onto a three-dimensional structure typically highlights the active site and the binding pockets while the surface remains variable. In the docking and drug-design lectures this is used in two ways: to identify or validate the binding site of a target when no ligand-bound structure exists, and to assess selectivity — a drug that contacts residues conserved across a protein family will inhibit all its members, whereas a drug that exploits residues variable between paralogs (or between a pathogen enzyme and its human ortholog) can be made selective. Conversely, when a mutation is observed in a patient or arises in a pathogen under drug pressure, the MSA tells us immediately whether the affected position tolerates variation; this is how resistance mutations are prioritised for the mutational studies performed with molecular dynamics.

    suma <- suma + 2^k * choose(m, k) * choose(n, k)
  }
  return(suma)
}

10.3 A minimal homology-search protocol

  1. Obtain the query in FASTA format and check it: length, composition, presence of low-complexity or transmembrane segments.
  2. Run BLASTp against UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot first (small, curated, every hit is annotated) and then against the non-redundant database (nr) or the PDB as needed.
  3. Sort by E-value; for each hit of interest, record E-value, bit score, percent identity, alignment length and query coverage.
  4. Decide whether the relationship is full-length (coverage > 80 %) or domain-level; for the latter, run CD-Search to name the domains.
  5. If no hit has E < 10−3, run PSI-BLAST (3–5 iterations, inspecting the newly added hits) or HHpred before concluding that the protein has no known homolog.
  6. For functional transfer, verify that the best hit is a plausible ortholog (reciprocal best hit, consistent species distribution) and not a paralog with a different function.
  7. Record the database version and the date: E-values and hit lists are not reproducible otherwise.

Section 11Self-assessment

Attempt each question before opening the answer.

1. A colleague reports that two proteins are "45 % homologous". What is wrong with the statement, and how should it be rephrased?

Homology is the inference of common ancestry and is a yes/no property; the quantity that can be 45 % is identity (or similarity) over an alignment. Rephrase as "the proteins share 45 % sequence identity over an alignment of N residues, which indicates that they are homologous" — and report the alignment length and E-value, because 45 % over 20 residues and 45 % over 300 residues have very different meanings.

2. Human haemoglobin α and chimpanzee haemoglobin α; human haemoglobin α and human haemoglobin β; human haemoglobin α and chimpanzee haemoglobin β. Classify each pair.

Orthologs (separated by the human–chimpanzee speciation); paralogs (separated by the α/β duplication); paralogs (the last common ancestor of the two genes is the α/β duplication, which predates the speciation — the fact that they are in different species does not make them orthologs).

3. Under the 4 × 4 matrix of Section 4.1, a gap penalty of −5, and the alignment of Section 4.7, the score was 1. Recompute the score with an affine scheme: open −5, extend −1.

The single gap of length 3 now costs −5 − 2 × 1 = −7 instead of −15, so the score becomes 1 + 15 − 7 = 9. The identities and mismatches contribute the same: −3 + 7 + 10 + 7 − 4 + 0 − 1 + 0 = 16; 16 − 7 = 9.

4. Why does the BLOSUM62 diagonal give W a score of 11 but L a score of 4, when both are conserved when matched?

The score is a log-odds ratio against the background. Tryptophan is rare (~1 % of residues), so two tryptophans aligned by chance are very unlikely and a W–W match is strong evidence of homology. Leucine is the most abundant residue (~10 %), so L–L matches arise often by chance and carry less information.

5. In the Needleman–Wunsch matrix of Section 5.3, compute F(3, 4) from its neighbours and state which move produced it.

Row 3 is T (third residue of GATTACA), column 4 is T (fourth residue of GCATGCG). Candidates: diagonal F(2, 3) + σ(T, T) = 1 + 1 = 2; up F(2, 4) + (−1) = 0 − 1 = −1; left F(3, 3) + (−1) = 0 − 1 = −1. F(3, 4) = 2, from the diagonal (a match).

6. What single change converts the Needleman–Wunsch recurrence into Smith–Waterman, and what two consequences does it have for the traceback?

Adding 0 as a fourth candidate in the maximum (and setting the first row and column to 0). The traceback starts at the highest cell of the matrix rather than the bottom-right corner, and it stops at the first cell with value 0 rather than at the origin; the alignment may therefore start and end anywhere.

7. A BLASTp hit has E = 2 × 10−8 against Swiss-Prot. You repeat the search against nr, which is about 400 times larger, and find the same alignment. What E-value do you expect, approximately, and why?

About 400 × 2 × 10−8 ≈ 8 × 10−6, because E is proportional to the database length n; the bit score is unchanged. The hit remains significant, but this illustrates why E-values are not comparable across databases while bit scores are.

8. A bit score of 40 is obtained for a 200-residue query against a database of 5 × 1010 residues. Estimate E.

E = m n 2−S′ = 200 × 5 × 1010 × 2−40 = 1013 / 1.1 × 1012 ≈ 9. About nine alignments this good are expected by chance; the hit is not significant.

9. Using the frequency PSSM of Section 7.5, score the sequence D K S W L E I and the sequence A A A A A A A, and explain the difference.

D K S W L E I: 0.6 + 0.6 + 0.8 + 1.0 + 0.6 + 0.6 + 0.6 = 4.8 / 7 (it is the consensus sequence and obtains the maximum possible score). A A A A A A A: 0 at every position, total 0 / 7, because alanine was never observed in any column. A real log-odds PSSM with pseudocounts would give the alanine sequence a strongly negative rather than zero score.

10. Why can a true homolog be missed by BLASTp even with the E-value cut-off set to 10?

Because the cut-off is applied only to alignments that were actually computed. If the two sequences share no 3-letter word with neighbourhood score ≥ T (or no pair of such words on one diagonal), no extension is triggered and the alignment is never produced. This is a sensitivity loss of the heuristic, independent of the statistics; Smith–Waterman (SSEARCH) or profile methods would be needed.

11. Explain "once a gap, always a gap" and name two strategies that modern aligners use to mitigate it.

In progressive alignment, once two sequences (or profiles) are aligned, the gaps placed between them are fixed and cannot be moved when further sequences are added, so errors made early propagate. Mitigations: iterative refinement (MAFFT, MUSCLE — realign subgroups after the first pass and keep improvements) and consistency-based scoring (T-Coffee, ProbCons — use a library of all pairwise alignments to inform each progressive step).

12. You want to model the 3D structure of a 320-residue protein. BLASTp against the PDB returns a template with 28 % identity over 300 residues (E = 10−20) and another with 65 % identity over 90 residues (E = 10−30). Discuss.

The second hit has the lower E-value but covers only 28 % of the target — it is a template for one domain. The first covers almost the whole protein but is in the twilight zone, where the alignment will contain errors and the expected model accuracy (by Chothia–Lesk) is around 2 Å or worse in the core, with unreliable loops. A sound strategy would be to use the first as the global template, improve the alignment with profile methods (PSI-BLAST/HHpred) and secondary-structure prediction, and use the second to refine the domain it covers; or, today, to compare with an AlphaFold model, whose MSA-based inference is designed for precisely this regime.

AppendixGlossary

Affine gap penalty
Gap cost of the form −o − (k−1)e, with a large opening cost o and a small extension cost e; models an indel of k residues as a single event.
Bit score (S′)
Raw alignment score normalised by the Karlin–Altschul parameters λ and K, in units of bits; comparable across scoring systems.
BLOSUM
BLOcks SUbstitution Matrix; log-odds matrices measured from ungapped conserved blocks clustered at a given identity (BLOSUM62: 62 %). Lower number = more divergent.
Coverage
Fraction of the query (or subject) included in the alignment.
Dynamic programming
Algorithmic technique that solves a problem by combining optimal solutions of its sub-problems; the basis of Needleman–Wunsch and Smith–Waterman.
E-value
Expected number of alignments with a score at least as high as the observed one that would arise by chance in a search of this query against this database.
Extreme-value (Gumbel) distribution
Distribution of the maximum of many random variables; the distribution followed by optimal local alignment scores of random sequences.
Global alignment
Alignment of two sequences over their entire lengths (Needleman–Wunsch).
Homology
Descent from a common ancestor. Binary; inferred, not measured.
HSP
High-scoring segment pair; a maximal ungapped local alignment produced by BLAST's extension step.
Identity
Percentage of aligned columns containing identical residues.
Indel
Insertion or deletion; represented by a gap. Pairwise alignment cannot distinguish the two.
Local alignment
Alignment of the highest-scoring subsequences of two sequences, ignoring the flanks (Smith–Waterman, BLAST).
Log-odds score
Logarithm of the ratio between the probability of an observation under the hypothesis of homology and under the hypothesis of chance.
MSA
Multiple sequence alignment; alignment of three or more sequences.
Ortholog
Homolog in another species, separated by speciation.
PAM
Point Accepted Mutation; Dayhoff's evolutionary-model matrices. 1 PAM = 1 accepted mutation per 100 residues. Higher number = more divergent.
Paralog
Homolog separated by gene duplication.
Progressive alignment
MSA heuristic that aligns sequences in the order dictated by a guide tree, freezing gaps once placed.
PSSM / profile
Position-specific scoring matrix: for each column of an alignment, a score for each residue type.
Sensitivity
Fraction of true homologs detected (TP / (TP + FN)).
Specificity
Fraction of non-homologs correctly rejected (TN / (TN + FP)).
Similarity
Percentage of aligned columns with identical or positively scoring residue pairs.
Transition / transversion
Nucleotide substitution within the purine or pyrimidine class / between classes.
Twilight zone
Range of ~20–35 % sequence identity in which sequence similarity alone cannot reliably distinguish homologs from unrelated pairs.
Word (k-mer)
Substring of fixed length k used by BLAST to seed alignments.
