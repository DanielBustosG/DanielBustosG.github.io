This document expands the third lecture of the module. Unit 2 ended with a structure — experimental or predicted — and the warning that its quality bounds everything downstream. This unit uses that structure to answer a new question: given a small molecule, where and how does it bind, and how tightly? The answer comes from two separable machines, a search that generates candidate poses and a scoring function that ranks them, and most of what a practitioner needs to know is which of the two is failing. To make that concrete, Section 7 reports a complete re-docking experiment performed for this document — indinavir into HIV-1 protease (PDB 1HSG) with AutoDock Vina — whose outcome is instructive precisely because one of the five runs fails.

Learning objectives

  • Use the vocabulary of docking correctly: ligand, receptor, binding site, pose, binding mode, scoring function, ranking.
  • Explain binding thermodynamics well enough to know what a docking score does and does not represent (ΔG, Kd, enthalpy–entropy, desolvation).
  • Prepare a receptor and a ligand: protonation states, tautomers, waters, partial charges, rotatable bonds, box definition.
  • Quantify the combinatorial size of the search and describe systematic, incremental and stochastic strategies, including how a genetic algorithm works.
  • Distinguish rigid, flexible-ligand, induced-fit and ensemble docking, and choose between them for a given target.
  • Classify scoring functions into force-field, empirical, knowledge-based and machine-learned families, and state the known failure modes of each.
  • Validate a docking protocol by re-docking, compute symmetry-corrected RMSD and interpret the 2 Å criterion.
  • Diagnose whether a docking failure comes from preparation, search or scoring.
  • Design a virtual screening campaign and evaluate it with enrichment factor, ROC-AUC and BEDROC.

Section 1Vocabulary

The terms below are used precisely in the literature, and confusing two of them is the commonest source of misunderstanding in a docking report.

Ligand
The molecule being placed — usually a small organic compound (< ~700 g mol⁻¹), but also a peptide, a metabolite or a fragment.
Receptor (target)
The macromolecule that provides the site — usually a protein, sometimes DNA, RNA or a membrane protein assembly.
Binding site
The set of residues that physically contact the ligand, conventionally those with any heavy atom within 4–6 Å of a ligand heavy atom. An orthosteric site is the one occupied by the natural substrate or agonist; an allosteric site is elsewhere and modulates activity at a distance; a cryptic site is not visible in the apo structure and opens only on ligand binding.
Pose
One candidate placement: a position, an orientation and an internal conformation of the ligand. A docking run produces many poses.
Binding mode
The correct pose — the orientation and conformation that the complex actually adopts, as determined experimentally. A pose is a hypothesis; a binding mode is a fact.
Scoring function
The function that assigns a number to a pose, intended to correlate with binding free energy.
Ranking
Ordering of poses (pose ranking) or of different ligands (affinity ranking) by score. The two tasks are different and a program may be good at one and poor at the other.
Docking
The computational procedure that samples poses and scores them.

Three tasks, three different success criteria

Docking literature evaluates three distinct abilities, and papers that conflate them are the reason docking has a mixed reputation.

  • Pose prediction (docking power): can the program reproduce a known crystallographic binding mode? Measured by RMSD, success threshold 2 Å. Modern programs succeed in roughly 60–80 % of cases on curated benchmarks.[20]
  • Affinity prediction (scoring power): does the score correlate with measured ΔG for a series of complexes? Pearson r is typically 0.4–0.6 on the CASF benchmark — enough to separate strong from weak binders, far from enough to rank analogues differing by a factor of three.[20],[21]
  • Virtual screening (screening power): is the score able to place true actives above decoys? Measured by enrichment and ROC-AUC (Section 8). This is the task docking is actually good at, and it only requires the ranking to be right on average.

Section 2What docking is and why it matters

Molecular docking predicts the preferred geometry of a complex between two molecules. The classical image is Emil Fischer's 1894 lock and key: a rigid ligand whose shape complements a rigid site. It is a useful first approximation and the basis of the earliest docking program, DOCK (Kuntz et al., 1982), which matched ligand atoms onto a negative image of the cavity built from overlapping spheres.[1] But proteins are not rigid. Koshland's induced fit (1958) describes a site that reorganises on binding, and the modern conformational selection view says that the apo protein already visits the bound conformation as part of its thermal ensemble and the ligand merely selects and stabilises it — the two basins of the energy landscape of Unit 2. The lecture's "hand in glove" analogy captures this: both partners adjust. Which model a docking program implements determines which errors it makes.

Two applications justify the effort.

  • Understanding molecular recognition. Cellular signalling depends on which molecules associate, in what orientation and how strongly; the orientation itself can determine the biological outcome, since an agonist and an antagonist may occupy the same pocket and differ only in which residues they contact and which conformational change they stabilise. Docking provides testable hypotheses about those contacts — hypotheses that mutagenesis can falsify.
  • Structure-based drug design. Given a validated target structure, docking is used to (i) screen compound libraries of 10⁵–10⁹ molecules for candidates worth buying and testing, (ii) explain the structure–activity relationship of a series and suggest which substituent to modify, and (iii) propose selectivity-determining contacts between a target and its off-target paralogues. The economics are the point: a docking run costs seconds of CPU per molecule, a biochemical assay costs orders of magnitude more per molecule, and a clinical candidate costs on the order of 10⁹ US dollars. Docking does not replace experiment; it decides which experiments to run first. Unit 4 develops this into a full design cycle.

Well-documented successes include HIV-1 protease inhibitors (the system used in Section 7), neuraminidase inhibitors, several kinase inhibitors, and — in the ultra-large library era — ligands for the D4 dopamine receptor and the melatonin receptors discovered by docking hundreds of millions of compounds.[22],[23] Equally well documented are the failures, and the rest of this unit is largely about recognising them before they reach a publication.

Section 3The physics that a score tries to approximate

Binding is an equilibrium: R + L ⇌ RL, with dissociation constant Kd = [R][L]/[RL]. The standard free energy of binding is

ΔG° = −RT ln(1/Kd · c°) = ΔH° − TΔS° R = 1.987 cal mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; at T = 298 K, RT = 0.593 kcal mol⁻¹ and a factor of ten in Kd corresponds to 1.36 kcal mol⁻¹. Thus Kd = 1 µM ⇒ ΔG ≈ −8.2 kcal mol⁻¹; Kd = 1 nM ⇒ ΔG ≈ −12.3 kcal mol⁻¹.

Two consequences deserve emphasis. First, the whole pharmacologically relevant range — millimolar fragment to picomolar drug — spans about 15 kcal mol⁻¹, and distinguishing a 10 nM from a 100 nM compound requires an accuracy of 1.4 kcal mol⁻¹, which is below the error of any scoring function and comparable to the thermal energy RT. Second, ΔG is a difference between the bound and the free states, so it includes contributions that a single pose cannot show: the ligand and the site must each be stripped of their ordered water (desolvation, a large positive enthalpy for polar groups), the ligand loses translational, rotational and torsional entropy on binding (roughly 0.5–0.7 kcal mol⁻¹ per frozen rotatable bond), and the protein may pay a reorganisation cost. A pose that looks perfect can still correspond to weak binding because the price of desolvating it exceeds the reward of its contacts.

In a real complex these interactions are summed over dozens of atom pairs. The indinavir complex analysed in Section 7 illustrates the mixture: the drug makes nine short polar contacts, including the hydroxyl that bridges both catalytic aspartates of the protease dimer, while most of its surface — the phenyl, indanyl, pyridyl and tert-butyl groups — makes hydrophobic contact with valines, isoleucines and alanines on both subunits.

Section 4Preparation: where most errors are introduced

4.1 Receptor preparation

  1. Choose the structure and the state. Prefer an experimental structure of adequate resolution (≤ 2.5 Å) with a ligand of the same chemotype bound; prefer holo over apo when the site is known to reorganise. Check the Unit 2 criteria: resolution, R-free, B-factors of the site, residues missing from REMARK 465, and the validation report's ligand fit. An AlphaFold model may be used when nothing else exists, but remember that it is apo, single-state and ligand-free (Unit 2, Section 10.3).
  2. Select chains and the biological assembly. A crystallographic asymmetric unit is not necessarily the biological unit. HIV-1 protease is an obligate homodimer and its site lies on the dimer axis: docking into a single chain is meaningless. Conversely, docking into a site formed by a crystallographic contact is a classic artefact.
  3. Decide about waters. The default is to remove them, because a water in the way of the ligand blocks it. But conserved, buried, well-ordered waters that bridge ligand and protein are part of the site. The canonical example is the flap water of HIV-1 protease, which accepts hydrogen bonds from both flap Ile50 amides and donates to two carbonyls of the inhibitor; it is present in almost every protease–inhibitor structure, and keeping it improves re-docking (Section 7).[26] Criteria for keeping a water: buried, B-factor comparable to the protein, present in several independent structures, and making ≥ 2 hydrogen bonds to the protein.
  4. Add hydrogens and assign protonation. X-ray structures have no hydrogens. Most positions are unambiguous; the ones that are not are exactly the ones that matter — Asp/Glu (could be protonated in a buried, low-dielectric site), His (which of the two tautomers, or doubly protonated), Cys (thiol or thiolate), Lys (rarely neutral), and the orientation of Asn/Gln amides and His rings, which X-ray cannot distinguish because N and O have almost the same electron count. Use PROPKA/PDB2PQR[33] or H++, and check the result by eye against the hydrogen-bond network. In HIV-1 protease, the catalytic dyad Asp25/Asp25′ is widely modelled with one aspartate protonated when a hydroxyl-containing inhibitor is bound, and the choice changes the score.
  5. Assign partial charges and atom types as required by the program (Gasteiger charges for AutoDock-family programs; force-field charges for others).
  6. Keep cofactors, metals and structural ions that form part of the site, with the correct charge and coordination geometry. Zinc, magnesium and nickel sites are handled poorly by generic scoring functions and may need specialised parameters.

4.2 Ligand preparation

A docking program needs a chemically sensible three-dimensional molecule, not a drawing. The steps are:

  • From 2D to 3D. Generate coordinates from SMILES with a conformer generator (RDKit ETKDG, OMEGA, CORINA) and relax with a force field. This starting conformer is not innocuous: most programs keep ring conformations, bond lengths and bond angles fixed and vary only the declared rotatable torsions, so the input conformer silently fixes part of the answer. Section 7 quantifies this with two conformers of the same molecule that give completely different results.
  • Stereochemistry. Every undefined stereocentre doubles the number of molecules to dock. Enumerate them explicitly; never let the program choose.
  • Protonation and tautomers at the assay pH. A carboxylic acid is ionised at pH 7.4, an aliphatic amine is protonated, an imidazole may be either; a 2-hydroxypyridine may exist as the pyridone. Each form has different hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors and therefore a different pose. The lecture gives the two acceptable strategies: dock the single form expected at physiological pH, or enumerate all plausible forms, dock each and keep the best — stating which was done.
  • Partial charges and rotatable-bond definition. Amide bonds are usually treated as non-rotatable; terminal groups such as methyl and hydroxyl may be ignored to save degrees of freedom.
  • Sanity filters for libraries: remove salts and solvents, keep one form per molecule, apply drug-likeness or lead-likeness rules if appropriate, and remove known assay-interfering substructures (PAINS) before, not after, docking.

4.3 Defining the binding site and the box

Docking programs search inside a user-defined region. Three situations arise:

  1. A ligand is already bound in the structure or in a homologous structure: centre the box on it. This is the re-docking or cross-docking situation and is the only one in which a protocol can be validated (Section 7).
  2. The site is known from biochemistry (catalytic residues, mutagenesis): centre the box on the centroid of those residues.
  3. The site is unknown. Use a cavity-detection algorithm — fpocket, SiteMap, CASTp, DoGSiteScorer — which rank surface pockets by volume, enclosure and hydrophobicity, or perform blind docking over the whole protein with a large box. Blind docking is much less reliable: the search space is larger, the scoring function has more opportunities to be fooled, and the result must be treated as a hypothesis to be tested by site-directed mutagenesis.

The box should enclose the site with a margin of roughly 5–8 Å beyond the expected ligand extent; too small truncates poses, too large degrades both speed and accuracy because the search has more decoy space to explore. In Section 7 the box is 26 × 22 × 22 Å centred on the crystallographic ligand, whose own extent is 14.9 × 10.2 × 8.6 Å.

Section 5Search algorithms

5.1 The size of the problem

Placing a rigid body requires six degrees of freedom — three translations and three rotations. Each rotatable bond in the ligand adds one torsional degree of freedom, and allowing side chains of the site to move adds several more. If each torsion is sampled in steps of θ degrees, a ligand with N rotatable bonds has (360/θ)N conformations, before the six rigid-body degrees of freedom are even considered.

Exhaustive enumeration is therefore out of the question for anything larger than a fragment, exactly as in the protein-folding case of Unit 2. Every practical algorithm either reduces the dimensionality (dock rigid conformers generated beforehand) or samples intelligently (incremental construction, stochastic optimisation).

5.2 Rigid docking: shape complementarity

In rigid docking the ligand conformation is fixed during the run; ligand flexibility is handled by pre-generating many conformers and docking each separately. The original DOCK algorithm — described in the lecture — constructs a negative image of the cavity as a set of overlapping spheres whose centres fill the empty space, then matches ligand atoms to sphere centres so that the inter-atomic distances reproduce the inter-centre distances within a tolerance, orients the ligand by least-squares superposition of the matched pairs, rejects placements with steric clashes, and scores the rest.[1] New orientations come from new atom–sphere matchings, exhaustively. The approach is fast, it is still the basis of shape-based methods (and of protein–protein docking by Fourier correlation, Section 9), and its weakness is obvious: a ligand whose bound conformation is not in the pre-generated set can never be placed correctly.

5.3 Flexible-ligand docking

Flexible-ligand docking, the standard today, generates ligand conformations during the run. Two strategies dominate.

Incremental construction (FlexX, DOCK 4+, Glide's hierarchical funnel, Surflex) splits the ligand at its rotatable bonds, docks a rigid base fragment chosen for its ability to make specific interactions, and then adds the remaining fragments one at a time, enumerating their torsions and keeping the best partial solutions at each stage. It is deterministic and fast, and its failure mode is an early commitment to the wrong anchor placement.

Stochastic optimisation (AutoDock, AutoDock Vina, GOLD, rDock, ICM) samples the pose space with randomness: Monte Carlo with simulated annealing, genetic algorithms, Lamarckian genetic algorithms (a GA whose offspring are locally energy-minimised, with the minimised coordinates written back into the genome), particle swarms, or — in Vina — a Monte Carlo chain of randomised steps each followed by a quasi-Newton local optimisation, run several times in parallel.[2],[3],[4] Stochastic methods find good solutions in minutes but give a different answer each run unless the random seed is fixed, and their thoroughness is controlled by a parameter — exhaustiveness in Vina, the number of GA runs in AutoDock — that must be raised for ligands with many rotatable bonds.

5.4 How a genetic algorithm works

The lecture describes the genetic algorithm used by GOLD and AutoDock; it is worth stating precisely, since it is the clearest example of a stochastic search.

  1. Encoding. A pose is represented as a chromosome: a string of genes holding the translation (3 numbers), the orientation (4 numbers as a quaternion, or 3 Euler angles), and one gene per rotatable torsion. GOLD adds genes that pair ligand and protein hydrogen-bonding groups.
  2. Initial population. A few hundred chromosomes are generated at random inside the box.
  3. Fitness. Each chromosome is decoded into coordinates and scored; the score is its fitness.
  4. Selection. Parents are drawn preferentially from the fitter half (roulette-wheel or tournament selection).
  5. Crossover. Two parents exchange blocks of genes — for example the translation of one with the torsions of the other — producing children that combine a good position with a good conformation.
  6. Mutation. Genes are perturbed at random with small probability, which is what allows the population to escape a local minimum.
  7. Replacement and iteration. The children replace the least fit members, and steps 3–6 repeat for a fixed number of generations or until the best fitness stops improving.

The Lamarckian variant of AutoDock inserts a local minimisation after decoding and writes the optimised values back into the chromosome, so that acquired improvements are inherited. In practice this converges with far fewer energy evaluations than a pure GA.[2]

5.5 Receptor flexibility: induced fit and ensembles

Treating the receptor as rigid is the strongest approximation in routine docking, and it is often the reason a known active fails to dock. Four levels of treatment exist, in increasing cost.

Ensemble docking deserves the emphasis the lecture gives it. Instead of one structure, one uses several — crystal structures of the same protein with different ligands, NMR models, frames extracted from a molecular dynamics trajectory (Unit 5), or alternative AlphaFold predictions — docks every ligand into every receptor, and keeps for each ligand its best score across the ensemble.[12],[13] This is a direct implementation of conformational selection and it recovers actives that any single structure misses. Its cost is that each additional receptor also gives decoys a new chance to score well, so enrichment does not always improve; the ensemble should be chosen deliberately (distinct, experimentally supported states) rather than by taking arbitrary trajectory snapshots.

The lecture's slide also notes that AlphaFold ranks kinase models across the inactive and active basins — a reminder that predicted structures can themselves supply an ensemble, provided the states are checked against what is known about the target.

Key points — Section 5

  • Search space = 6 rigid-body + N torsional degrees of freedom; exhaustive enumeration scales as (360/θ)N.
  • Rigid docking fixes the conformer; flexible-ligand docking builds it incrementally or samples it stochastically.
  • Genetic algorithms encode a pose as a chromosome and evolve a population by selection, crossover and mutation; Lamarckian GAs add local minimisation.
  • Stochastic runs are not reproducible unless the seed is fixed, and their thoroughness is a parameter (exhaustiveness) that must scale with ligand flexibility.
  • Receptor flexibility is the biggest remaining approximation; ensemble docking is the practical compromise.

Section 6Scoring functions

6.1 Four families

A scoring function must be evaluated millions of times per run, so it trades rigour for speed. The lecture's classification is the standard one.[14]

Consensus scoring combines several functions — by rank, vote or normalised score — on the assumption that their errors are partly independent. It typically improves screening enrichment modestly and is cheap, since the expensive step is pose generation.[15] It does not improve pose prediction much and it cannot fix a systematic error shared by all the functions.

6.2 A concrete function: AutoDock Vina

Because Section 7 uses it, the Vina function is worth writing out. It is empirical and additive over ligand–protein heavy-atom pairs, with each term a simple function of the surface distance dij = rij − Ri − Rj (the interatomic distance minus the two van der Waals radii):[3]

c = Σi<j [ −0.0356 · gauss1 − 0.00516 · gauss2 + 0.840 · repulsion + (−0.0351) · hydrophobic + (−0.587) · H-bond ] gauss1 = e−(d/0.5)²; gauss2 = e−((d−3)/2)²; repulsion = d² if d < 0, else 0; hydrophobic = a linear ramp from 1 to 0 between d = 0.5 and 1.5 Å for carbon/halogen pairs; H-bond = a ramp from 1 to 0 between d = −0.7 and 0 Å for donor–acceptor pairs. The reported affinity is the inter-molecular part divided by (1 + 0.0585 · Nrot), an entropic penalty for the rotatable bonds frozen on binding.

Three features are typical of the whole empirical family. There is no explicit electrostatic term — charge interactions are absorbed into the hydrogen-bond and steric terms, which is why Vina handles metals and highly charged sites poorly. There is no desolvation term. And the score is reported in kcal mol⁻¹, which invites the reader to interpret it as a free energy; the weights were fitted to reproduce affinities of a training set, so the units are meaningful on average and misleading in any individual case.

6.3 What scoring functions cannot do

Known limitations, with their consequences

  • Entropy is approximated by a count. Torsional entropy enters as a penalty per rotatable bond; the translational and rotational entropy of binding and the entropy of released water are essentially absent.
  • Desolvation is poorly modelled, so polar and charged ligands are usually overrated and the burial of a charged group is not penalised as it should be.
  • Scores grow with molecular size. More atoms make more contacts; nearly every scoring function therefore prefers bigger molecules, independently of their real affinity. Section 7.3 measures this bias and shows the standard correction, ligand efficiency = −ΔG/heavy atom.[16]
  • Protein reorganisation and the cost of the ligand's bound conformation are usually ignored.
  • Water-mediated interactions are missed unless the relevant waters are kept explicitly.
  • Consequence.[24] Treat a docking score as a classifier — likely binder / unlikely binder — and never as a measured affinity. Do not report a score as "predicted ΔG" or convert it to a Kd. Do not compare scores obtained with different programs, receptors or box definitions. For quantitative estimates, use the more expensive methods of Unit 5 (MM/PBSA, MM/GBSA, free-energy perturbation).

Section 7Validation: a re-docking experiment performed for this document

A docking protocol is only credible if it can reproduce a known answer. The standard test is re-docking: take a crystal structure of a complex, remove the ligand, dock it back, and measure how far the predicted pose is from the experimental one. The conventional success criterion is an RMSD below 2.0 Å, roughly the resolution at which a ligand's orientation can be considered correctly reproduced.[17] The experiment reported here was run specifically for this document; every number is reproducible with the code in Section 11.

7.1 The system

The target is HIV-1 protease in complex with indinavir (PDB 1HSG, X-ray, 2.0 Å), the structure that supported the development of one of the first antiretroviral drugs.[27] The protease is an obligate homodimer of 99 residues per chain whose active site lies on the two-fold axis, with the catalytic Asp25–Thr26–Gly27 triad contributed by both subunits and covered by two mobile β-hairpin flaps. Indinavir (PDB chemical component MK1) is a 613.8 g mol⁻¹ peptidomimetic with 45 heavy atoms, 11 rotatable bonds, 4 hydrogen-bond donors, 7 acceptors, cLogP 2.87 and TPSA 118 Å² (computed here with RDKit).

Analysis of the crystallographic complex gives the reference against which the docking is judged. Twenty-six residues have a heavy atom within 4 Å of the ligand, thirteen from each subunit — the site is strictly shared between the two copies — and forty residues within 6 Å. Nine polar contacts shorter than 3.5 Å are present:

Preparation followed Section 4: protein atoms only, hydrogens added at pH 7.4 with Gasteiger charges, the flap water HOH 308 retained as part of the receptor, the ligand's bond orders restored from the PDB chemical-component SMILES, and a 26 × 22 × 22 Å box centred on the crystallographic ligand centroid. AutoDock Vina 1.2.7 was used throughout, with a fixed random seed.

7.2 Results: the search, not the score, is what fails

Two control calculations come first. Scoring the crystallographic pose exactly as deposited gives −11.30 kcal mol⁻¹; allowing a local optimisation of that pose, which relieves the small clashes inevitable when hydrogens are added to a 2.0 Å structure, gives −11.73 kcal mol⁻¹. This is the value a successful docking run should approach. (Without the flap water the same two numbers are −10.95 and −11.34, so the water contributes about 0.35–0.40 kcal mol⁻¹ to the score of the native pose.)

Five docking runs were then performed, differing only in the input conformer and in the exhaustiveness. All RMSD values are symmetry-corrected: they are the minimum over the molecule's own topological symmetry (RDKit CalcRMS) and over the C2 symmetry of the protease dimer, which maps chain A onto chain B with a Cα RMSD of 0.52 Å — without this correction a pose that is correct but related to the reference by the dimer axis would be scored as a failure.

Conformer B, generated by RDKit with one random seed, differs from the bound conformation by 3.36 Å after optimal alignment; conformer A, generated with another seed, by 4.20 Å. From conformer B, Vina reproduces the crystallographic pose to 0.48 Å at an exhaustiveness of only 16, in 46 s. From conformer A, the rank-1 pose is 4.36 Å away — a failure by the 2 Å criterion — and remains so at exhaustiveness 128 with two different seeds, although a near-native pose at 1.9 Å does appear further down the list, at rank 7.

What this experiment teaches

  • Re-docking from the bound conformer is not a validation. Run 1 reproduces the pose to 0.45 Å, but it was given the answer: Vina varies only the declared torsions and keeps ring conformations, bond lengths and bond angles exactly as supplied. A paper that reports re-docking RMSD without stating how the input conformer was generated has demonstrated very little.[25]
  • Conformer generation is part of the docking protocol. The same molecule, the same program and the same receptor gave 0.48 Å and 4.36 Å depending on which conformer entered. Generate several conformers, or use a program that samples ring and angle geometry.
  • Distinguish search failure from scoring failure. Here the score of the native pose is 2 kcal mol⁻¹ better than any decoy, so scoring is adequate and search is the bottleneck. The diagnostic is exactly the one performed above: score the experimental pose. If it scores better than everything the search produced, the search failed; if the search produced a near-native pose but ranked it below a wrong one, the scoring failed; if the experimental pose scores badly, the preparation is wrong.
  • Exhaustiveness is not a cure. Multiplying it by four changed the rank-1 RMSD from 4.36 to 4.36 Å. More sampling cannot create a degree of freedom the program does not have.
  • Report the full pose list, not only rank 1. In runs 4 and 5 a near-native pose existed at rank 7 and 9. In prospective work, inspecting the top five to ten poses, and clustering them, is standard practice.

7.3 Size bias and ligand efficiency

Section 8Virtual screening

Structure-based virtual screening applies docking to a library of compounds in order to rank them and buy or synthesise only the top fraction. Commercial libraries now reach 10⁹–10¹⁰ make-on-demand molecules (Enamine REAL, ZINC-22), and campaigns at that scale have delivered potent ligands directly from the docking output.[22],[23] Two things make it work: the task requires only that actives be enriched near the top, not correctly ordered; and the hit rate of docking (typically 1–20 % at the chosen cut-off, depending on the target) is one to two orders of magnitude above random screening.

Design of a campaign

  1. Validate the protocol first by re-docking (Section 7) and, better, by retrospective screening: dock a set of known actives together with decoys and check that the actives are enriched. Decoys must be property-matched (similar MW, logP, charge, rotatable bonds) but topologically dissimilar, as in the DUD-E and LIT-PCBA sets; unmatched decoys produce flattering, meaningless enrichment.[18],[19]
  2. Prepare the library: desalt, enumerate protonation states and stereoisomers, filter by drug-likeness or lead-likeness and remove PAINS and reactive groups.
  3. Dock in stages: a fast, coarse pass over the whole library, then a more exhaustive pass over the top 1–5 %, then rescoring with an orthogonal function or with MM/GBSA.
  4. Cluster and inspect by hand. Chemical diversity of the selected set matters more than squeezing the last 0.2 kcal mol⁻¹, and visual inspection of poses still catches artefacts that no filter does — a ligand making no interaction with any conserved residue, a buried unsatisfied charge, a pose jammed into a crystallographic artefact.
  5. Test experimentally and feed the result back. A campaign that is never tested has produced no information.

Section 9Beyond protein–ligand docking

Protein–protein docking addresses a different geometry: two large, mostly rigid bodies with a broad, flat interface and no deep pocket. The classical algorithms search the six rigid-body degrees of freedom exhaustively using fast Fourier transform correlation of shape and electrostatic grids (ZDOCK, FTDock, ClusPro), then rescore and refine the top thousands of orientations. HADDOCK takes a different route, converting experimental or predicted information about which residues are at the interface — mutagenesis, chemical shift perturbation, crosslinking, coevolution — into ambiguous interaction restraints that drive a flexible docking in CNS.[11] Since 2021 the field has been reshaped by AlphaFold-Multimer and AlphaFold 3, which predict many complexes more accurately than docking, although they remain weaker for transient, antibody–antigen and peptide interactions.

Covalent docking handles ligands that form a bond with a residue (acrylamide warheads targeting cysteine, β-lactams targeting serine) by tethering the ligand to the nucleophile and sampling around the forming bond; the score then reports only the non-covalent part and must be complemented by the chemistry of the warhead. Fragment docking deals with molecules of 10–20 heavy atoms, where scores are small and discrimination is poor, and is therefore normally paired with experimental fragment screening (X-ray, NMR, SPR). Docking into nucleic acids and into membrane proteins require their own parameters: both environments are dominated by interactions that generic functions describe badly — charged phosphate backbones in one case, a low-dielectric lipid interior in the other.

Finally, deep-learning pose prediction (EquiBind, TankBind, DiffDock, and the ligand-aware AlphaFold 3) treats docking as a generative problem rather than a search, producing poses in seconds. Benchmarks show high apparent success on the PDBBind test set but a sharp drop on proteins dissimilar to the training set, and a tendency to produce poses with non-physical geometry; they are an active research area rather than a replacement for the methods in this unit.[34],[35]

11.2 Reproducing the re-docking of Section 7

The experiment uses the Vina Python API, RDKit and Meeko. Install with conda install -c conda-forge vina rdkit openbabel and pip install meeko.

import json, urllib.request, numpy as np, subprocess
from rdkit import Chem
from rdkit.Chem import AllChem, rdMolAlign
from Bio.PDB import PDBParser, Superimposer
from vina import Vina

# ---- 1. Receptor: protein + the conserved flap water, hydrogens at pH 7.4 ----
urllib.request.urlretrieve("https://files.rcsb.org/download/1HSG.pdb", "1HSG.pdb")
keep = [l for l in open("1HSG.pdb")
        if l.startswith("ATOM") or (l.startswith("HETATM") and l[17:20] == "HOH"
                                    and int(l[22:26]) == 308)]
open("rec.pdb", "w").write("".join(keep) + "END\n")
subprocess.run("obabel rec.pdb -xr -O rec.pdbqt -h -p 7.4 --partialcharge gasteiger",
               shell=True, check=True)

# ---- 2. Ligand: bond orders from the PDB chemical component, then a de-novo conformer ----
cc = json.loads(urllib.request.urlopen(
        "https://data.rcsb.org/rest/v1/core/chemcomp/MK1").read())
smiles = cc["rcsb_chem_comp_descriptor"]["SMILES_stereo"]
open("lig_xtal.pdb", "w").write("".join(l for l in open("1HSG.pdb")
                                        if l.startswith("HETATM") and l[17:20] == "MK1"))
xtal = AllChem.AssignBondOrdersFromTemplate(Chem.MolFromSmiles(smiles),
                                            Chem.MolFromPDBFile("lig_xtal.pdb"))
m = Chem.AddHs(Chem.MolFromSmiles(smiles))
AllChem.EmbedMolecule(m, randomSeed=7)          # change this seed and the result changes
AllChem.MMFFOptimizeMolecule(m)
Chem.MolToMolFile(m, "lig.sdf")
subprocess.run("mk_prepare_ligand.py -i lig.sdf -o lig.pdbqt", shell=True, check=True)

# ---- 3. Box centred on the crystallographic ligand ----
centre = np.array(xtal.GetConformer().GetPositions()).mean(0).tolist()

# ---- 4. Control: score the experimental pose, then dock ----
v = Vina(sf_name="vina", cpu=8, seed=2026)
v.set_receptor("rec.pdbqt")
v.set_ligand_from_file("lig.pdbqt")
v.compute_vina_maps(center=centre, box_size=[26, 22, 22])
v.dock(exhaustiveness=16, n_poses=10)
v.write_poses("poses.pdbqt", n_poses=10, overwrite=True)
print(v.energies(n_poses=10)[:, 0])
subprocess.run("mk_export.py poses.pdbqt -s poses.sdf", shell=True, check=True)

# ---- 5. Symmetry-corrected RMSD: molecular symmetry AND the C2 axis of the dimer ----
model = PDBParser(QUIET=True).get_structure("x", "1HSG.pdb")[0]
caA = [r["CA"] for r in model["A"] if "CA" in r]; caB = [r["CA"] for r in model["B"] if "CA" in r]
sup = Superimposer(); sup.set_atoms(caB + caA, caA + caB)      # A->B and B->A at once
rot, tran = sup.rotran
xtal_sym = Chem.Mol(xtal); conf = xtal_sym.GetConformer()
for i, p in enumerate(np.array(xtal.GetConformer().GetPositions()) @ rot + tran):
    conf.SetAtomPosition(i, p.tolist())
for i, pose in enumerate(Chem.SDMolSupplier("poses.sdf", removeHs=False)):
    p = Chem.RemoveHs(pose)
    rmsd = min(rdMolAlign.CalcRMS(p, xtal), rdMolAlign.CalcRMS(p, xtal_sym))
    print(f"pose {i+1}: RMSD {rmsd:.2f} A")

11.3 A protocol checklist

  1. Record the provenance of the receptor (PDB ID and date, or model source and version) and of every ligand (source, SMILES, protonation).
  2. State explicitly: which waters were kept and why; how protonation states were assigned; how the box was defined; how conformers were generated; the program, version, scoring function, exhaustiveness and random seed.
  3. Validate by re-docking from an independently generated conformer, and report the RMSD definition used (heavy atoms, symmetry correction).
  4. Score the experimental pose as a control, so that a failure can be attributed to search or to scoring.
  5. Repeat stochastic runs with several seeds and report the spread, not a single number.
  6. Inspect the top poses visually and list the interactions each makes with named residues.
  7. Never convert a score into an affinity; compare scores only within one protocol.
  8. For screening, add a retrospective enrichment test with property-matched decoys before the prospective run.

Section 12Self-assessment

Attempt each question before opening the answer.

1. Distinguish "pose" from "binding mode", and explain why the distinction matters when writing a results section.

A pose is any candidate placement produced by the program; the binding mode is the placement the complex actually adopts, known only from experiment. Writing "the ligand binds in the following mode" when one has only a docking pose asserts an experimental fact that has not been established. The correct phrasing is that the best-scoring pose suggests interactions with named residues, which mutagenesis or a structure could test.

2. A ligand with 8 rotatable bonds is sampled at 30° increments. How many internal conformations does systematic enumeration generate, and what does this imply?

(360/30)8 = 128 = 429,981,696 ≈ 4.3 × 108, before any rigid-body placement. At 104 evaluations per second that is about twelve hours for one ligand, which is why systematic search is abandoned in favour of incremental or stochastic strategies.

3. Your docking of a known nanomolar inhibitor gives a best score of −6.2 kcal mol⁻¹ and a pose that makes no contact with the catalytic residues. List, in order, the things you would check.

(i) Preparation of the receptor: correct biological assembly and chains, no missing loops bordering the site, protonation states, essential waters, metals or cofactors present. (ii) The box: does it actually enclose the site, with margin? (iii) Preparation of the ligand: protonation and tautomer at assay pH, stereochemistry, sensible 3D conformer. (iv) Score the experimental pose if one exists for a related complex — if it scores well, the search failed; if it scores badly, the preparation or the scoring function is at fault. (v) Only then raise exhaustiveness, allow side-chain flexibility or try an ensemble.

4. Why is re-docking the crystallographic conformer a weak validation, and what is the stronger test?

Most programs keep ring conformations, bond lengths and bond angles fixed from the input and vary only the declared torsions, so supplying the bound conformer gives away part of the answer; Section 7 obtained 0.45 Å this way and 4.36 Å from an independently generated conformer of the same molecule. The stronger tests are re-docking from a de-novo conformer (reported with the generation method), cross-docking — docking the ligand into a structure solved with a different ligand — and retrospective enrichment against property-matched decoys.

5. In Section 7, the score of the locally minimised crystal pose is −11.73 kcal mol⁻¹ and the best pose from conformer A is −9.95 kcal mol⁻¹ at 4.36 Å. Is this a scoring failure or a search failure? Justify.

A search failure. The scoring function correctly ranks the native pose nearly 2 kcal mol⁻¹ better than the best decoy — so if the search had generated the native pose, the score would have selected it. The failure is that the search, constrained by the internal geometry of conformer A, never produced a pose in that basin, and four times more sampling did not change this.

6. Why must the RMSD in Section 7 be corrected for the symmetry of the protease dimer as well as for the symmetry of the ligand?

Ligand symmetry: topologically equivalent atoms (the two methyls of a tert-butyl, the carbons of a phenyl) can be matched in several ways, and naive atom-by-atom RMSD would penalise a correct pose that happens to label them differently. Dimer symmetry: HIV-1 protease is a C2 homodimer whose site lies on the symmetry axis, so a pose related to the reference by that axis is physically the same binding mode in an indistinguishable copy of the site. Without both corrections, correct poses are recorded as failures.

7. Why does the Vina score correlate with heavy-atom count, and when is ligand efficiency the right correction?

The score is a sum over ligand–protein atom pairs, so adding atoms that make any favourable contact improves it; the only size-dependent penalty is the small torsional term. Ligand efficiency (−ΔG or −score per heavy atom) corrects for this and is the appropriate metric when comparing molecules of different size that are all known or presumed to bind — fragment screening, hit-to-lead optimisation. It is meaningless when applied to molecules that do not bind at all, as Figure 7 shows: aspirin's high "efficiency" in the protease site reflects a small molecule making a few contacts, not affinity.

8. What is ensemble docking, what problem does it address, and what is its cost?

Docking each ligand into several receptor conformations — crystal structures with different ligands, NMR models, MD frames or alternative predicted models — and keeping the best result per ligand. It addresses receptor flexibility and implements conformational selection, recovering actives that a single rigid structure cannot accommodate. Its cost is linear in the number of structures, and because each extra receptor also gives decoys another chance to score well, enrichment can degrade if the ensemble members are not genuine, experimentally supported states.

9. A screening campaign reports ROC-AUC 0.78 but a hit rate of 2 % among the 50 compounds purchased from the top of the ranking. Are these consistent, and which number should guide the next campaign?

They are consistent: AUC measures the whole ranking and is insensitive to the composition of the extreme top, where purchases are made. A respectable AUC with poor early recognition is common. Report EF1 % or BEDROC alongside AUC, and let the experimentally confirmed hit rate guide the next campaign, since it is the only prospective measure.

10. Name three situations in which you would not trust a docking score at all, and say what you would do instead.

(i) A metal-containing site (zinc protease, nickel urease): generic functions lack proper coordination terms — use specialised parameters, a QM/MM treatment, or rely on pose geometry rather than score. (ii) Binding dominated by water-mediated networks or by large desolvation penalties — keep waters explicitly and move to MM/GBSA or free-energy methods (Unit 5). (iii) Comparing congeneric analogues that differ by a methyl or a halogen, where the real ΔΔG is a few tenths of a kcal mol⁻¹ — use relative free-energy perturbation, or simply make and test the compounds.

11. Your target has no experimental structure; you have an AlphaFold model with pLDDT > 90 over the whole domain. What are the two main risks when docking into it?

First, the model is apo and in one conformational state: the pocket may be collapsed, occupied by side chains that would move on binding, or in the wrong state (inactive vs active) for the ligands of interest. Second, cofactors, metals and structural waters are absent, so a site that requires them is incomplete. High pLDDT tells you the backbone is reliable, not that the pocket is ready for docking. Mitigations: compare with holo structures of homologs, relax the pocket, use an ensemble (MD frames or alternative predictions), and validate with known actives before trusting any prospective result.

12. Write, in two sentences, what you would report about the Section 7 experiment in the methods of a paper.

Example: "Indinavir was re-docked into the HIV-1 protease structure 1HSG (2.0 Å; protein plus the conserved flap water HOH 308, hydrogens added at pH 7.4 with Gasteiger charges) using AutoDock Vina 1.2.7 in a 26 × 22 × 22 Å box centred on the crystallographic ligand, with ligand conformers generated independently with RDKit ETKDG and exhaustiveness 16–128 at fixed seeds. The locally minimised crystallographic pose scored −11.73 kcal mol⁻¹; docking reproduced it to 0.48 Å from one de-novo conformer but failed (rank-1 RMSD 4.36 Å) from another, with all RMSD values corrected for ligand symmetry and for the C2 symmetry of the dimer."
