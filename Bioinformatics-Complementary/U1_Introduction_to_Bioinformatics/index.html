What is bioinformatics?

Bioinformatics is the scientific discipline that develops and applies computational, mathematical and statistical methods to acquire, store, organise, analyse and interpret biological data, above all data about the molecules that carry and execute hereditary information: DNA, RNA and proteins. The word combines bio (life) and informatics (the science of information processing). It was coined around 1970 by the Dutch theoretical biologists Paulien Hogeweg and Ben Hesper, who used bioinformatica to mean "the study of informatic processes in biotic systems", that is, how living systems themselves store and process information[1]. Today the word is used more narrowly, for the computational analysis of molecular data, but the original meaning is worth keeping in mind: a cell is an information-processing system, and bioinformatics studies both the information and the processing.

Three activities make up the daily work of the field. The first is data management: designing databases and file formats, curating records, and linking different kinds of data (a gene to its transcripts, its protein, its three-dimensional structure, its variants and the diseases associated with them). The second is algorithm and model development: designing procedures that solve biological questions efficiently, for example aligning two sequences, assembling a genome from millions of short fragments, or predicting the structure of a protein from its sequence. The third is analysis and interpretation: applying these tools to answer concrete questions, such as which variant explains a patient's disease, which genes change their expression in a tumour, or which bacterial strain caused an outbreak.

A distinction you will find in the literature is between bioinformatics and computational biology. Some authors reserve "bioinformatics" for the engineering of tools and databases, and "computational biology" for the use of computation to discover biological knowledge. In practice the boundary is blurred and both terms are used interchangeably; this course uses "bioinformatics" for the whole spectrum. Related fields include systems biology (models of networks of interacting molecules), biostatistics (statistical design and inference in biomedicine), chemoinformatics (computation on small molecules such as drugs) and health informatics (clinical records and hospital data). A medical bioengineer frequently works at the intersection of all of them.

What must we know in bioinformatics?

Bioinformatics is intrinsically interdisciplinary, and newcomers often ask how much biology, mathematics and programming they need. The honest answer is "some of each, and enough to talk to specialists in the others". Concretely, this course will build competence in five areas:

  • Molecular biology and evolution: the structure of DNA, RNA and proteins, the flow of information between them, and the evolutionary processes (mutation, selection, drift, duplication) that create similarity between sequences. Without evolution, sequence comparison would be meaningless.
  • Mathematics and statistics: probability, logarithms, information theory, basic linear algebra and statistical inference, needed to understand scores, E-values, models and their uncertainty.
  • Computing: the command line, at least one programming language (Python in this course), and an understanding of how algorithms scale with the size of the data.
  • Databases and formats: knowing where data live (GenBank, UniProt, PDB, Ensembl…), how records are structured, and how to retrieve them programmatically.
  • Critical interpretation and ethics: knowing the limitations of each method, recognising artefacts, and respecting the rights of the people whose data are analysed.

Pitfall: bioinformatics is not "running a program"

Web servers make it easy to obtain an output: paste a sequence, press a button, receive a table. The scientific work begins after that. Every result rests on a model with assumptions (independence between positions, a substitution matrix, a reference genome, a training set). A bioinformatician must know those assumptions well enough to decide whether the output answers the biological question. Throughout the course, each method is accompanied by its limitations for this reason.

Evolution, phenotypes and natural selection: a virtuous circle?

The most important single idea in bioinformatics is borrowed from biology: all living organisms descend from common ancestors, and their genomes are modified copies of ancestral genomes. Darwin described the mechanism of natural selection[B1]: individuals of a population differ in heritable traits; some variants survive and reproduce more than others in a given environment; therefore the frequency of those variants increases over generations. The modern synthesis added genetics: heritable variation arises from mutations in DNA, and what selection "sees" is the phenotype, the observable set of characteristics produced by the genotype in interaction with the environment.

The lecturer's slides ask whether this is "a virtuous circle". It is a circle in a precise sense. Genotype produces phenotype through molecular mechanisms; phenotype determines survival and reproduction; reproduction determines which genotypes are transmitted; and the transmitted genotypes produce the next generation's phenotypes. Whether the circle is "virtuous" depends on the point of view. Selection increases adaptation to the current environment, but it does not plan ahead, does not optimise for human well-being and can maintain variants that are harmful in some individuals.

Sickle-cell haemoglobin, the running case of this course, is the textbook demonstration. The HbS allele of the β-globin gene is harmful in homozygotes, who develop sickle-cell disease. Heterozygous carriers, however, are partially protected against severe Plasmodium falciparum malaria. Allison showed this protection in East African populations in 1954[2], and global maps of HbS allele frequency coincide geographically with historical malaria endemicity[3]. Selection therefore keeps the allele frequent where malaria is common, because the advantage in carriers outweighs the cost in homozygotes. This is called balancing selection (more specifically, heterozygote advantage). The circle is efficient for the population under malaria pressure and tragic for the affected children.

For bioinformatics, evolution matters in two ways. First, because sequences descend from common ancestors, sequence similarity is evidence of shared ancestry (homology), and shared ancestry predicts shared structure and function. This is the principle behind database searching with BLAST (Unit 4A), multiple alignment and phylogenetics (Unit 4B), and homology modelling of protein structures (Unit 5). Second, selection leaves statistical signatures in sequences. Positions that are important for function change slowly, because most mutations there are removed by purifying selection. We will measure such signatures with information theory later in this section.

How old are humans?

Evolutionary arguments require a time scale. Life on Earth is over three billion years old, but our own species is young. Until 2017 the oldest fossils attributed to Homo sapiens came from eastern Africa (Ethiopia) and were considerably younger than those described below. In 2017 Hublin and colleagues described new fossils from Jebel Irhoud (Morocco) with a modern-looking face and a more archaic, elongated braincase[4], and Richter and colleagues dated them by thermoluminescence of heated flints to approximately 315 ± 34 thousand years[5]. These findings moved the earliest known members of the H. sapiens lineage to about 300,000 years ago and suggested that the species evolved across Africa rather than in a single region. For genomics this is a short time: about 12,000 human generations if a generation is taken as 25 years. Two unrelated humans therefore share most of their DNA sequence, and the variants that distinguish them, including disease alleles, are what clinical bioinformatics must find.

Key ideas

  • Bioinformatics develops and applies computational and statistical methods to biological, mainly molecular, data. The term was coined by Hogeweg and Hesper around 1970.
  • Its three activities are data management, method development, and analysis with interpretation.
  • Natural selection acts on phenotypes and changes the frequencies of genotypes; the circle genotype → phenotype → reproduction → genotype is efficient for adaptation but does not optimise human health (HbS and malaria).
  • Common descent is why sequence comparison works: similarity indicates homology, and homology predicts structure and function.
  • Modern Homo sapiens has existed for roughly 300,000 years (Jebel Irhoud).

The central paradigm of bioinformatics

Molecular biology is organised around the central dogma, formulated by Francis Crick in 1958 and restated in 1970: sequence information is transferred from nucleic acid to nucleic acid (replication, transcription, reverse transcription) and from nucleic acid to protein (translation), but once information has passed into protein it cannot flow back to nucleic acid[6]. Bioinformatics extends this idea into what the lecturer's slides call the central paradigm of bioinformatics: a chain of four levels of information (Figure 1).

  1. Genetic information: the nucleotide sequence of the genome, its genes, regulatory elements and variants. Data: genome sequences, variant catalogues. Typical questions: where are the genes? Which variant does this patient carry?
  2. Structural information: the sequence of the encoded RNA and protein, and their three-dimensional structure. Data: protein sequences, experimentally determined or predicted structures. Typical questions: what does the protein look like? Where does the variant fall in the structure?
  3. Biochemical information: what the molecule does, with whom it interacts, and in which pathway it works. Data: enzyme kinetics, interaction networks, pathways, expression levels. Typical questions: does the variant change binding, stability or catalysis?
  4. Phenotype (symptoms): the observable consequences at the level of cells, tissues and the whole organism, which in medicine are signs and symptoms. Data: clinical records, phenotype ontologies, disease databases. Typical question: does this molecular change explain the disease?

The paradigm is useful because it organises the whole discipline. Most bioinformatics methods try to infer a level from the level before it: gene prediction infers transcripts from genomic DNA; structure prediction infers a fold from an amino-acid sequence (Unit 5); functional annotation infers biochemical roles from sequence and structure (Units 3 and 4); and clinical variant interpretation tries to connect a DNA change to a symptom. Each arrow is uncertain, and uncertainty accumulates along the chain. This is why it is much easier to predict the protein encoded by a gene than to predict a patient's disease from his or her genome.

Running case: the central paradigm for sickle-cell disease

In 1949 Pauling and colleagues showed by electrophoresis that haemoglobin from patients with sickle-cell anaemia migrates differently from normal haemoglobin, and called it "a molecular disease"[7]; in 1957 Ingram identified the cause as a single amino-acid substitution, glutamate to valine, in the β chain[8]. Read along the paradigm: a single-nucleotide change (A→T) in codon 7 of the HBB coding sequence (GAG→GTG) replaces glutamate at position 6 of the mature protein by valine (genetic → structural); the hydrophobic valine on the surface of deoxygenated haemoglobin creates a sticky patch that lets tetramers polymerise into long fibres (structural → biochemical); the fibres deform red cells into the sickle shape, which causes haemolysis, blocked small vessels, pain crises and organ damage (biochemical → phenotype)[9]. The numbering difference (codon 7 versus residue 6) arises because the initiator methionine is removed from the mature protein; it is a frequent source of confusion and will be discussed in Unit 3.

Pitfall: the paradigm is not a one-way, one-to-one map

The arrows of Figure 1 are simplifications. One gene can produce several transcripts and proteins (Section 1.2), proteins are chemically modified after translation, phenotype depends on the environment and on many other genes, and regulatory RNAs and epigenetic marks act on the genetic level itself. Even sickle-cell disease, caused by one variant, varies greatly in severity between patients because of other genetic factors (for example, persistence of fetal haemoglobin) and of environment and care.

A brief history of bioinformatics

Bioinformatics did not begin with the Human Genome Project; it grew from the moment biologists had more sequence data than they could compare by eye. Figure 2 summarises selected milestones, and the following paragraphs explain why each one mattered.

From protein chemistry to the first database (1950s–1960s). Watson and Crick's double-helix model of DNA (1953) explained how genetic information could be copied[10], and Frederick Sanger's determination of the amino-acid sequence of insulin in the early 1950s proved that proteins have defined sequences. By the early 1960s dozens of protein sequences were known. Margaret Oakley Dayhoff, a physical chemist, began to collect them systematically and published the Atlas of Protein Sequence and Structure in 1965: the first sequence database, distributed as a printed book and later on magnetic tape[11]. Dayhoff also wrote early computer programs to compare sequences and, in the 1970s, derived the PAM substitution matrices from observed mutations, which you will use in Unit 4A.

Algorithms and the name (around 1970). In 1970 Needleman and Wunsch published the first dynamic-programming algorithm for the global alignment of two protein sequences[12], the ancestor of every alignment program in use today. In the same period Hogeweg and Hesper coined the term bioinformatica[1].

Structures and nucleic-acid sequences (1971–1982). The Protein Data Bank (PDB) was announced in 1971 as an archive of atomic coordinates of macromolecules, with a handful of structures at its start[13][14]. In 1977 Sanger, Nicklen and Coulson described chain-termination DNA sequencing[15], which made DNA sequences much easier to obtain than protein sequences and shifted the field towards nucleic acids (Unit 2). GenBank, the nucleotide database of the United States, was established in 1982[16]; it later joined the European (EMBL) and Japanese (DDBJ) databases in the International Nucleotide Sequence Database Collaboration[17].

Fast database searching (1985–1990). As databases grew, exact dynamic-programming comparison of a query against all entries became slow. Lipman and Pearson introduced FASTP/FASTA, which first finds short identical words and only then performs alignment[18][19]; the FASTA file format you will use every day comes from this program. In 1990 Altschul and colleagues published BLAST, a heuristic with a rigorous statistical theory of scores that became the most used tool in biology[20].

Genomes (1990–2004). The Human Genome Project started in 1990. In 1995 the first complete genome of a free-living organism, the bacterium Haemophilus influenzae, was assembled from randomly sheared fragments by whole-genome shotgun sequencing[21]. Two drafts of the human genome were published in 2001, one by the public consortium and one by Celera Genomics[22][23], and the "finished" euchromatic sequence in 2004[24]. Assembling and annotating these genomes was a computational problem as much as a laboratory one.

Massively parallel sequencing (2005 onwards). Pyrosequencing in picolitre reactors (454 Life Sciences, 2005)[25] and reversible-terminator sequencing (Solexa/Illumina, used to resequence a human genome in 2008)[26] reduced costs by orders of magnitude and turned biology into a data-intensive science. Long-read technologies later enabled the first truly complete, telomere-to-telomere (T2T) human genome in 2022[27] and a first human pangenome reference in 2023[28]. Unit 2 treats these technologies in detail.

Artificial intelligence and structure (2018 onwards). In 2020–2021 the deep-learning system AlphaFold2 predicted protein structures with accuracy often comparable to experiment[29], and AlphaFold 3 extended prediction to complexes with nucleic acids, ligands and ions[30]. In 2024 the Nobel Prize in Chemistry was awarded one half to David Baker "for computational protein design" and the other half jointly to Demis Hassabis and John Jumper "for protein structure prediction"[W2]. For a discipline that began with a printed atlas of sequences, it was a striking recognition that computation can solve central problems of chemistry and biology.

Information theory: uncertainty, surprise and the bit

The second half of curriculum item 1.1 is information theory, the mathematical theory of how much information a message carries. It was founded by Claude Shannon in 1948 to solve an engineering problem, the reliable transmission of messages through noisy telephone lines[31], and it turned out to be the natural language for describing biological sequences. A DNA molecule is literally a message written with a four-letter alphabet; a protein is a message written with twenty letters. Information theory lets us ask quantitative questions about such messages. How much information does a position in a sequence carry? How predictable is the next base? How much does a binding site stand out from random DNA? How small can a genome file be made?

Shannon's starting point is that information is the reduction of uncertainty. If you already know what the next symbol of a message will be, receiving it tells you nothing. If any of many symbols could arrive with equal probability, receiving one tells you a lot. The amount of information associated with observing an event of probability p is called its surprisal:

I(x) = −log2 p(x)   bits where p(x) is the probability of event x; with base-2 logarithms the unit is the bit (binary digit).

A fair coin toss has p = 1/2 for each face, so observing the result gives −log2(1/2) = 1 bit. One bit is therefore the information needed to decide between two equally likely alternatives, the answer to one well-posed yes/no question. A certain event (p = 1) gives 0 bits, and rare events give many bits: an event with p = 1/1024 gives 10 bits. Logarithms are used because information from independent events should add: two coin tosses have four equally likely outcomes, and −log2(1/4) = 2 bits = 1 + 1.

Shannon entropy

The entropy of a source of symbols is the average surprisal, the expected information per symbol:

H = −Σi pi log2 pi where the sum runs over the symbols of the alphabet, pi is the probability (in practice, the relative frequency) of symbol i, and terms with pi = 0 contribute 0.

Entropy measures uncertainty, or equivalently the average number of yes/no questions needed to identify a symbol drawn from the source. For a source with two symbols of probabilities p and 1 − p, entropy is largest (1 bit) when p = 0.5 and falls to 0 when one symbol is certain (Figure 3A). The general rule is that entropy is maximal when all K symbols of an alphabet are equally probable, and then equals log2K.

This gives two numbers that every bioinformatician should know. For DNA, with four bases, the maximum entropy is log24 = 2 bits per base. You can see why with questions: "is it a purine (A or G)?" and then "is it A?" identify any base with exactly two questions. For proteins, with twenty standard amino acids, the maximum is log220 = 4.32 bits per residue. No DNA sequence can have more than 2 bits of entropy per base, and no protein more than about 4.32 bits per residue, whatever its function or complexity.

Formal note: properties of entropy

(1) H ≥ 0, with equality only if one symbol has probability 1. (2) H ≤ log2K, with equality only for the uniform distribution; this follows from Jensen's inequality applied to the concave logarithm. (3) For two independent sources, the entropy of the pair is the sum of the entropies, H(X, Y) = H(X) + H(Y); in general H(X, Y) ≤ H(X) + H(Y). (4) Changing the base of the logarithm only changes the unit: natural logarithms give "nats", and 1 nat = 1/ln 2 ≈ 1.443 bits. (5) Shannon's source-coding theorem states that no lossless code can use fewer than H bits per symbol on average for an independent source, which is why entropy is also a bound on compression. A rigorous treatment is given by Cover and Thomas[B2].

Maximum entropy and the entropy of real sequences

What happens when we measure the entropy of real biological sequences? Using relative frequencies as estimates of probabilities, we computed the order-0 entropy (which ignores the order of the symbols) of several sequences retrieved for this unit (Figure 3B):

  • The complete genome of Escherichia coli K-12 MG1655 (NCBI NC_000913.3, 4,641,652 bp) contains 1,142,742 A, 1,180,091 C, 1,177,437 G and 1,141,382 T; its entropy is 1.9998 bits per base, practically the maximum of 2.
  • The first 10 Mb of human chromosome 11 (GRCh38; 60,000 unknown bases N removed) gives 1.9952 bits per base: the human sequence is AT-rich (GC content 0.459), so the four bases are slightly unequal.
  • The coding sequence (CDS) of human β-globin (HBB, from NM_000518.5; 444 nt) gives 1.9828 bits per base.
  • Proteins are further from their maximum. Human β-globin (UniProt P68871, 147 residues) has 3.95 bits per residue, and the amino-acid composition of all reviewed human proteins (UniProt Swiss-Prot) gives 4.18 bits, below 4.32 because amino acids are used very unequally: leucine alone accounts for 10.0% of residues.

The general lesson is that biological sequences look almost random when you only count letters. This is not because they carry no information (they obviously encode proteins and regulation) but because order-0 entropy ignores the order of the letters, and the biologically important information lies precisely in which letter is at which position and in the dependencies between positions. We will capture these with conditional entropy, relative entropy and mutual information.

Low-complexity regions

Some segments, however, have strikingly low entropy even when counting letters. They are called low-complexity regions: homopolymeric runs (AAAAA…), short tandem repeats such as (CA)n, or protein segments rich in one or two amino acids. Huntingtin, the protein mutated in Huntington's disease, is a classic example. Its N-terminal region contains a polyglutamine (polyQ) tract encoded by a CAG repeat; in the UniProt reference sequence the tract occupies residues 18–38 (21 consecutive Q). The first 90 residues of huntingtin have an entropy of only 2.73 bits per residue, against 4.10 bits for the whole 3,142-residue protein. A sliding-window calculation shows the entropy falling to zero inside the polyQ tract (Figure 4).

Low-complexity regions matter in practice. They produce spurious matches in database searches, because two unrelated proteins with polyQ tracts look similar; BLAST therefore masks them by default with the SEG algorithm, which is based on exactly this kind of local compositional complexity[32]. They also matter clinically: expansion of the CAG repeat beyond a threshold causes Huntington's disease, and repeat expansions are hard to measure with short sequencing reads (Unit 2).

Conditional entropy and Markov dependence

Order-0 entropy treats each position as independent of the others. Real sequences are not like that: in human DNA, a C is rarely followed by a G (Section 1.4), in coding DNA the third base of a codon depends on the first two, and in proteins hydrophobic residues alternate with polar ones in surface helices. The tool that measures how much the context reduces uncertainty is the conditional entropy:

H(Xn | Xn−k … Xn−1) = H(k+1-mers) − H(k-mers) the average uncertainty about the base at position n when the k preceding bases are known; H(k-mers) is the entropy of the distribution of words of length k.

If the sequence were generated without any dependence between positions, knowing the preceding bases would not help, and the conditional entropy would stay equal to the order-0 entropy for every k. If the next base were fully determined by the preceding ones, the conditional entropy would be 0. Real sequences lie in between. The value of k is the order of the context, and a model in which the probability of a base depends only on the k preceding bases is a Markov chain of order k, introduced formally in Section 1.4.

Figure 5 shows conditional entropies computed for k = 0 to 8. For the whole E. coli genome the value falls slowly from 1.9998 to 1.8888 bits; for 1 Mb of human chromosome 11 it falls from 1.9924 to 1.8733 at k = 5 and then faster, reflecting repeated sequences such as transposable elements; for the shuffled E. coli genome, in which the bases are the same but their order is random, it stays at 1.9997 up to k = 4. The difference between the real and the shuffled curves is a direct measurement of the information contained in the order of the bases.

Pitfall: entropy estimates need enough data

Estimating a probability from counts requires that each possible event be observed many times. With k = 8 there are 49 = 262,144 possible 9-mers; the 444-nt HBB CDS contains at most 436 of them, each seen once or twice, so the estimated conditional entropy falls to 0.041 bits, a meaningless value. Even 1 Mb of genome starts to under-sample at k = 8 (compare the two E. coli curves in Figure 5). The rule of thumb is that the number of observations should be much larger than the number of parameters: here, the sequence length should be much larger than 4k+1. The same issue reappears whenever you train a model on few data.

Relative entropy: comparing a distribution with a background

Often we do not want the absolute uncertainty of a distribution, but how different it is from a reference or background distribution. The measure is the relative entropy, also called the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence:

D(P ‖ Q) = Σi pi log2 (pi / qi) where P is the observed distribution (for example, amino-acid frequencies in one protein or base frequencies at one position of a binding site) and Q is the background (for example, average frequencies in the proteome or genome). D ≥ 0, and D = 0 only if P = Q.

Relative entropy has an operational meaning: it is the average number of extra bits per symbol you would need if you encoded data from P with a code optimised for Q, and it is the average log-likelihood ratio in favour of P when data truly come from P. With the composition of all reviewed human proteins as background, β-globin has D = 0.23 bits per residue (its composition is fairly typical, although histidine and valine are about twice as frequent as in the average human protein), whereas the polyQ-rich N-terminal 90 residues of huntingtin have D = 1.38 bits per residue, a strongly atypical composition.

Relative entropy is one of the most used quantities in the rest of the course. Three uses will appear repeatedly:

  • Log-odds scores. Each term log2(pi/qi) is a log-odds score: positive if symbol i is more frequent in the model than in the background. All the substitution matrices used for sequence alignment (PAM, BLOSUM) are tables of such log-odds scores, and the relative entropy of a matrix measures the average information per aligned pair; Altschul showed that it determines how long an alignment must be to be distinguishable from chance[34][35][36]. This is the subject of Unit 4A.
  • Sequence logos and profiles. The height of a column in a sequence logo is its information content, which is the relative entropy of the column distribution against the background (Section 1.1.10 below).
  • Classifiers. The Markov classifier of CpG islands in Section 1.4 sums log-odds scores of transition probabilities.

Mutual information: dependence between two positions

The third measure asks how much knowing one variable tells you about another. The mutual information between two random variables X and Y is

I(X; Y) = Σx,y p(x,y) log2 [ p(x,y) / (p(x) p(y)) ] = H(X) + H(Y) − H(X,Y) the relative entropy between the joint distribution and the product of the marginal distributions; I = 0 if and only if X and Y are independent.

Applied to a sequence, X can be the base at position i and Y the base at position i + d; I(d) then measures how strongly positions d apart are correlated. Protein-coding DNA has a characteristic signature: because the genetic code is read in triplets and codon positions have different compositions, mutual information is higher at distances that are multiples of 3. This was recognised early as a way to detect coding regions[37] and analysed systematically with mutual information by Grosse and colleagues[38]. Our calculation on the 4,318 annotated protein-coding sequences of E. coli reproduces the period-3 pattern: the mutual information at d = 6 (0.0013 bits) and d = 9 (0.0016 bits) is higher than at the neighbouring distances d = 5 (0.0012) and d = 7 (0.0005) (Figure 6).

Mutual information has a second, very important application that you will meet in Unit 4B: the detection of covariation in RNA alignments. In a structural RNA such as a transfer RNA, two positions that form a base pair in the secondary structure must remain complementary; if a mutation changes one (G→A), a compensating mutation tends to appear in the partner (C→U). Across many homologous sequences, the two columns therefore show high mutual information even when each column, considered alone, is variable. Covariation analysis was used to infer the secondary structures of ribosomal and transfer RNAs before they were determined experimentally, and it underlies covariance models for RNA families[39]. The same idea, extended to protein alignments, helped to predict residue contacts and ultimately contributed to modern structure prediction (Unit 5).

Formal note: the identities that link the four measures

For two variables, H(X,Y) = H(X) + H(Y | X) (chain rule). Combining with the definition of mutual information gives I(X;Y) = H(Y) − H(Y | X): the mutual information is the reduction in uncertainty about Y obtained by knowing X. Mutual information is also a relative entropy, I(X;Y) = D(p(x,y) ‖ p(x)p(y)), so it is never negative. In Figure 5, the drop of conditional entropy from k = 0 to k = 1 is exactly the mutual information between adjacent bases.

Information content of binding sites

Proteins that regulate genes, the spliceosome that removes introns, and the ribosome that starts translation all recognise specific short sequences in DNA or RNA. How much information does such a recognition site contain? Thomas Schneider and colleagues answered this question in 1986 with information theory[40]. Take a set of aligned sites (for example, many copies of a binding site, each written in the same orientation). At each position l, the uncertainty before seeing any site is the background entropy (2 bits for equiprobable bases), and the uncertainty that remains among the observed sites is the column entropy H(l). The information content of the position is the difference:

Rsequence(l) = 2 − H(l) = 2 + Σb fb,l log2 fb,l   bits,    Rsequence = Σl Rsequence(l) where fb,l is the frequency of base b at position l among the sites. A completely conserved position carries 2 bits; a position with all four bases equally frequent carries 0. With unequal background frequencies the relative entropy against the background is used instead of 2 − H.

Schneider's key insight was a comparison with a second quantity. If a genome of G positions contains γ functional sites, then locating one site requires Rfrequency = log2(G/γ) bits. For several systems the information in the sites (Rsequence) was found to be close to the information needed to find them in the genome (Rfrequency): sites contain about as much information as is needed to be recognised, and not more[40]. The sequence logo, introduced by Schneider and Stephens in 1990, displays this information graphically: at each position, a stack of letters whose total height is Rsequence(l) and in which each letter's height is proportional to its frequency[41].

We applied this method to real human splice sites. From Ensembl release 116[W4] we took the canonical transcripts of all 235 protein-coding genes annotated in the first 10 Mb of chromosome 11 and extracted every exon–intron boundary (5′ or donor splice sites: last 3 exon bases and first 6 intron bases) and every intron–exon boundary (3′ or acceptor sites: last 20 intron bases and first 3 exon bases), always in the orientation of the transcript. This gave 1,484 donor and 1,484 acceptor sites (Figure 7). Of the donor sites, 98.6% begin the intron with GT and 1.3% with GC; 99.9% of acceptor introns end with AG. These are the famous GT–AG rules of splicing, described from sequence statistics by Shapiro and Senapathy[42].

The donor-site logo illustrates how to read information. Positions +1 and +2 (the intronic G and T) carry 1.99 and 1.90 bits, nearly the maximum, because they are almost invariant. Position +5 carries 1.14 bits (mostly G), position −1 carries 1.12 bits (mostly G), and positions far from the boundary carry little. The total, Rsequence = 8.1 bits, means that a donor site is roughly as specific as a fully conserved word of four bases (4 × 2 = 8 bits). That is not enough to identify donor sites unambiguously in a genome of three billion bases, which is one reason why gene prediction is difficult and why splicing also depends on other signals (branch points, enhancers, silencers).

Clinical connection: splice-site variants

Because positions +1 and +2 of the donor site carry almost 2 bits each, almost any change there abolishes normal splicing. Clinical variant-interpretation guidelines therefore treat variants at the ±1 and ±2 positions of introns as presumptively loss-of-function. In HBB, exon 1 ends with coding nucleotide c.92 (we computed this from the Ensembl exon coordinates: exon 1 spans 142 nt, of which the first 50 are 5′ untranslated), so the invariant G of intron 1 is c.92+1; variants at this position (historically called IVS-I-1) are a classical cause of β0-thalassaemia, in which no β-globin is produced. The information content of a position is a first, quantitative guess of how damaging a variant there may be; Unit 3 shows how to find such variants in ClinVar.

Pitfall: small samples inflate information

With few sites, frequencies are noisy and columns look more conserved than they are, so Rsequence is overestimated. Schneider derived a small-sample correction approximately equal to (s − 1)/(2 ln 2 · n) bits per position, where s = 4 is the alphabet size and n the number of sites[40]. For our 1,484 donor sites the correction is 0.0015 bits per position, negligible; for a motif built from 10 sites it would be about 0.22 bits per position, which is not negligible at all.

Data volume and compression as a measure of redundancy

Information theory also answers a practical question: how much storage does a genome need? Ensembl reports a "golden path" length of 3,099,750,718 bases for the GRCh38.p14 human reference assembly[W4]. Stored as text, one character per base, this is about 3.1 gigabytes (GB). Because there are only four bases, each can be encoded with 2 bits, so the same haploid sequence fits in 775 megabytes (MB), and a diploid genome (two copies, one from each parent) in about 1.55 GB. Raw sequencing data are much larger: a typical clinical whole-genome sequencing run produces about 30 reads covering each position (30× coverage), and each base in a FASTQ file is stored together with a quality character. As an order-of-magnitude estimate, 30 × 3.1 × 109 bases × 2 bytes ≈ 186 GB before compression (Figure 8B). Data volume is the reason why bioinformatics depends on efficient formats, compression and computing clusters (Section 1.3).

Compression is also a scientific tool. A lossless compressor replaces repeated or predictable patterns by shorter codes; the more redundant a sequence, the more it shrinks. Shannon's theorem guarantees that no compressor can use, on average, fewer bits per symbol than the true entropy rate of the source, so the compressed size is an upper bound on the information content. We compressed 1 Mb of several sequences with three general-purpose compressors (Figure 8A). E. coli DNA could not be compressed below 2 bits per base even by the best of them (xz: 2.08 bits per base): bacterial genomes are dense, with little repetition. Human DNA compressed to 1.84 bits per base with xz, because it contains many repeated elements. A shuffled sequence did not compress at all, and a (CA)n repeat compressed to 0.008 bits per base with gzip, because it is perfectly predictable. Specialised DNA compressors, which model reverse complements and approximate repeats, do considerably better than general-purpose tools[43].

Key ideas

  • Information is reduction of uncertainty; an event of probability p carries −log2p bits. Entropy H = −Σ p log2 p is the average information per symbol.
  • Maximum entropy is 2 bits per base for DNA and log220 ≈ 4.32 bits per residue for proteins. Real sequences are close to these maxima in composition; low-complexity regions are not.
  • Conditional entropy measures how much the preceding bases reduce uncertainty (Markov dependence); its estimation requires sequences much longer than 4k+1.
  • Relative entropy (KL divergence) compares a distribution with a background; it underlies log-odds scores, substitution matrices, sequence logos and classifiers.
  • Mutual information measures dependence between two positions; it reveals the period-3 structure of coding DNA and base-pair covariation in RNA.
  • The information content of aligned sites (Rsequence) is read from sequence logos; human donor splice sites carry about 8 bits.
  • A haploid human genome is about 3.1 × 109 bases (≈ 0.77 GB at 2 bits per base); compression ratios measure redundancy.

Section 1.2Genomics and proteomics

Official curriculum: «Genómica y proteómica.» — Genomics and proteomics.

The suffix -ome denotes the complete set of something in an organism or cell, and the suffix -omics the discipline that studies it with high-throughput methods. Genomics studies genomes; proteomics studies proteomes. These two are the pillars of the field, and all other omics (transcriptomics, epigenomics, metabolomics, microbiomics…) are organised around them. This section answers two questions from the lecturer's slides that every student should be able to answer with current numbers: how many genes, and how many proteins, are there in the human body?

Genomes and genomics

A genome is the complete hereditary DNA of an organism (or RNA, in some viruses): all chromosomes, including genes, regulatory elements, repeated sequences and DNA of unknown function, plus the DNA of organelles such as mitochondria. Genomics studies genomes as wholes. It is usually divided into structural genomics (sequencing, assembling and mapping genomes; in a different sense, the term also designates large-scale determination of protein structures), functional genomics (what genes do, when and where they are expressed, how they are regulated), and comparative genomics (what can be learned by comparing genomes of different species or individuals).

Genome sizes vary enormously. The E. coli K-12 chromosome analysed in Section 1.1 has 4,641,652 bp and 4,318 annotated protein-coding sequences in its NCBI RefSeq record, totalling 4,026,887 bp, the equivalent of 87% of the chromosome (a small amount of overlap between genes is ignored here). The human reference genome (GRCh38.p14, the assembly used by Ensembl release 116) has a golden-path length of 3,099,750,718 bp distributed over 22 autosomes, the X and Y sex chromosomes and the mitochondrial DNA[W4]; the first complete telomere-to-telomere assembly, T2T-CHM13, closed the gaps that remained in GRCh38, adding the sequence of centromeres and of the short arms of the acrocentric chromosomes[27]. In humans only about 1–2% of the genome codes for protein[22]; the rest includes introns, regulatory regions, non-coding RNA genes and a large fraction of repeated, transposon-derived sequences.

Two further concepts matter for medicine. First, almost every nucleated cell of the body contains the same genome; what differs between a neuron and a hepatocyte is which genes are expressed. Exceptions include mature red blood cells (which have no nucleus at all, and therefore no genome, which is relevant to the running case), gametes (haploid), lymphocytes (which rearrange their immunoglobulin or T-cell receptor genes) and tumour cells (which accumulate somatic mutations). Second, no two humans have the same genome, apart from identical twins. A single reference genome cannot represent this diversity, which is why the field is moving towards pangenome references built from many individuals[28].

Pitfall: "the" human genome

GRCh38 is a reference: a mosaic assembled mostly from a few anonymous donors, used as a common coordinate system. It is neither a "normal" nor an "ideal" genome, and many of its bases are the minor allele in some populations. When a report says that a patient "has a mutation", it usually means that the patient's sequence differs from the reference at that position, which may or may not be pathogenic.

How many genes are there in the human body?

Before the human genome was sequenced, estimates of the number of human genes varied widely, and textbooks commonly cited about 100,000. The draft sequence of 2001 lowered the estimate to 30,000–40,000 protein-coding genes[22], the finished sequence of 2004 to 20,000–25,000[24], and continued curation has lowered it further: a 2010 review documented the history of these estimates[44], and in 2014 Ezkurdia and colleagues argued, from proteomic evidence and cross-species conservation, that there may be as few as about 19,000 protein-coding genes[45]. The current reference annotation is produced jointly by the GENCODE project and Ensembl, combining manual curation, transcript evidence (including long-read RNA sequencing) and computational prediction[46][47].

For this unit we retrieved the complete current annotation from the Ensembl REST service (release 116, gene set GENCODE 50, last updated November 2025), scanning all chromosomes of GRCh38 in 5-Mb windows[W4]. The primary assembly contains 78,733 annotated genes, of which 20,107 are protein-coding, 34,866 are long non-coding RNA genes, 15,199 are pseudogenes and 7,111 encode small non-coding RNAs (microRNAs, small nuclear and nucleolar RNAs, and others) (Figure 9A). The answer to the slide question is therefore: about 20,000 protein-coding genes, and roughly four times as many genes if non-coding RNA genes and pseudogenes are included. The number of protein-coding genes in humans is similar to that of other vertebrates and not much larger than that of a nematode worm, which shows that organismal complexity is not a simple function of gene number.

Pitfall: gene counts are annotations, not measurements

A gene count depends on the definition of "gene" and on the evidence required. Small open reading frames, genes expressed only in one tissue, and genes in recently resolved regions are continually added or removed; pseudogenes can be reclassified when evidence of translation appears. That is why you should always quote a gene count together with its source and release (for example, "Ensembl 116 / GENCODE 50"), as is done here, and why different databases (Ensembl/GENCODE, RefSeq, CHESS, UniProt) give somewhat different numbers.

How many proteins? Isoforms, post-translational modifications and proteoforms

If there are about 20,000 protein-coding genes, are there about 20,000 proteins? There are at least three reasons why the true number of distinct protein molecules is far larger.

Alternative transcripts and isoforms. Most human genes produce several mRNAs through alternative transcription start sites, alternative splicing and alternative polyadenylation. In the chromosome-11 region we analysed, protein-coding genes have a median of 5 and a mean of 17.8 annotated transcripts, with a long tail of genes with dozens (Figure 9B). Not all transcripts encode a protein (some are retained introns or are degraded by nonsense-mediated decay), but many encode distinct protein isoforms. Even HBB, a small gene with three exons, has 19 annotated transcripts in Ensembl 116, although only one of them (ENST00000335295, HBB-201) is designated canonical and encodes the familiar 147-residue β-globin.

Post-translational modifications (PTMs). After translation, proteins are chemically modified: the initiator methionine is often removed (as in β-globin), and residues may be phosphorylated, acetylated, glycosylated, methylated, ubiquitinated or cleaved. A protein with ten possible phosphorylation sites can, in principle, exist in 210 = 1,024 phosphorylation states.

Proteoforms. To name this diversity, Smith and Kelleher proposed the term proteoform: a specific molecular form of a protein product, defined by its exact amino-acid sequence and its exact combination of modifications[48]. A gene gives rise to a family of proteoforms through genetic variation, alternative splicing and PTMs. How many human proteoforms exist is not known; Aebersold and colleagues reviewed the evidence and concluded that the number is very large, possibly in the millions, of which only a small fraction has been characterised experimentally[49].

Protein databases reflect these levels. In UniProt release 2026_03[W5] the human reviewed (Swiss-Prot) section contains 20,431 entries, one per protein-coding gene approximately, and 42,562 sequences when curated isoforms are included; the unreviewed (TrEMBL) section adds 190,275 computationally annotated human entries[50]. Across all organisms UniProtKB holds 150,006,383 entries, of which only 575,748 are manually reviewed. The HUPO Human Proteome Project publishes annual reports on how many predicted human proteins have been confirmed at the protein level, mainly by mass spectrometry[51].

Key ideas: genes versus proteins

  • Current annotation (Ensembl 116 / GENCODE 50): about 20.1 thousand protein-coding genes among about 79 thousand annotated genes. The old figure of 100,000 genes is historical.
  • One gene → several transcripts → several protein isoforms → many proteoforms (with PTMs). "How many proteins?" has different answers at each level: ≈ 20,000 canonical, ≈ 42,000 curated isoforms, possibly millions of proteoforms.
  • Always quote counts with the database and release.

Transcriptomics and proteomics in practice

Between genome and proteome lies the transcriptome, the set of RNA molecules present in a cell at a given time. Unlike the genome, the transcriptome changes with cell type, developmental stage, disease and environment; measuring it tells us which genes are active. Microarrays were the first genome-wide method, and they were replaced in the 2010s by RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), in which RNA is converted to complementary DNA, fragmented and sequenced (Unit 2). Single-cell RNA-seq measures expression in thousands of individual cells, and long-read sequencing reads full-length transcripts, which is changing the annotation of isoforms described above.

Proteomics measures proteins directly. Its main technology is mass spectrometry (MS). In the common bottom-up strategy, proteins are digested with trypsin into peptides, the peptides are separated by liquid chromatography and measured by tandem MS, and the spectra are matched computationally against the peptides predicted from a protein database. Identification is therefore itself a bioinformatics problem: spectra must be scored against candidates and the false-discovery rate controlled statistically. Top-down proteomics measures intact proteins and is the main way to observe proteoforms directly. Antibody-based methods and, more recently, affinity reagents and protein arrays complement MS. Proteomics is harder than genomics because proteins cannot be amplified like DNA, their abundances span many orders of magnitude, and their chemistry is diverse.

Microbiomics

The human body is an ecosystem. The microbiota is the community of bacteria, archaea, fungi and viruses that live on and in us, and the microbiome is, strictly, the collection of their genomes (the two words are often used interchangeably). Sender, Fuchs and Milo revised the popular claim that bacterial cells outnumber human cells ten to one: for a reference adult man they estimated about 3.8 × 1013 bacteria and 3.0 × 1013 human cells, a ratio close to 1:1, with most bacteria in the colon[52]. Bacterial genes, however, outnumber human genes by orders of magnitude, because each species brings its own gene set.

Microbiomics became possible when sequencing allowed communities to be studied without culturing their members, most of which cannot be grown in the laboratory. Two approaches dominate. Amplicon sequencing of the 16S ribosomal RNA gene, which contains conserved regions for primer binding and variable regions that distinguish taxa, gives a census of which bacteria are present and in what proportions. Shotgun metagenomics sequences all DNA in a sample, allowing identification at higher resolution and reconstruction of the functional genes present. The Human Microbiome Project, launched in 2007, characterised the microbiota of healthy adults at multiple body sites and showed that communities differ strongly between sites and between individuals, while their metabolic functions are more stable[53][54].

Clinical connection: the microbiome in medicine

Microbiome composition is associated with antibiotic-associated diarrhoea, inflammatory bowel disease, metabolic disease and response to some cancer immunotherapies, and gut bacteria can metabolise drugs directly. Faecal microbiota transplantation is an accepted treatment for recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection. For bioinformatics, microbiome studies pose specific problems: assigning short reads to taxa, handling compositional data (proportions that sum to one), and distinguishing contamination from signal in low-biomass samples.

Pharmacogenomics

Pharmacogenomics studies how genetic variation between individuals affects the response to drugs: how much drug reaches its target (pharmacokinetics) and what effect it has there (pharmacodynamics)[55]. The term pharmacogenetics is used for single genes and pharmacogenomics for genome-wide approaches, but they are often used as synonyms. The genes involved encode drug-metabolising enzymes (especially the cytochrome P450 family), drug transporters, drug targets and immune molecules (some HLA alleles predispose to severe hypersensitivity reactions).

The antiplatelet drug clopidogrel, widely prescribed after myocardial infarction and coronary stenting, is the canonical example. Clopidogrel is a prodrug: it must be converted to its active metabolite in the liver, mainly by the enzyme CYP2C19. Carriers of loss-of-function alleles of CYP2C19 (such as *2) form less active metabolite. Mega and colleagues showed in 2009 that, among patients treated with clopidogrel, carriers of reduced-function alleles had lower levels of active metabolite, less platelet inhibition and a higher rate of cardiovascular events than non-carriers[56]. The Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium (CPIC) guideline, updated in 2022, recommends that for patients with acute coronary syndromes or undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention, CYP2C19 poor and intermediate metabolisers should, if there is no contraindication, receive an alternative antiplatelet agent (prasugrel or ticagrelor) instead of standard-dose clopidogrel[57].

Clinical connection: from a genotype to a prescription

Pharmacogenomic implementation follows the central paradigm of Figure 1 in reverse. A laboratory genotypes a patient (genetic information); the genotype is translated into star-allele "diplotypes" and a predicted metaboliser phenotype (for example, CYP2C19 *1/*2 → intermediate metaboliser); the phenotype is mapped by a guideline to a prescribing recommendation; and the recommendation is delivered at the point of care, ideally by an alert in the electronic health record. Each step is a bioinformatics task: variant calling, allele assignment, phenotype translation, and integration with clinical systems. Allele frequencies differ between populations, so guidelines and tests validated in one population may not perform equally well in others, including Latin American populations.

Key ideas

  • Genomics studies whole genomes (structural, functional, comparative); the human reference genome GRCh38 has about 3.1 × 109 bases, of which only 1–2% code for protein.
  • The human genome has about 20,000 protein-coding genes (Ensembl 116 / GENCODE 50); proteins are far more numerous once isoforms and proteoforms are counted.
  • Transcriptomics (RNA-seq) and proteomics (mass spectrometry) measure gene products; metabolomics measures small molecules.
  • Microbiomics studies microbial communities with 16S amplicon and shotgun metagenomic sequencing.
  • Pharmacogenomics links genotype to drug response; CYP2C19 and clopidogrel is the reference example, with guidelines from CPIC.

Operating systems: why Unix dominates bioinformatics

An operating system (OS) is the program that manages the hardware of a computer (processor, memory, disks, network) and provides services to all other programs: it starts and stops processes, allocates memory, organises files and controls who may access what. The three families you will meet are Linux, macOS and Windows.

Almost all bioinformatics servers, high-performance computing clusters and cloud machines run Linux, a free, open-source operating system of the Unix family. There are historical and practical reasons. Unix was designed in the 1970s around a powerful command-line interface, a single hierarchical file system and the philosophy of small programs that do one thing well and can be combined. Biological data are mostly plain-text files (FASTA, FASTQ, GenBank, VCF, PDB), and Unix tools are extremely efficient at processing text. Most bioinformatics programs are written by academic groups for Linux first, and many are available only for Unix-like systems. Linux is free, which matters when a cluster has thousands of nodes.

macOS is also a Unix system (it is certified as compliant with the UNIX standard), and its Terminal application gives access to the same shell and most of the same commands; a small number of command options differ from the Linux (GNU) versions. Windows has a different heritage. Since 2016 it offers the Windows Subsystem for Linux (WSL; the current version is WSL 2), which runs a genuine Linux kernel and a Linux distribution such as Ubuntu inside Windows. For this course, a Windows user should either install WSL 2 or use Google Colab, which provides a Linux virtual machine in the browser.

The Unix shell from zero: files, directories and paths

The shell is a program that reads commands typed in a terminal and asks the operating system to execute them. The most common shells are bash (the default on most Linux systems) and zsh (the default on recent macOS); for everything in this course they behave identically. A command has the form program -options arguments: for example ls -l data runs the program ls (list), with the option -l (long format), on the argument data (a directory).

Unix files are organised in a single tree that starts at the root directory, written /. Every file has a path: an absolute path starts from the root (/home/student/project/data/hbb.fasta), a relative path starts from the current working directory (data/hbb.fasta). Two special names are always available: . means "this directory" and .. means "the parent directory"; ~ means your home directory. File and directory names are case-sensitive (HBB.fasta and hbb.fasta are different files), and spaces in names cause problems in the shell, so bioinformaticians use names such as hbb_mrna.fasta.

Pipes and text processing: grep, awk and sed on FASTA files

The real power of Unix comes from two ideas. First, most commands read text from their standard input and write text to their standard output. Second, the pipe symbol | connects the output of one command to the input of the next, so that simple tools can be combined into an analysis; the symbol > redirects output to a file. Three tools do most of the work on biological text files.

grep prints the lines that contain a pattern, which can be a fixed string or a regular expression (a small language for describing patterns; for example [A-Z] means "any capital letter"). With -c it counts matching lines and with -o it prints only the matching part.

Pitfall: line endings, hidden characters and spreadsheets

Files edited on Windows end lines with two characters (carriage return + line feed) instead of one; many Unix tools then see an invisible \r at the end of each sequence line and produce wrong lengths or failed matches. Convert such files with dos2unix or tr -d '\r'. Never open sequence or gene-list files in a spreadsheet and save them again: spreadsheets are known to convert some gene symbols into dates, and they may silently change long numbers. Keep raw data read-only and generate everything else with scripts.

Package managers, environments and containers

Bioinformatics software depends on other software: a given version of a program may require specific versions of libraries, compilers or other programs. Installing everything by hand on every computer is slow and error-prone, and different projects may require incompatible versions. Three solutions are standard.

Package managers download and install software together with its dependencies. pip installs Python packages. conda installs packages written in any language into isolated environments: each environment is a directory with its own versions of Python, libraries and programs, so that one project can use one version of a tool and another project a different one. The Bioconda channel distributes thousands of bioinformatics packages for conda, maintained by a community of volunteers[63]. A command such as conda create -n course -c conda-forge -c bioconda python=3.11 biopython blast mafft creates an environment with Python, Biopython, BLAST and the aligner MAFFT in one step.

Containers go further: a container image packages a program with its entire user-space operating system, so that it runs identically on a laptop, a cluster or the cloud. Docker is the most popular container system; Apptainer (formerly Singularity) is preferred on shared clusters because it does not require administrator privileges[64]. The BioContainers project provides ready-made container images for bioinformatics tools, many generated automatically from Bioconda recipes[65].

Workflow managers: Snakemake, Nextflow and Galaxy

A real analysis is a chain of steps: quality control of reads, alignment to a reference, variant calling, annotation, filtering, reporting. A workflow manager describes this chain formally, as a set of rules that state which input files each step needs and which output files it produces. The manager then decides the order of execution, re-runs only the steps whose inputs changed, runs independent steps in parallel, and records what was done. Snakemake uses a Python-based syntax inspired by the classic Unix tool make[66]; Nextflow uses a dataflow model and powers the community-curated nf-core pipelines[67]. Both integrate with conda and containers, so that a workflow specifies not only the steps but also the exact software of each step. Galaxy offers the same capabilities through a web interface, without programming: users choose tools from menus, connect them into workflows and share histories, which makes it popular for teaching and for laboratories without programmers[68].

Version control with git and GitHub

git is a version-control system: it records the history of a set of files (usually code and small data), so that every change is saved with its author, date and a message, any previous version can be recovered, and several people can work on the same project and merge their changes. GitHub (and similar services such as GitLab) host git repositories online, add tools for collaboration and code review, and are where most open-source bioinformatics software is developed and distributed. Perez-Riverol and colleagues give practical rules for scientists starting with git[69]. The basic cycle is: modify files, git add the changes you want to record, git commit -m "message" to save a snapshot, and git push to send the commits to the remote repository. Large data files are not stored in git; they are deposited in archives (for example ENA or Zenodo) and referenced by accession.

High-performance and cloud computing

Some tasks exceed a personal computer. Aligning the reads of a 30× human genome, assembling a genome or running molecular dynamics (Unit 5) requires many processor cores, hundreds of gigabytes of memory, or weeks of computing time. A high-performance computing (HPC) cluster consists of many computers (nodes) connected by a fast network and sharing a file system. Users log in remotely with ssh to a login node and submit jobs to a scheduler (Slurm is the most common), which queues them and runs them on compute nodes when resources become free. The rule on clusters is never to run heavy computations on the login node. Cloud computing rents virtual machines and storage from commercial providers by the hour, which is flexible and scalable but raises questions of cost and, for clinical data, of where data are physically stored and who can access them (Section 1.5). GPUs (graphics processing units), originally designed for video games, perform many arithmetic operations in parallel and are essential for deep learning, including AlphaFold, and for molecular simulations.

Data and many tools are accessed through a few large public institutions that you will use throughout the course:

Section 1.4Biological sequences. Mathematical models of biological sequences

Official curriculum (item numbered 1.3): «Secuencias biológicas. Modelos matemáticos de secuencias biológicas.» — Biological sequences. Mathematical models of biological sequences.

For a computer, a DNA molecule is a string of characters such as ATGGTGCATCTG…. Treating molecules as strings is a powerful abstraction: it lets us count, search, compare and model sequences with the tools of mathematics and computer science. It is also a simplification, because it discards chemistry, three-dimensional shape and the cellular context. This section first defines biological sequences precisely and then introduces the probabilistic models used throughout bioinformatics, from the simplest (each position independent of the others) to Markov chains and an introduction to hidden Markov models. We use the models to solve a real problem: recognising CpG islands in human DNA.

Biological sequences, alphabets and IUPAC codes

A sequence is a finite string of symbols drawn from an alphabet. DNA uses the alphabet {A, C, G, T}, RNA uses {A, C, G, U}, and proteins use the twenty standard amino acids, each written with a one-letter code. Sequences have a direction. Nucleic acids are written from the 5′ end to the 3′ end, the direction in which they are synthesised; proteins are written from the amino (N) terminus to the carboxyl (C) terminus, the direction in which the ribosome synthesises them. Because DNA is double-stranded and the strands are antiparallel and complementary (A pairs with T, C with G), every DNA sequence implies a second one, its reverse complement: complement each base and read the result backwards. The reverse complement of 5′-ATGC-3′ is 5′-GCAT-3′. Genes can be on either strand, so programs that search DNA must search both.

Real sequences are not always certain. A sequencing machine may be unable to decide between two bases, a position may be polymorphic in a population, and a primer may be designed to match several sequences. The International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC) and the International Union of Biochemistry defined standard codes for incompletely specified bases[74], which every bioinformatics program understands (Table 5).

Sequence file formats

Sequences are stored in text files with standard formats. The simplest and most universal is FASTA, inherited from the program of the same name[19]: each record has a header line beginning with > followed by an identifier and an optional description, and then one or more lines of sequence (you saw examples in Section 1.3). FASTQ is the format of raw sequencing reads: each record has four lines (identifier, sequence, a separator +, and a quality string with one character per base encoding the confidence of the base call); it is explained in Unit 2. GenBank and EMBL flat files contain a sequence together with rich annotation (source organism, references, and a feature table describing genes, exons and coding sequences); Unit 3 dissects a GenBank record line by line. Other formats store alignments (Clustal, Stockholm, SAM/BAM for reads aligned to a genome), variants (VCF) and structures (PDB and mmCIF, Unit 5).

Composition and GC content

The simplest description of a sequence is its composition: the count or frequency of each symbol. For DNA, the most informative single number is the GC content, the fraction of bases that are G or C:

GC = (nG + nC) / (nA + nC + nG + nT)

Because of base pairing, the GC content of a double-stranded molecule is the same on both strands. GC content varies between species, from below 20% in some bacteria to above 70% in others, and is a characteristic of a genome. In our data, the E. coli K-12 genome has GC = 0.508, the first 10 Mb of human chromosome 11 have 0.459, and the HBB coding sequence has 0.561: human coding sequences are generally richer in GC than the surrounding non-coding DNA. GC content also varies along a genome, which is why it is often computed in sliding windows. It matters in the laboratory: G–C pairs are held by three hydrogen bonds and stack more strongly than A–T pairs, so GC-rich DNA melts at higher temperature, which affects the design of PCR primers and probes, and GC-rich or GC-poor regions are sequenced less evenly by some technologies (Unit 2).

GC skew and the replication of bacterial chromosomes

Base pairing guarantees that the two strands together contain equal numbers of G and C, but nothing obliges a single strand to do so. The GC skew of a window of one strand is defined as

skew = (nG − nC) / (nG + nC)

In many bacteria the skew changes sign at the origin and the terminus of replication. Replication of a circular chromosome starts at one origin and proceeds in both directions to the terminus, so each half of the chromosome is copied with one strand as the "leading" strand and the other as the "lagging" strand. Mutational and selective processes differ between the two, so that the leading strand becomes enriched in G over C[75]. Plotting the cumulative skew, the running sum of window skews, makes the switch points easy to find: the minimum marks the origin and the maximum the terminus[76]. For the E. coli K-12 genome, our calculation in 10-kb windows places the minimum at 3,925,000 bp and the maximum at 1,545,000 bp (Figure 12); the minimum coincides with the region where the replication origin oriC is annotated. This simple statistic, computed from sequence alone, recovers a fundamental biological feature of the chromosome, and it is used to locate origins in newly sequenced bacterial genomes.

Words and k-mer spectra

A k-mer is a substring of length k; a sequence of length n contains n − k + 1 overlapping k-mers. There are 4k possible DNA k-mers: 16 dinucleotides, 64 trinucleotides, 65,536 8-mers, and more than 268 million 14-mers. The k-mer spectrum of a sequence is the table of counts of all its k-mers, and it is a surprisingly rich description. In the 4.64-Mb E. coli genome we observed 65,360 of the 65,536 possible 8-mers, 898,115 of the 1,048,576 possible 10-mers, 3,478,960 of the 16.8 million possible 12-mers, and 4,432,224 distinct 14-mers, which is 95.5% of all 14-mer positions in the genome. In other words, short words occur many times, but most words longer than about 14 bases occur only once in a bacterial genome; in a human genome, about a thousand times larger, the corresponding length is somewhat greater, because 4k must greatly exceed the genome size (416 ≈ 4.3 × 109) for most words to be unique. This is why 20–30-base seeds are unique enough to map sequencing reads to a genome, why k-mer graphs can assemble genomes, and why BLAST starts from short words (Units 2 and 4A). K-mer spectra are also used to compare genomes without alignment and to estimate genome size and sequencing errors from raw reads.

Dinucleotide relative abundance and CpG depletion

Comparing the observed frequency of a dinucleotide xy with the frequency expected if its bases were independent, fxy / (fx fy), reveals biological processes (Figure 13). In E. coli all sixteen ratios lie between 0.75 (TpA) and 1.28 (GpC). In human DNA one dinucleotide is dramatically depleted: CpG (a C followed by a G on the same strand, written CpG to distinguish it from a C–G base pair) occurs at only 0.29 of its expected frequency, whereas the reverse order GpC is at 0.99, and TpG and CpA are enriched (1.21 and 1.20). In the HBB coding sequence the CpG ratio is even lower, 0.14.

The explanation is chemical. In vertebrates most CpG cytosines are methylated (5-methylcytosine), a mark associated with gene silencing. Methylated cytosine tends to lose its amino group spontaneously (deamination) and become thymine; the resulting T·G mismatch is not always repaired correctly. Over evolutionary time CpG has therefore mutated to TpG (and, on the other strand, to CpA), which explains both the depletion of CpG and the excess of TpG and CpA. CpG is the most mutable dinucleotide in the human genome, and CpG sites contribute a disproportionate share of the single-base substitutions observed in patients. The exceptions are the CpG islands: stretches of a few hundred to a few thousand bases, often around gene promoters, in which CpG remains frequent because the cytosines are kept unmethylated[77][78]. In the Ensembl annotation of the first 10 Mb of chromosome 11 there are 196 CpG islands (median length 876 bp, range 404–4274 bp), covering 2.1% of the region. Recognising them from sequence is a classic problem, which we now solve with probabilistic models.

The zero-order (i.i.d.) model

A probabilistic model of sequences assigns a probability to every possible sequence. Models let us ask whether an observed pattern is surprising, compare hypotheses, and classify sequences. The simplest model assumes that each position is drawn independently from the same distribution (i.i.d.: independent and identically distributed), with base probabilities pA, pC, pG, pT. The probability of a sequence x = x1…xL is then the product

P(x) = ∏i=1…L pxi,    log2P(x) = Σi log2 pxi Probabilities of long sequences are astronomically small, so we always work with logarithms. For the 444-nt HBB CDS under its own base composition, log2P = -880.4, which equals −444 times the entropy computed in Section 1.1.

The model predicts how often a word should occur by chance. The expected number of occurrences of a word w of length k in a sequence of length n is (n − k + 1) ∏ pwj. For the EcoRI site GAATTC in the E. coli genome, with the observed base frequencies, the expectation is 1097 occurrences; we counted 646 in Section 1.3, only 0.59 of the expectation. Many palindromic restriction sites are under-represented in bacterial genomes, a pattern often interpreted as a consequence of selection imposed by restriction–modification systems. This is the basic logic of motif statistics: a model of "random" sequence defines what is expected, and deviations from it suggest biological function. The danger is that the conclusion depends on the model. If dinucleotide frequencies are not taken into account, for example, any word containing CpG will appear "significantly" rare in human DNA for a reason that has nothing to do with the word itself.

Markov chains of order k

The i.i.d. model cannot represent CpG depletion, because it ignores neighbours. A first-order Markov chain makes the probability of each base depend on the preceding base:

P(x) = P(x1) ∏i=2…L axi−1xi,    ast = P(xi = t | xi−1 = s) The transition probabilities ast form a 4 × 4 matrix in which each row sums to 1. They are estimated from training sequences as ast = cst / Σt′ cst′, where cst is the number of times t follows s.

A Markov chain of order k conditions on the k preceding bases and has 4k × 3 free parameters (each of the 4k contexts needs three probabilities; the fourth is determined because they sum to 1). The i.i.d. model is the Markov chain of order 0. Higher orders capture more structure, as the conditional entropies of Figure 5 showed, but need more data to estimate. Gene-finding programs often use fifth-order chains (45 = 1,024 contexts, 3,072 free parameters) because, together with the predicted base, they capture hexamer (dicodon) statistics spanning six bases.

Worked example: a Markov classifier for CpG islands

Durbin and colleagues used CpG islands to introduce Markov models in their classic textbook[B4]; we reproduce their approach on current human data. The idea is to build two first-order models, a "+" model trained on CpG islands and a "−" model trained on ordinary DNA, and to classify a new sequence by asking which model explains it better.

  1. Data. From the Ensembl annotation of chromosome 11, positions 1–10,000,000 (GRCh38), we took the 196 CpG islands. We assigned alternate islands to a training set (98 islands) and a test set (98 islands). As negative examples we drew random background windows with the same length distribution, at least 2 kb away from any annotated island and free of N (490 for training, 490 for testing).
  2. Training. We counted the sixteen transitions in each training set (adding a pseudocount of 1 to every cell so that no probability is zero) and normalised each row. The estimated probability that C is followed by G is 0.282 in islands and only 0.057 in background DNA (Table 6). Durbin and colleagues, using a different data set, obtained 0.274 and 0.078.
  3. Scoring. For a sequence x, the log-odds score is S(x) = log2[P(x | +)/P(x | −)] = Σi βxi−1xi, where βst = log2(a+st/a−st). The score is a sum of 16 possible terms; the largest, βCG = 2.32 bits, rewards every CpG. Dividing by the length gives a score in bits per base that is comparable between sequences of different lengths. S > 0 favours "island".
  4. Evaluation. On the held-out test set the length-normalised score separates the two classes well (Figure 14A–B). With the natural threshold S = 0, 100% of the test islands and 8.8% of the background windows are classified as islands; the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) is 0.998.

Pitfall: a good AUC is not a good gene annotator

The excellent AUC of Figure 14B was obtained on a favourable test: whole islands against background windows kept far from islands. In a real genome scan the classifier must decide where islands begin and end, the background includes CpG-rich repeats, and false positives accumulate over three billion bases. Moreover, the "truth" used for evaluation is itself an annotation made with sequence criteria. Always ask what a performance figure was measured on.

Codon usage

The genetic code assigns 61 sense codons to 20 amino acids, so most amino acids are encoded by several synonymous codons: leucine, serine and arginine by six, many others by four or two, and only methionine and tryptophan by one. Synonymous codons are not used with equal frequency, and the preference differs between species and between genes. A convenient measure is the relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU): the observed count of a codon divided by the count expected if all synonymous codons of its amino acid were used equally. RSCU = 1 means no preference; RSCU > 1 means the codon is preferred[79].

Figure 15 compares RSCU values in the 4,318 protein-coding sequences of E. coli K-12 with 233 human coding sequences (the canonical transcripts of chromosome 11, positions 1–10 Mb, retrieved from Ensembl). Arginine is the most striking case. E. coli uses CGT and CGC (RSCU 2.30 and 2.41) and almost avoids AGG (RSCU 0.12), whereas human genes use AGG frequently (RSCU 1.26). Leucine codon CTG is strongly preferred in both species (RSCU 2.99 in E. coli, 2.76 in human). In HBB, 15 of the 18 leucine codons are CTG (see the snippet below).

Codon bias has several causes: mutational pressure (genomes rich in GC favour codons ending in G or C), selection for translational efficiency and accuracy in highly expressed genes, which use codons matching the most abundant transfer RNAs, and effects on mRNA folding and stability[80]. It has practical consequences. A human gene expressed in E. coli for producing a recombinant protein may translate poorly if it contains many codons that are rare in E. coli, such as AGG; such genes are "codon-optimised" by changing synonymous codons. Codon statistics are also one of the signals that gene-finding programs use to recognise coding regions, together with the period-3 signal of Figure 6.

Introduction to hidden Markov models

The Markov classifier answers "is this whole sequence an island?" It cannot answer the more useful question "which parts of this long sequence are islands?". A hidden Markov model (HMM) solves this by adding a layer of unobserved ("hidden") states[81]. For CpG islands, imagine two states, "island" and "background". The model moves along the sequence; at each position it is in one state, emits a base according to that state's probabilities, and then either stays in the same state or switches to the other with given transition probabilities. We observe only the bases, not the states. Given a sequence, efficient dynamic-programming algorithms compute the most probable path of hidden states (the Viterbi algorithm), which segments the sequence into islands and background, and the total probability of the sequence summed over all paths (the forward algorithm), which serves as a score[B4].

Formal note: the ingredients of an HMM

An HMM is defined by (1) a set of states k; (2) transition probabilities akl = P(πi = l | πi−1 = k) between states; (3) emission probabilities ek(b) = P(xi = b | πi = k). The joint probability of a sequence x and a state path π is P(x, π) = a0π1 ∏i eπi(xi) aπiπi+1. The Viterbi algorithm finds argmaxπ P(x, π) in time proportional to L × (number of states)2, using the recursion vl(i+1) = el(xi+1) maxk[vk(i) akl], the same dynamic-programming idea as the Needleman–Wunsch alignment algorithm. To reproduce the first-order dependence of the CpG model, Durbin and colleagues use eight states (A+, C+, G+, T+, A−, C−, G−, T−), each emitting its own base deterministically.

HMMs are among the most important models in bioinformatics. Gene finders model exons, introns and intergenic regions as hidden states; profile HMMs represent protein families, with match, insert and delete states for each column of a multiple alignment, and are the basis of the Pfam database and the HMMER software that you will use in Unit 4B. Learning to think in terms of states, transitions and emissions now will make those methods much easier to understand later.

Key ideas

  • Sequences are strings over an alphabet with a direction (5′→3′; N→C); DNA has a reverse complement. IUPAC codes represent ambiguity (R, Y, N…).
  • FASTA (sequence), FASTQ (reads with qualities) and GenBank (annotated records) are the core formats.
  • Composition and GC content characterise genomes; GC skew reveals the replication origin and terminus of bacterial chromosomes.
  • K-mer spectra summarise sequences; most 14-mers are unique in a bacterial genome. CpG is depleted to about 0.3 of expectation in human DNA because of methylation-induced deamination, except in CpG islands.
  • The i.i.d. model multiplies base probabilities; it predicts expected word counts but ignores dependence. Markov chains of order k condition on the preceding k bases.
  • Two first-order Markov chains and a log-odds score classify CpG islands with high accuracy on held-out data.
  • Codon usage is non-uniform and species-specific (RSCU); it affects heterologous expression and helps detect genes.
  • Hidden Markov models add hidden states and allow segmentation (Viterbi) and scoring (forward); profile HMMs model protein families.

Section 1.5Regulations, fields of application and ethical issues in health

Official curriculum (item numbered 1.4): «Normativas, campo de aplicación de la bioinformática y temas éticos de su aplicación en el campo de la salud.» — Regulations, field of application of bioinformatics, and ethical issues of its application in health.

Genetic data are unlike other medical data. They are permanent (a genome does not change after a data breach the way a password can), predictive (they say something about future disease risk), familial (they reveal information about relatives who never consented), and identifying (a genome is the ultimate biometric). At the same time, sharing them is indispensable: rare-disease diagnosis, cancer genomics and pathogen surveillance depend on comparing data from many people and many countries. This section first surveys the applications of bioinformatics in health, then the ethical problems they raise, and finally the international and Chilean instruments that regulate them. The legal summaries are for orientation in a science course; they are not legal advice, laws change, and the official texts (cited) prevail.

Fields of application of bioinformatics in health

Clinical genomics and rare disease. Exome and genome sequencing are now first-line tests for many suspected genetic disorders. Bioinformatics pipelines align reads, call variants, annotate them with population frequencies and predicted effects, and filter thousands of variants per patient down to a few candidates that a clinical scientist interprets. In the pilot of the United Kingdom's 100,000 Genomes Project, genome sequencing of patients with rare diseases and their relatives yielded new diagnoses in about a quarter of participants, some with immediate consequences for treatment[82].

Pharmacogenomics. As described in Section 1.2, genotypes of drug-metabolising enzymes such as CYP2C19 guide drug choice and dosing[55][57].

Cancer genomics. Tumours are genetic diseases of somatic cells. Comparing tumour and normal genomes of the same patient identifies driver mutations, mutational signatures and therapeutic targets. The Pan-Cancer Analysis of Whole Genomes consortium analysed whole genomes of more than 2,600 tumours across 38 tumour types and showed, among other findings, that most tumours carry identifiable driver events[83]. Tumour sequencing panels are now used routinely to select targeted therapies.

Pathogen genomic surveillance. Sequencing bacterial and viral genomes from patients allows outbreaks to be traced, transmission chains reconstructed and resistance genes detected. During the COVID-19 pandemic, public sharing of SARS-CoV-2 genomes and phylogenetic tools such as Nextstrain[84] allowed variants of concern to be detected and followed almost in real time. This application uses alignment and phylogenetics (Unit 4B) at a global scale.

Drug discovery. Bioinformatics identifies and validates drug targets from genetic evidence, predicts protein structures (Unit 5), screens virtual libraries of compounds by docking, and repurposes approved drugs. The companion material on computer-aided drug design treats these methods in depth.

Other applications include newborn screening and prenatal testing (Unit 2), transplantation (HLA typing), forensic genetics, epidemiology of the microbiome, and the design of gene and cell therapies, such as the CRISPR-Cas9 editing of haematopoietic stem cells to reactivate fetal haemoglobin in sickle-cell disease and β-thalassaemia[85].

Privacy and re-identification

A common belief is that removing names and identification numbers ("de-identification") makes genetic data anonymous. In 2013 Gymrek and colleagues showed how false this can be. Short tandem repeats on the Y chromosome are inherited, like surnames in many societies, along the paternal line; recreational genealogy databases link Y-chromosome profiles with surnames. Starting from "anonymous" genomes in public research datasets, the authors inferred surnames by querying such databases and then, by combining the surname with publicly available metadata such as age and state of residence, traced the identities of several research participants and their relatives[86]. Since then, forensic investigators have used consumer genealogy databases to identify suspects through distant relatives, demonstrating the same principle on a large scale.

The lesson is that genomic data should be treated as potentially identifiable in all circumstances. Consequently, many genomic datasets are deposited under controlled access: researchers must apply to a data access committee, justify their use and sign agreements that prohibit attempts at re-identification. Technical measures (encryption, access control, audit logs, secure computing environments) complement legal ones. Aggregate data, such as allele frequencies, are generally shared openly, although even aggregate statistics can leak information under some conditions.

Informed consent

Informed consent is the voluntary agreement of a competent person to participate in research or to undergo a procedure, after receiving adequate information about its purpose, risks, benefits and alternatives. It is the central principle of the Declaration of Helsinki of the World Medical Association, whose most recent revision was adopted in October 2024[87]. Genomics strains the classical model in two ways. First, data and samples are often reused for purposes that could not be foreseen when they were collected. This has led to models of broad consent (consent to future research within a defined framework, under ethics-committee oversight), tiered consent (choices between categories of use) and dynamic consent (participants manage their preferences over time through an online interface). Second, results may concern relatives who have not consented. In Chile, Ley 20.120 requires that all scientific research in a human being have his or her prior, express, free and informed consent[W6], and its regulation, as modified in 2013, requires that consent be personal and in writing and that research be reviewed favourably by an accredited ethics committee[W7].

Incidental and secondary findings

An exome or genome sequenced to diagnose, say, a neurological disorder also contains information about thousands of other genes. A pathogenic variant unrelated to the reason for testing, found by chance, is an incidental finding; one deliberately searched for is a secondary finding. Should laboratories look for and report them? The American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) recommends that laboratories offer to report pathogenic and likely pathogenic variants in a minimum list of genes associated with conditions for which effective prevention or treatment exists ("medically actionable"), such as hereditary breast and ovarian cancer (BRCA1, BRCA2), Lynch syndrome, familial hypercholesterolaemia and inherited cardiomyopathies and arrhythmias. The current version, ACMG SF v3.3 (2025), contains 84 genes[88][W8]. Patients must be able to opt out, because some people do not wish to know their risk, and the decision affects relatives. Different countries and laboratories handle these questions differently, and the policy that will be applied should be explained to the patient before testing.

Genetic discrimination

If insurers or employers can see genetic data, they might treat people differently because of predicted disease risk. The United States' Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act of 2008 (GINA) prohibits the use of genetic information by health insurers and by employers; notably, it does not cover life, disability or long-term-care insurance[89][W9]. In Chile, Ley 20.120 prohibits any form of arbitrary discrimination based on a person's genetic heritage and establishes that the genetic information of a human being is confidential ("reservada")[W6]. Fear of discrimination is itself a harm: it can lead people to refuse useful genetic tests or participation in research.

Equity and ancestry bias

Genomic medicine is only as good as the data behind it, and those data are unequally distributed. Popejoy and Fullerton analysed genome-wide association studies and found that in 2009 about 96% of participants were of European descent; by 2016 the proportion had fallen to about 81%, mostly because of more studies in Asian populations, while people of African, Latin American and indigenous ancestry remained severely under-represented[90]. The consequences are concrete. Variant-frequency databases are less informative for under-represented populations, so a benign variant common in one population may be misclassified as pathogenic in a patient from that population; polygenic risk scores predict less accurately outside the populations in which they were developed; and reference genomes miss sequences present in other populations, which pangenome references begin to correct[28]. For Chilean and Latin American patients, who have mixed European, indigenous and African ancestry in varying proportions, these biases are a practical problem and a strong argument for local genomic research.

Clinical connection: one reference, many patients

The HbS allele illustrates both the need and the risk of population-specific knowledge. Its frequency is highest in populations with a history of malaria exposure in Africa, the Mediterranean, the Middle East and India[3], and migration has spread it worldwide. A screening programme designed only for the "majority" population of a country can miss affected newborns; conversely, associating a disease with an ethnic group risks stigmatisation. Equity requires data on all populations and careful communication.

HeLa and the use of samples beyond consent

In 1951, cells were taken from a cervical tumour of Henrietta Lacks, an African-American patient in Baltimore, without her knowledge or consent, as was common practice at the time. They became HeLa, the first immortal human cell line, used in thousands of laboratories and in discoveries from the polio vaccine to cancer biology. In 2013 two groups published the genome of HeLa cells[91][92]. Because the genome of the cells carries the germline of Henrietta Lacks, it revealed information about her living descendants, who had not been consulted. After discussions with the family, the U.S. National Institutes of Health agreed to place HeLa genome data under controlled access, with a committee including family members to review requests[93]. The case illustrates almost every issue in this section: consent, familial information, re-identification, commercial benefit and equity.

Benefit sharing and the Nagoya Protocol

Who should benefit from discoveries made with genetic resources? The Convention on Biological Diversity recognises the sovereign rights of states over their genetic resources, and its Nagoya Protocol, adopted in 2010, establishes rules for access to genetic resources and the fair and equitable sharing of benefits arising from their utilisation (access and benefit sharing, ABS)[W10]. The protocol concerns mainly non-human resources (plants, microorganisms, animals) and is relevant, for example, to drug discovery from microbial or plant biodiversity; whether and how it should apply to digital sequence information deposited in public databases is an ongoing international debate. For human data, the analogous concerns are captured by principles of data sovereignty for indigenous peoples, such as the CARE principles (Collective benefit, Authority to control, Responsibility, Ethics), which complement the technical FAIR principles[94].

FAIR data and the Global Alliance for Genomics and Health

The FAIR principles state that scientific data should be Findable (with persistent identifiers and rich metadata), Accessible (retrievable by standard protocols, with clear access conditions), Interoperable (in standard formats and vocabularies) and Reusable (with licences and provenance)[95]. FAIR does not mean "open": sensitive data can be FAIR under controlled access. The Global Alliance for Genomics and Health (GA4GH) develops technical standards and policy frameworks that allow genomic and health data to be shared responsibly across institutions and countries: file formats (such as those for aligned reads and variants), APIs for discovering whether a dataset contains a variant without revealing individuals, standards for describing consent and data-use conditions, and "passports" that certify researchers' access rights[96][W11].

Artificial intelligence in health

Machine-learning models are increasingly used to interpret images, predict variant pathogenicity, prioritise patients and predict protein structures[97]. They raise specific ethical problems: models trained on unrepresentative data reproduce and can amplify biases; complex models are difficult to explain to patients and clinicians; performance measured retrospectively may not hold in real clinical use; responsibility for errors is unclear; and training on health records requires lawful access to large amounts of sensitive data. The World Health Organization's guidance on the ethics and governance of artificial intelligence for health sets out principles including protection of autonomy, promotion of well-being and safety, transparency and explainability, responsibility and accountability, inclusiveness and equity, and sustainability[W12]. As Section 1.4 showed with a simple classifier, the first ethical duty of a model developer is to measure and report performance honestly, on appropriate data.

Chilean legislation

Chile has a specific legal framework for research on human beings and their genome, for patients' rights and for personal data, and it was the first country to give constitutional protection to mental integrity in relation to neurotechnology. The following summary is based on the official texts published by the Biblioteca del Congreso Nacional (BCN).

Ley 20.120 (2006), on scientific research in the human being, its genome, and prohibiting human cloning. Its purpose is to protect the life of human beings, their physical and psychic integrity, and their genetic diversity and identity in relation to biomedical research and its clinical applications. It prohibits eugenic practices (except genetic counselling), human cloning whatever its purpose or technique, and any arbitrary discrimination based on genetic heritage; it declares that knowledge of the human genome is the common heritage of humanity, so that nobody may claim ownership of it; it requires prior, express, free and informed consent for research; it establishes that a person's genetic information is confidential; and it requires that research projects be reviewed by a scientific ethics committee[W6]. A bill to amend the law in order to regulate human genome editing (Boletín 15076-11) has been presented to Congress.

Ley 20.584 (2012), on the rights and duties of persons in relation to their health care. It establishes rights to dignified treatment, information, and acceptance or refusal of treatment, and regulates the clinical record (ficha clínica): the information it contains is confidential, third parties not directly involved in the patient's care have no access to it, and it must be kept for at least fifteen years. It also establishes that every person must be informed and has the right to choose whether to participate in biomedical research, in the terms of Ley 20.120[W19].

Ley 19.628 (1999) and Ley 21.719 (2024), on personal data. Ley 19.628, on the protection of private life, classified data on health and physical or moral characteristics as sensitive data, but lacked a supervisory authority with sanctioning powers[W20]. Ley 21.719, published on 13 December 2024, substantially reforms this framework: it regulates the processing of personal data with standards close to the GDPR, strengthens the rights of data subjects (including data portability), imposes duties of security and breach notification, and creates the Agencia de Protección de Datos Personales[W21]. According to its first transitory article, the reform enters into force on 1 December 2026. As of October 2026, a bill to postpone the entry into force by one year (Boletín 18.623-07) was under discussion in the Senate; until such a bill is enacted, the date fixed by the law remains 1 December 2026[W22]. Students should check the current status on the BCN website.

Ley 21.383 (2021), constitutional reform on neuro-rights. It added a final paragraph to article 19, number 1, of the Political Constitution, establishing that scientific and technological development shall be at the service of people and carried out with respect for life and physical and psychic integrity, that the law shall regulate the requirements, conditions and restrictions for its use in people, and that it shall especially protect brain activity and the information derived from it[W23]. Although it concerns neurotechnology rather than genomics, it expresses a principle (protection of a person's most intimate biological information) that is directly relevant to bioinformatics.

Scientific ethics committees

In Chile, research with human beings must be reviewed and approved by a Comité Ético Científico (CEC) accredited by the health authority before it begins[W7]. The committee evaluates the scientific justification, the balance of risks and benefits, the consent process and documents, the protection of confidentiality, and the handling of samples and data. For bioinformatics projects, ethics committees typically ask: Were the data obtained with consent compatible with the proposed use? Are they identifiable? Where will they be stored and who will access them? Will results be returned to participants? Will data be transferred abroad, for example to a cloud service or an international database? Answering these questions is part of the bioinformatician's job, not only of the clinician's.

Pitfall: "public data" are not free of obligations

Downloading a dataset from a public repository does not release you from its conditions of use. Controlled-access datasets require approval; even open datasets may have licences, citation requirements or prohibitions on re-identification; and combining public datasets can create identifiable information that neither contained alone. Read the data-use conditions before you start, and record them with your analysis.

Key ideas

  • Bioinformatics underpins clinical genomics, pharmacogenomics, cancer genomics, pathogen surveillance, drug discovery and gene therapy.
  • Genetic data are permanent, predictive, familial and identifying; "de-identified" genomes can be re-identified (surname inference).
  • Consent models (specific, broad, tiered, dynamic), secondary findings (ACMG SF v3.3, 84 genes), genetic discrimination (GINA; Ley 20.120), ancestry bias in data, and benefit sharing (Nagoya, CARE) are the main ethical issues.
  • FAIR principles and GA4GH standards enable responsible sharing, often under controlled access.
  • International framework: UNESCO declarations (1997, 2003, 2005), Oviedo Convention (1997), Declaration of Helsinki (2024 revision), GDPR, HIPAA, GINA.
  • Chile: Ley 20.120 (research and genome; cloning prohibition), Ley 20.584 (patients' rights, clinical record), Ley 19.628 → Ley 21.719 (personal data; in force 1 December 2026 unless postponed by law), Ley 21.383 (neuro-rights), accredited scientific ethics committees.

Running caseβ-globin, haemoglobin and sickle-cell disease seen through Unit 1

Each unit of the course applies its own methods to the same biological system: the human β-globin gene HBB, the haemoglobin it helps to build, and sickle-cell disease, caused by the HbS variant. Unit 1 contributes the vocabulary, the information-theoretic view and the ethical frame. All values below were computed in this unit from records retrieved on 18 October 2026.

1. The gene and its products (genetic → structural information)

In Ensembl release 116 the canonical transcript of HBB, ENST00000335295 (HBB-201), lies on the reverse strand of chromosome 11 (GRCh38) between positions 5,225,464 and 5,227,071 and has three exons: exon 1 at 5,226,930–5,227,071, exon 2 at 5,226,577–5,226,799 and exon 3 at 5,225,464–5,225,726. Ensembl annotates 19 transcripts for the gene, illustrating the transcript diversity of Section 1.2. The RefSeq mRNA NM_000518.5 has 628 nt, of which 444 form the coding sequence: 148 codons, from the ATG start codon to the TAA stop codon, encoding the 147-residue β-globin of UniProt P68871. Two β-globin chains associate with two α-globin chains (encoded by HBA1 and HBA2) and four haem groups to form adult haemoglobin A (α2β2).

2. Information content

The HBB CDS has composition A 88, C 113, G 136, T 107, GC content 0.561 and order-0 entropy 1.983 bits per base, close to the maximum of 2. The protein has 3.95 bits per residue (maximum 4.32). Writing down the CDS therefore requires at most 444 × 2 = 888 bits, about 111 bytes; the whole protein sequence, at most 147 × 4.32 ≈ 635 bits. A single-nucleotide variant of the CDS is one of 3 × 444 = 1,332 possible substitutions, so specifying which one occurred requires log21,332 = 10.4 bits. Ten bits, the information in ten coin tosses, separate a healthy β-globin from the sickle-cell allele: a striking reminder that information content and biological importance are different things.

3. Sequence models

The HBB CDS contains only 5 CpG dinucleotides, an observed/expected ratio of 0.14, even lower than the chromosome-11 average of 0.29. The Markov classifier of Section 1.4 gives the gene body a score of −0.353 bits per base (clearly "background"), and Ensembl annotates no CpG island in the β-globin locus. The gene uses leucine codon CTG for 15 of its 18 leucines, a preference shared with human genes in general (Figure 15). Its splice sites follow the GT–AG rule of Figure 7: intron 1 begins at c.92+1, the position of the classical IVS-I-1 β-thalassaemia variants.

4. The sickle-cell variant

The HbS allele is catalogued in dbSNP as rs334, at chr11:5,227,002 (GRCh38) on the forward strand, where the reference base is T; the HbS allele is A on the forward strand, equivalent to c.20A>T on the coding strand, changing codon 7 from GAG (glutamate) to GTG (valine). Ensembl classifies it as a missense variant with clinical-significance terms that include "pathogenic" and "protective" (the latter reflecting protection of carriers against severe malaria). In protein numbering the change is Glu6Val, because the initiator methionine is removed from the mature chain. Its high frequency in populations historically exposed to malaria is the classic example of balancing selection (Section 1.1)[2][3].

5. Ethics and regulation

Sickle-cell disease raises in concentrated form the ethical questions of Section 1.5. Carrier testing reveals information about relatives and about reproductive choices, so it requires genetic counselling and informed consent. Newborn screening (Unit 2) detects affected infants before symptoms, but the result also reveals the carrier status of parents. Because HbS is frequent in specific ancestral groups, screening policies risk both under-diagnosis (if they ignore minority populations) and stigmatisation (if they single them out). Genome-editing therapies for sickle-cell disease[85] raise questions of access and cost in the countries where the disease is most common. In Chile, research involving these data would require approval by an accredited scientific ethics committee and written informed consent (Ley 20.120), and the handling of identifiable genetic data is subject to personal-data legislation (Ley 19.628, reformed by Ley 21.719).

Self-assessmentSelf-assessment questions

Answer each question in writing before opening the worked answer. Questions marked (N) are numerical, (C) conceptual and (A) applied or interpretive.

1. (C) Define bioinformatics and name its three main activities. Who coined the term, and with what original meaning?

Bioinformatics develops and applies computational, mathematical and statistical methods to acquire, store, analyse and interpret biological data, mainly molecular data (DNA, RNA, proteins). Its three activities are data management (databases, formats, curation, linking), method development (algorithms and models) and analysis with interpretation (answering biological and clinical questions). Paulien Hogeweg and Ben Hesper coined bioinformatica around 1970 to mean the study of informatic processes in biotic systems, that is, how living systems store and process information.

2. (C) Explain the "central paradigm of bioinformatics" with the example of sickle-cell disease, and give one reason why predicting the last level from the first is difficult.

Genetic information (an A→T change in codon 7 of HBB, GAG→GTG) determines structural information (valine instead of glutamate at residue 6 of β-globin), which determines biochemical information (polymerisation of deoxygenated HbS through the hydrophobic valine), which determines the phenotype (sickled red cells, haemolysis, vaso-occlusion, pain). Prediction is difficult because each arrow is uncertain and modulated by other factors: alternative transcripts, post-translational modifications, other genes (for example those controlling fetal haemoglobin), environment and treatment. Uncertainty accumulates along the chain.

3. (C) In what sense is natural selection "a virtuous circle", and why is the HbS allele frequent in some populations despite causing disease?

Genotype produces phenotype; phenotype affects survival and reproduction; reproduction determines which genotypes are transmitted. The circle increases adaptation to the current environment but does not optimise individual health. HbS homozygotes have sickle-cell disease, but heterozygous carriers are protected against severe falciparum malaria (Allison, 1954). Where malaria is endemic, the carrier advantage maintains the allele at high frequency: balancing selection by heterozygote advantage.

4. (C) How old is Homo sapiens according to the Jebel Irhoud fossils, and why is this relevant to genomics?

About 300,000 years: the Jebel Irhoud fossils were dated to approximately 315 ± 34 thousand years (Richter et al., 2017) and attributed to the early H. sapiens lineage (Hublin et al., 2017). In genomic terms this is short (on the order of 104 generations), so humans share most of their DNA sequence and differ at a relatively small number of variant positions, which are what clinical bioinformatics must identify and interpret.

5. (N) Compute the Shannon entropy of a DNA sequence with base frequencies A = 0.5, C = 0.25, G = 0.125, T = 0.125. Compare with the maximum.

H = −[0.5 log20.5 + 0.25 log20.25 + 2 × 0.125 log20.125] = −[0.5(−1) + 0.25(−2) + 0.25(−3)] = 0.5 + 0.5 + 0.75 = 1.75 bits per base. The maximum for DNA is log24 = 2 bits, reached when all bases have frequency 0.25. The sequence is therefore somewhat predictable: on average 0.25 bits per base less uncertain than random DNA.

6. (N) What is the maximum entropy per residue of a protein sequence? Why is the entropy of the average human amino-acid composition lower?

log220 = 4.32 bits per residue. The composition of reviewed human proteins gives 4.18 bits because amino acids are not equally frequent: leucine accounts for about 10% of residues, whereas tryptophan, cysteine and methionine are rare. Any departure from uniform frequencies reduces entropy.

7. (A) The order-0 entropy of the E. coli genome is practically 2 bits per base. Does this mean the genome contains no information? Explain.

No. Order-0 entropy depends only on base frequencies, which are almost equal in E. coli. It ignores the order of bases, where the biological information lies: genes, codons, regulatory sites. Measures that consider context (conditional entropy, mutual information at multiples of 3, information content of aligned sites) reveal structure that order-0 entropy cannot see. High entropy of composition is compatible with highly specific sequences, just as a text in a language uses all letters frequently but is far from random.

8. (A) Why does the conditional entropy of the 444-nt HBB CDS fall almost to zero at Markov order 8, and what general rule does this illustrate?

With k = 8 the estimate requires frequencies of 49 = 262,144 possible 9-mers, but the sequence contains at most 436 of them, most seen once. Each observed context is then followed by a single base, which makes the next base look perfectly predictable. The decline is a sampling artefact. The general rule is that the amount of data must greatly exceed the number of parameters estimated: sequence length ≫ 4k+1.

9. (N) Calculate the relative entropy D(P‖Q) of a position where P = (A 0.7, C 0.1, G 0.1, T 0.1) against a uniform background Q = 0.25 for each base. How does it compare with 2 − H(P)?

D = 0.7 log2(0.7/0.25) + 3 × 0.1 log2(0.1/0.25) = 0.7 × 1.485 + 0.3 × (−1.322) = 1.040 − 0.397 = 0.643 bits. H(P) = −[0.7 log20.7 + 3 × 0.1 log20.1] = 0.360 + 0.997 = 1.357 bits, so 2 − H = 0.643 bits. They are identical, because with a uniform background the relative entropy equals log24 − H. With a non-uniform background they differ, and the relative entropy is the correct measure of information content.

10. (C) Give two uses of mutual information in sequence analysis.

(1) Mutual information between bases at distance d reveals the period-3 structure of protein-coding DNA (peaks at d = 3, 6, 9…), which helps to recognise coding regions. (2) In alignments of structural RNAs, mutual information between two columns detects covariation (compensatory mutations preserving base pairs), which reveals the secondary structure; extended to proteins, covariation suggests residue contacts.

11. (A) In the donor splice-site logo of Figure 7, positions +1 and +2 carry almost 2 bits each, while position +6 carries little. What does this predict about the effect of variants at these positions?

A position with almost 2 bits is almost invariant: virtually all functional sites have G at +1 and T at +2, so a variant there is very likely to abolish recognition and cause aberrant splicing (exon skipping, intron retention or use of a cryptic site). A low-information position tolerates several bases, so variants there have, on average, smaller effects. Information content is therefore a first quantitative indicator of variant impact, used, for example, when HBB c.92+1 variants are classified as causes of β0-thalassaemia.

12. (N) A transcription factor has 50 binding sites in a bacterial genome of 4.64 × 106 bp. Compute Rfrequency and interpret it in Schneider's framework.

Counting both strands is a refinement; here use positions G = 4.64 × 106. Rfrequency = log2(G/γ) = log2(4.64 × 106/50) = log2(92,800) ≈ 16.5 bits. Schneider observed that Rsequence, the information in the aligned sites, tends to be close to Rfrequency: one would predict that these sites contain about 16–17 bits, equivalent to roughly eight fully conserved bases distributed over the site.

13. (N) How much storage does a haploid human genome need at 2 bits per base? How much as text with one byte per base?

With the GRCh38 golden-path length of 3,099,750,718 bases: 2 bits per base gives 3,099,750,718 × 2 / 8 ≈ 775 MB; one byte per base gives ≈ 3.1 GB. A diploid genome doubles both values. Raw sequencing data at 30× coverage with quality scores are about two orders of magnitude larger.

14. (A) Why did xz compress human DNA to 1.84 bits per base but not E. coli DNA below 2 bits?

Compression exploits redundancy. Human DNA contains many repeated elements (transposon-derived repeats, tandem repeats, duplications) that a dictionary compressor can encode as references to earlier occurrences. A bacterial genome is dense with unique protein-coding genes and has little repetition, so a general-purpose compressor cannot do better than the trivial 2-bit encoding (and is penalised by its overhead). Specialised DNA compressors that model reverse complements and statistical context do better on both.

15. (C) "Humans have about 100,000 genes." Correct this statement with current data and explain why the number of proteins is larger than the number of genes.

The 100,000 figure is a pre-genome estimate. Ensembl release 116 (GENCODE 50) annotates 20,107 protein-coding genes in GRCh38, about 20,000, among 78,733 genes of all biotypes. The number of distinct protein molecules is much larger because each gene can produce several transcripts and isoforms (alternative promoters, splicing and polyadenylation), and each protein can carry many combinations of post-translational modifications; the resulting molecular forms are called proteoforms. UniProt lists 20,431 reviewed human entries and 42,562 sequences when curated isoforms are included; the number of proteoforms may reach millions.

16. (C) Distinguish 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing from shotgun metagenomics.

Amplicon sequencing amplifies by PCR a variable region of the 16S ribosomal RNA gene from all bacteria in a sample, using primers in conserved flanking regions; it is inexpensive and gives a taxonomic census, usually to genus level, but no direct information on functional genes and it is affected by primer bias. Shotgun metagenomics sequences all DNA in the sample; it is more expensive and computationally demanding, but identifies organisms at higher resolution (including non-bacterial ones) and reveals the functional genes present.

17. (A) A patient with CYP2C19 *2/*2 genotype is scheduled for coronary stenting. What does pharmacogenomics predict about clopidogrel, and what does the CPIC guideline recommend?

CYP2C19 *2 is a loss-of-function allele; *2/*2 corresponds to a poor metaboliser. Clopidogrel is a prodrug activated mainly by CYP2C19, so this patient would form little active metabolite, achieve less platelet inhibition and be at higher risk of thrombotic events (Mega et al., 2009). For patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention or with acute coronary syndromes, the 2022 CPIC guideline recommends an alternative antiplatelet agent such as prasugrel or ticagrelor, if not contraindicated. (The actual prescription is a clinical decision.)

18. (A) Write a single Unix pipeline that counts the sequences in a FASTA file, and another that prints the total number of bases. Explain each component.

grep -c ">" file.fasta counts lines containing >, i.e. headers, one per sequence. grep -v ">" file.fasta | tr -d "\n" | wc -c: grep -v keeps only non-header (sequence) lines, tr -d "\n" deletes line breaks, and wc -c counts the remaining characters. (A header character > inside a description would not be counted twice, because grep -c counts lines, not occurrences.)

19. (C) Why are conda environments and containers important for reproducible bioinformatics? What does a workflow manager add?

Results depend on software versions. A conda environment fixes the versions of the language, libraries and tools used by a project, isolated from other projects; a container packages the program with its whole user-space operating system so that it runs identically on any machine. A workflow manager (Snakemake, Nextflow, Galaxy) formalises the chain of steps, their inputs and outputs, runs them in the correct order and in parallel, re-runs only what changed, and records what was done, ideally with a declared environment or container for each step.

20. (N) Under an i.i.d. model with equal base frequencies, how many times would you expect the 6-mer GAATTC in a random sequence of 4.64 Mb? Compare with the 646 observed in E. coli.

Probability of a given 6-mer = (1/4)6 = 1/4096. Expected count ≈ (4,641,652 − 5)/4096 ≈ 1,133. With the actual E. coli base frequencies the expectation is 1097. The observed 646 is about 0.6 of expectation, so EcoRI sites are under-represented, consistent with the general depletion of palindromic restriction sites in bacteria. The conclusion depends on the null model; a Markov model accounting for dinucleotide frequencies would give a somewhat different expectation.

21. (C) Explain the chemical cause of CpG depletion in vertebrate DNA and why CpG islands are exceptions.

Most CpG cytosines in vertebrates are methylated to 5-methylcytosine. Spontaneous deamination of 5-methylcytosine produces thymine, creating a T·G mismatch that is not always repaired to C·G; over evolution, CpG mutates to TpG (and CpA on the other strand). CpG becomes depleted (observed/expected ≈ 0.29 in our chromosome-11 sample) and TpG and CpA enriched. CpG islands, often at promoters, remain unmethylated, so their CpGs are not lost by this mechanism and retain near-expected frequencies.

22. (A) The CpG Markov classifier achieved AUC = 0.998 on its test set. List three reasons why its performance on a whole-genome scan would be worse.

(1) The test compared whole islands with background windows chosen far from islands; real scans must classify windows near island borders and define boundaries, which the model cannot do precisely. (2) The genome contains CpG-rich non-island sequences (some repeats, GC-rich coding exons) that raise false positives, and with billions of windows even a small false-positive rate produces many errors. (3) The "truth" is an annotation produced with sequence criteria, so agreement may partly reflect shared definitions; and training and test data came from a single 10-Mb region, which may not represent the whole genome. A hidden Markov model is better suited to segmentation.

23. (A) A research group publishes "anonymous" whole genomes of 200 volunteers with their age and region of residence. What risk does this create and how should the data have been shared?

Genomes are identifying. Y-chromosome short tandem repeats can be matched against genealogy databases to infer surnames, which combined with age and region can identify individuals and their relatives (Gymrek et al., 2013); relatives who never consented are exposed too. The data should have been deposited under controlled access, with a data access committee, a data-use agreement forbidding re-identification, minimal metadata, and consent that explicitly covered this form of sharing; summary statistics could be released openly. In Chile the project would also need approval by an accredited ethics committee and compliance with personal-data law.

24. (C) Summarise the main provisions of Ley 20.120 and Ley 21.719 relevant to a genomics project in Chile, and state the entry-into-force date of Ley 21.719.

Ley 20.120 (2006) protects the life, integrity and genetic diversity and identity of human beings in biomedical research; prohibits eugenic practices (except genetic counselling), human cloning and arbitrary discrimination based on genetic heritage; declares knowledge of the human genome common heritage of humanity; requires prior, express, free and informed consent; makes a person's genetic information confidential; and requires review by an accredited scientific ethics committee (regulation modified in 2013). Ley 21.719, published on 13 December 2024, reforms the personal-data framework of Ley 19.628 with GDPR-like standards, strengthens data-subject rights including portability, imposes security and breach-notification duties and creates the Agencia de Protección de Datos Personales. Its first transitory article sets entry into force on 1 December 2026; a postponement bill was in the Senate in October 2026, so the current status must be checked on bcn.cl. (This is not legal advice.)

25. (C) What are the FAIR principles, and does FAIR mean that genomic data must be open?

Findable (persistent identifiers, rich metadata, indexed), Accessible (retrievable by standard protocols, with clear authentication and authorisation where needed), Interoperable (standard formats and vocabularies), Reusable (clear licences, provenance and community standards). FAIR does not mean open: sensitive human data can be FAIR under controlled access, provided that their existence, metadata and access conditions are clear. GA4GH standards implement FAIR for genomic data across institutions.

The average remaining uncertainty about one variable when another (for example, the preceding bases) is known.
Container [contenedor]
A packaged software environment (Docker, Apptainer) that includes a program and its operating-system dependencies, so that it runs identically on different computers.
Controlled access [acceso controlado]
A data-sharing model in which researchers must apply to a data access committee and accept conditions of use before receiving sensitive data.
CpG island [isla CpG]
A region of a few hundred to a few thousand bases with high GC content and near-expected CpG frequency, usually unmethylated and often located at promoters.
Entropy (Shannon) [entropía de Shannon]
The average information per symbol of a source, H = −Σ pi log2 pi; maximal (log2K) when all K symbols are equally likely.
Exon / intron [exón / intrón]
Segments of a gene that are retained in (exons) or removed from (introns) the mature RNA by splicing.
FAIR principles [principios FAIR]
Guidelines that data be Findable, Accessible, Interoperable and Reusable.
FASTA format [formato FASTA]
Text format for sequences: a header line starting with > followed by lines of sequence.
FASTQ format [formato FASTQ]
Text format for sequencing reads with four lines per read: identifier, sequence, separator and per-base quality string.
GC content and GC skew [contenido GC; sesgo GC]
The fraction of bases that are G or C; the skew (G − C)/(G + C) measures strand asymmetry and changes sign at bacterial replication origins and termini.
Genome / genomics [genoma / genómica]
The complete hereditary DNA of an organism; the discipline that studies genomes as wholes.
Hidden Markov model (HMM) [modelo oculto de Markov]
A probabilistic model with unobserved states that emit symbols and switch between each other with given probabilities; used for gene finding and protein families.
i.i.d. model [modelo i.i.d. o de orden cero]
A model in which each position of a sequence is independent of the others and drawn from the same distribution.
Information content (Rsequence) [contenido de información]
The information in a set of aligned sites, the sum over positions of 2 − H(l) (or of the relative entropy against the background).
Informed consent [consentimiento informado]
Voluntary agreement of a competent person to participate in research or a procedure after adequate information; required in Chile by Ley 20.120.
IUPAC codes [códigos IUPAC]
Standard one-letter codes for bases and ambiguous positions (R = A/G, Y = C/T, N = any…).
K-mer [k-mero]
A substring of length k; a k-mer spectrum is the table of counts of all k-mers in a sequence.
Low-complexity region [región de baja complejidad]
A sequence segment with strongly biased composition (homopolymers, short repeats), detected by low local entropy and usually masked in database searches.
Markov chain of order k [cadena de Markov de orden k]
A model in which the probability of each symbol depends only on the k preceding symbols.
Microbiome [microbioma]
The community of microorganisms living in a habitat, such as the human gut, or the collection of their genomes.
Mutual information [información mutua]
The information that one variable provides about another, I(X;Y) = H(X) + H(Y) − H(X,Y); zero for independent variables.
Pharmacogenomics [farmacogenómica]
The study of how genetic variation affects drug response, for example CYP2C19 genotype and clopidogrel.
Phenotype [fenotipo]
The observable characteristics of an organism, produced by its genotype in interaction with the environment; in medicine, signs and symptoms.
Pipe [tubería]
The Unix operator |, which sends the output of one command to the input of the next.
Proteome / proteomics [proteoma / proteómica]
The set of proteins of a cell or organism; the discipline that studies it, mainly by mass spectrometry.
Proteoform [proteoforma]
A specific molecular form of a protein, defined by its exact sequence and combination of post-translational modifications.
Reference genome [genoma de referencia]
An assembled genome sequence used as a common coordinate system (for humans, GRCh38 or T2T-CHM13).
Relative entropy (KL divergence) [entropía relativa]
D(P‖Q) = Σ pi log2(pi/qi), a non-negative measure of how a distribution differs from a background.
Reverse complement [complemento reverso]
The sequence of the opposite DNA strand, read 5′→3′: complement each base and reverse the order.
Secondary finding [hallazgo secundario]
A clinically significant result deliberately sought in genes unrelated to the reason for testing (ACMG SF list); an incidental finding is one found by chance.
Sequence logo [logo de secuencia]
A graphical representation of aligned sites in which the height of each column is its information content and letter heights are proportional to frequencies.
Shell [intérprete de comandos]
The program (bash, zsh) that reads commands typed in a terminal and asks the operating system to execute them.
Splice site [sitio de splicing]
The boundary between an exon and an intron: donor (5′, usually GT at the start of the intron) or acceptor (3′, usually AG at its end).
Workflow manager [gestor de flujos de trabajo]
Software (Snakemake, Nextflow, Galaxy) that executes multi-step analyses from declared rules, inputs and outputs, reproducibly.
