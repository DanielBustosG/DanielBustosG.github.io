A trained model will return a number for any molecule you give it, including molecules it has no business commenting on. 1. The question: how far is an answer worth anything?

A quantitative structure–activity model is fitted on a finite set of molecules. It learns a function on the region of chemical space those molecules occupy, and it has no information about anywhere else. Asked about a molecule far outside that region it will still produce a probability, because that is what the arithmetic does, but the probability is an extrapolation of a fitted surface beyond any data that constrained it.

The OECD validation principles for (Q)SAR models make this explicit. Principle 3 requires "a defined domain of applicability", alongside a defined endpoint, an unambiguous algorithm, appropriate measures of goodness-of-fit, robustness and predictivity, and — where possible — a mechanistic interpretation.[2] The domain is not an optional extra: without it, the reported performance has no stated scope, and a performance figure without a scope is not a usable claim.

Several families of domain definition exist — range and distance-based methods in descriptor space, probability-density methods, and similarity-based methods in structural space — and reviews of the alternatives agree that no single definition is correct for all purposes.[3] For classification models specifically, the choice of domain measure materially changes which predictions are retained and how accurate the retained set is.[4] The study we are reading prespecified two criteria and required a molecule to pass both:

  1. an ECFP4 nearest-neighbour Tanimoto similarity of at least 0.40 to the closest training molecule, and
  2. a Mahalanobis distance in the 25-descriptor space within the chi-square 0.95 quantile.[1]

The rest of this notebook implements both, measures what they exclude, and then examines how stable those measurements are.

2. The similarity criterion

Why this analysis exists. The first criterion asks a structural question: does the query molecule have any close structural analogue among the molecules the model was trained on? For the paper's claim that external libraries lie overwhelmingly outside the domain to be wrong, a large share of CLUE, COCONUT or DSSTox molecules would have to find a UI-Ref neighbour at Tanimoto $\ge 0.40$. They do not.

Extended-connectivity fingerprints of diameter 4 encode each molecule as a sparse set of circular substructure environments up to two bonds from each atom, hashed into a 2,048-bit vector.[6] Two molecules are compared by the Tanimoto coefficient,

$$T(A,B) \;=\; \frac{|A \cap B|}{|A| + |B| - |A \cap B|},$$

which is 1 for identical bit patterns and 0 when no bit is shared. A threshold of 0.40 is a loose criterion by medicinal-chemistry standards — the conventional rule of thumb for "similar activity likely" sits nearer 0.7–0.85 for ECFP-type fingerprints, and the relationship between similarity value and shared activity is itself fingerprint-dependent.[7] Choosing 0.40 therefore makes the domain generous, which matters when reading the fractions below.

For the training set itself we must evaluate the criterion leave-one-out: the nearest neighbour of a UI-Ref molecule is its nearest other UI-Ref molecule, not itself. Forgetting this gives every training molecule a similarity of 1.00 and an out-of-domain fraction of zero.

def fingerprints(smiles, generator=ecfp4, nbits=2048):
    """Dense uint8 bit matrix, one row per molecule."""
    out = np.zeros((len(smiles), nbits), dtype=np.uint8)
    for i, s in enumerate(smiles):
        mol = Chem.MolFromSmiles(s)
        if mol is not None:
            out[i] = generator.GetFingerprintAsNumPy(mol)
    return out

def tanimoto(A, B):
    """Tanimoto similarity matrix between two sets of bit vectors."""
    A = A.astype(np.float32); B = B.astype(np.float32)
    inter = A @ B.T
    return inter / (A.sum(1)[:, None] + B.sum(1)[None, :] - inter + 1e-9)

def nearest_similarity(Fq, Fref, loo=False, block=2500):
    """Similarity to the nearest reference molecule; loo=True excludes self-matches."""
    out = np.empty(len(Fq))
    for i in range(0, len(Fq), block):
        S = tanimoto(Fq[i:i + block], Fref)
        if loo:
            np.fill_diagonal(S[:, i:i + block], -1.0)
        out[i:i + block] = S.max(1)
    return out

3. The Mahalanobis distance, properly

The second criterion works in descriptor space rather than structural space, and it needs more care than it usually receives.

3.1 Why Euclidean distance is the wrong distance

Suppose we describe a molecule by its molecular weight and its molar refractivity. These two descriptors are strongly correlated: heavier molecules are more polarisable. The cloud of training molecules is therefore a long, thin, tilted ellipse, not a circle. A query at a given Euclidean distance from the centre may be entirely typical if it lies along the ellipse's long axis, and completely atypical if it lies across it. Euclidean distance cannot tell those two cases apart, because it treats every direction as equivalent and ignores the correlation.

The Mahalanobis distance fixes exactly this. For a query $x$, a training mean $\mu$ and a training covariance $\Sigma$,

$$d_M(x) \;=\; \sqrt{(x-\mu)^{\top} \Sigma^{-1} (x-\mu)},$$

which is the Euclidean distance measured after the data have been rotated to their principal axes and rescaled so each axis has unit variance.[5] Its level sets are the ellipses of constant data density rather than circles; it is invariant to any affine reparameterisation of the descriptors, so changing units from daltons to kilodaltons leaves it unchanged; and when $\Sigma = I$ it reduces to Euclidean distance.

3.2 Traced by hand on a 2×2 matrix

Take two standardised descriptors with correlation $\rho$, so that

$$\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \rho \\ \rho & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \Sigma^{-1} = \frac{1}{1-\rho^{2}} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -\rho \\ -\rho & 1 \end{pmatrix},$$

and a query displaced by $(u, v)$ from the mean. Then

$$d_M^{2} \;=\; \frac{u^{2} - 2\rho uv + v^{2}}{1 - \rho^{2}}.$$

Read off the two cases with $\rho = 0.9$. A query displaced along the correlation, $(u,v) = (1,1)$: the numerator is $1 - 1.8 + 1 = 0.2$, the denominator $0.19$, so $d_M^2 \approx 1.05$ and $d_M \approx 1.03$ — close to the centre, as it should be, because such a molecule is typical. A query displaced across it, $(u,v) = (1,-1)$: the numerator is $1 + 1.8 + 1 = 3.8$, so $d_M^2 = 20$ and $d_M \approx 4.47$. The two queries are at identical Euclidean distance $\sqrt{2}$ from the mean, and their Mahalanobis distances differ by a factor of more than four. Note also what happens as $\rho \to 1$: the denominator goes to zero and $d_M$ diverges. That is the first warning about Section 4.

3.3 The chi-square cutoff and what it assumes

If the descriptors were multivariate normal, then $d_M^2$ would follow a chi-square distribution with $p$ degrees of freedom, where $p$ is the number of descriptors. The threshold is then the $0.95$ quantile, $\chi^2_{0.95, p}$, and the criterion claims to exclude 5% of molecules drawn from the training distribution. That claim is exactly as good as the normality assumption behind it — a point Section 4 will cash out, since in these data univariate normality is rejected for all 25 descriptors.[1]

Key idea

Mahalanobis distance is the right distance for correlated descriptors because it is affine-invariant and its level sets follow the data. The chi-square cutoff attached to it is a separate claim, resting on multivariate normality and on a covariance matrix that can actually be inverted. Accepting the distance does not oblige you to accept the cutoff.

4. Where the descriptor criterion breaks

Why this analysis exists. The Mahalanobis criterion requires $\Sigma^{-1}$. For that inverse to exist, the 25 descriptors must be linearly independent. They are not, and the consequence is not a rounding error — it moves a published percentage by a factor of four. For the paper's characterisation of this criterion as "an uncalibrated conservative flag" rather than a 95% probability statement to be wrong, the covariance would have to be well conditioned and the result insensitive to the choice of inverse.

4.1 Diagnosing the matrix

Two numbers diagnose a covariance matrix. The condition number is the ratio of its largest to its smallest eigenvalue, $\kappa(\Sigma) = \lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}$; it bounds how much a relative perturbation of the input can be amplified in the solution of a linear system. Double precision carries roughly 16 significant decimal digits, so a condition number above about $10^{16}$ means no digits of the inverse are reliable. The numerical rank counts eigenvalues distinguishable from zero at machine tolerance; a rank below $p$ means some descriptor is an exact or near-exact linear combination of others, and $\Sigma$ is singular.

Why would these descriptors be collinear? Look at the list. MolWt and ExactMolWt are the same quantity computed with average and isotopic masses. NumAtoms and NumHeavyAtoms differ only by the hydrogen count. MolMR, HeavyAtomMolWt, NOCount and NumHeteroAtoms are near-linear functions of composition. Collinearity here is not bad luck; it is what happens when you compute 25 descriptors from one molecular graph.

4.2 The consequence: pseudo-inverse against ordinary inverse

When $\Sigma$ is singular, $\Sigma^{-1}$ does not exist and a choice has to be made. The Moore–Penrose pseudo-inverse $\Sigma^{+}$ inverts only the non-null directions and sets the null ones to zero, which amounts to measuring the distance inside the 21-dimensional subspace the data actually occupy. An ordinary inv call does not fail — it returns a matrix built from the numerical noise in the four null directions, so displacements along directions that carry no variance are divided by almost zero and amplified without bound. Both choices are defensible lines of code; they are not the same criterion.

cut = chi2.ppf(0.95, len(DESC))

    d = Xq - mu
    return float((np.einsum("ij,jk,ik->i", d, M, d) > cut).mean())

Pitfall

Three defences against this failure mode, in order of preference. Prune the descriptor block before computing a covariance — drop one of every pair with |r| > 0.95, or prune greedily to a variance inflation factor below 10. Regularise the covariance: Ledoit–Wolf shrinkage pulls it towards a scaled identity by an analytically optimal amount and guarantees a well-conditioned, invertible estimate.[12] Report the sensitivity when you do neither. What you must never do is call inv on a matrix whose condition number you have not checked, and then quote the resulting percentage to two decimal places.

6. Sensitivity: the threshold or the representation?

Why this analysis exists. A prespecified threshold invites the obvious objection that the result was produced by the threshold. The paper tests that objection and finds it is the wrong objection: the threshold barely matters, and the fingerprint matters enormously. For the published conclusion to be wrong, the out-of-domain fractions would have to be stable across representations and unstable across thresholds. The opposite holds.

Two sensitivity analyses are therefore needed, and they are not interchangeable. A threshold sweep varies a number inside a fixed method. A representation sweep varies the method itself. Reporting only the first is the more common and the less informative choice.

7. Conformal prediction from first principles

The applicability domain answers a question about the training set: is this molecule represented? Conformal prediction answers a different question about this prediction: how wide must the answer be to be right a stated fraction of the time? The two are complementary, and the paper uses both.

7.1 The construction

Start from a fitted model and a nonconformity score $\alpha(x, y)$, a number measuring how poorly the pair (molecule, candidate label) fits the model. For a probabilistic classifier the standard choice is

$$\alpha(x, y) \;=\; 1 - \hat{p}(y \mid x),$$

so a confidently correct pair scores near 0 and a confidently wrong pair near 1. The score need not be probabilistic, or even good — validity does not depend on it, only efficiency does.

Now split the data three ways: a proper training set used to fit the model, a calibration set that the model has never seen, and a test set. Score the calibration molecules with their true labels to obtain $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n$. For a chosen miscoverage level $\alpha$ — we will write $\epsilon$ for the level to avoid clashing with the score — take the quantile

$$q \;=\; \alpha_{(k)}, \qquad k \;=\; \lceil (n+1)(1-\epsilon) \rceil ,$$

the $k$-th smallest calibration score. Then, for a test molecule $x$, return the prediction set

$$\Gamma^{\epsilon}(x) \;=\; \{\, y : 1 - \hat{p}(y \mid x) \le q \,\} ,$$

every label whose score is no worse than the calibration quantile. This is inductive — or split — conformal prediction.[9]

The guarantee it buys is

$$\Pr\!\left(y_{\text{test}} \in \Gamma^{\epsilon}(x_{\text{test}})\right) \;\ge\; 1 - \epsilon ,$$

and the argument behind it is a counting argument, not a distributional one. If the calibration and test scores are exchangeable — their joint distribution is invariant to permutation — then the test score is equally likely to fall in any of the $n+1$ gaps of the sorted calibration scores, so the probability that it exceeds the $k$-th is at most $\epsilon$ by the choice of $k$.[8] No assumption about the model, the features or the true conditional distribution is needed. Exchangeability is the whole of the price, and Section 8 is about paying it.

7.2 Traced by hand

Nine calibration molecules of one class give sorted nonconformity scores

$$0.02,\; 0.05,\; 0.11,\; 0.18,\; 0.26,\; 0.35,\; 0.52,\; 0.71,\; 0.94 .$$

At $\epsilon = 0.10$: $k = \lceil 10 \times 0.90 \rceil = 9$, so $q = \alpha_{(9)} = 0.94$ — with only nine calibration points the quantile is the maximum, and the interval is as wide as the worst calibration molecule. At $\epsilon = 0.20$: $k = \lceil 10 \times 0.80 \rceil = 8$, giving $q = 0.71$. At $\epsilon = 0.05$: $k = \lceil 10 \times 0.95 \rceil = 10 > 9$, so no valid quantile exists. This is not a coding inconvenience; it is the attainability condition. A level $\epsilon$ requires at least

$$n \;\ge\; \frac{1}{\epsilon} - 1$$

calibration examples per class when the quantile is label-conditional, which is 19 at $\epsilon = 0.05$ — the exact figure the paper uses to disqualify one of its stages.[1]

7.3 Mondrian (label-conditional) conformal prediction

Plain inductive conformal prediction guarantees coverage marginally, averaged over classes. With 178 potent molecules against 932 weak ones, a predictor could cover 100% of the weak class and 45% of the potent class and still report 91% overall. Mondrian conformal prediction computes a separate quantile $q_c$ within each class, so the guarantee holds conditionally on the true label.[10] For an imbalanced problem this is the version you want, and it is the version the paper used.

def conformal_quantile(scores, eps):
    """k-th smallest calibration score, k = ceil((n+1)(1-eps)); None if unattainable."""
    s = np.sort(scores); n = len(s)
    k = int(np.ceil((n + 1) * (1 - eps)))
    return None if k > n else float(s[k - 1])

Two responses are available. The honest conservative one, used below, sets $q_c = 1$ when the quantile is unattainable, which forces class $c$ into every prediction set: coverage is then trivially satisfied for that class and the sets are maximally uninformative, which is the correct way for a predictor to report that it cannot calibrate. The alternative — silently clamping to the largest calibration score — looks like it works and quietly breaks validity, and is the bug to watch for in your own implementation. The remaining cells use the five rotating splits, for which the quantile is attainable throughout.

def mondrian_conformal(seed=0, eps=0.10, n_estimators=300):
    """Inductive, label-conditional (Mondrian) conformal classifier.

    Returns per-split summary statistics and the raw scores needed for the
    exchangeability diagnostics of Section 8. An unattainable class quantile is
    handled conservatively by admitting that class to every prediction set.
    """
    tr, cal, te = document_grouped_split(seed)
    model = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=n_estimators, class_weight="balanced",
                                   random_state=RANDOM_STATE, n_jobs=-1).fit(Xs[tr], ys[tr])
    a_cal = 1 - model.predict_proba(Xs[cal])[np.arange(len(cal)), ys[cal]]
    pr_te = model.predict_proba(Xs[te])
    a_te = 1 - pr_te[np.arange(len(te)), ys[te]]
    q, attainable = {}, True
    for c in (0, 1):
        qc = conformal_quantile(a_cal[ys[cal] == c], eps)
        if qc is None:
            qc, attainable = 1.0, False        # conservative: always include this class
        q[c] = qc
    sets = [{c for c in (0, 1) if (1 - pr_te[i, c]) <= q[c]} for i in range(len(te))]
    covered = np.array([ys[te][i] in sets[i] for i in range(len(te))])
    size = np.array([len(st) for st in sets])
    return (dict(n_train=len(tr), n_cal=len(cal), n_test=len(te), q0=q[0], q1=q[1],
                 attainable=attainable, coverage=covered.mean(), mean_set_size=size.mean(),
                 singleton=(size == 1).mean(), both=(size == 2).mean(), empty=(size == 0).mean()),
            dict(a_cal=a_cal, a_te=a_te, docs=gs[te], covered=covered, y_cal=ys[cal]))

7.4 Prediction sets, not probabilities

The output is a set, and all four possibilities are informative, which is the main practical advantage over a probability.

  • A singleton $\{\text{potent}\}$ or $\{\text{weak}\}$ is a decisive prediction at the stated confidence.
  • A two-class set $\{\text{potent}, \text{weak}\}$ means the data do not distinguish the classes for this molecule at this confidence. It is not a failure of the method; it is the method reporting, honestly, that both labels are consistent with the calibration evidence. The printed both-class fraction is substantial here.
  • The empty set means neither label is consistent with the calibration scores — the molecule is more atypical than anything in calibration. It is a flag, strictly more informative than any probability, and it is the conformal analogue of "out of domain".
  • Set size is a continuous measure of how much the model knows about this molecule, and it is the quantity to monitor when tuning.

A calibrated probability of 0.55 and a two-class conformal set carry different information. The probability invites a decision by comparison with a cut-off; the set says that no cut-off is supported. Calibration and conformal prediction answer different questions,[13] and the paper reports both: Platt scaling reduced the expected calibration error of its ranking stage from 0.156 to 0.051, while the conformal analysis showed that 19.7–57.1% of prediction sets contained both classes at 90% nominal coverage, rising to 66.2% for the potency task at 95%.[1]

The sign is not the finding. A valid conformal predictor should reproduce the nominal level within sampling error from either direction, and systematic deviation in either direction — our under-coverage at every level, or the published over-coverage that grows as the level loosens — is evidence that the quantile is not estimating what it is supposed to estimate. Over-coverage is the more comfortable failure and the easier one to overlook, because the predictor looks safe while its sets are wider than the stated confidence requires. The next section finds out what is behind both.

8. The assumption, tested

Why this analysis exists. Conformal validity rests on exchangeability between calibration and test, and almost every applied conformal paper assumes it silently. The paper tests it. For its decision to report empirical coverage without a guarantee to be wrong, the nonconformity-score distributions would have to be indistinguishable between calibration and test document groups, and per-document coverage would have to be no more dispersed than binomial sampling allows.

Why would exchangeability fail here? Because the split is by publication, and publications differ. Each contributes a congeneric series, measured in one laboratory against one organism with one protocol. A model trained on some publications and calibrated on others is calibrated against the difficulty of those publications; a test publication with a different chemotype produces systematically different nonconformity scores. The exchangeability that holds for a random split of molecules does not hold for a grouped split of publications — which is precisely why the grouped split is the honest one.

A two-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov test compares the two empirical distribution functions through their largest vertical gap, $D = \sup_t |F_{\text{cal}}(t) - F_{\text{test}}(t)|$, and makes no distributional assumption.

8.1 What survives a rejected assumption

Everything, except the word guarantee. The machinery still produces prediction sets, those sets still have a measured coverage on this corpus, and the measurement is useful. What it is not is a distribution-free promise about the next molecule from a publication not in this corpus. The paper's disposition is therefore to classify every model-based stage as reporting "empirical coverage only, no guarantee", against four explicit conditions: coverage attained, no score shift, per-document homogeneity and an attainable quantile.[1]

For stage 1A, "on a single document-grouped split its coverage is 0.899 against a nominal 0.950, whereas over five rotating splits it is 0.940 (0.922–0.954) at alpha = 0.05 and 0.895 (0.873–0.913) at alpha = 0.10. Key idea

Exchangeability is testable, cheap to test, and usually false for grouped data. Test it with a two-sample comparison of calibration and test nonconformity scores and with a per-cluster coverage analysis. If it is rejected, report empirical coverage and say explicitly that you are not claiming a guarantee. Nothing else in the pipeline needs to change — but that sentence does.

9. The cascade that refuses

The paper assembles these components into a five-stage cascade, each stage with an explicit label semantics, a validation unit, a calibrated probability, a conformal set and a domain check.[1]

Stage Question Status in the published cascade
1A measured active vs measured inactive narrow-scope diagnostic; excluded from the general cascade
1B UI-Ref member vs property-matched untested ranking tool only; the negatives are untested, not inactive
2 potent (pAct $\ge$ 6) vs weak predictive, on measured values
3 structural-alert status deterministic SMARTS rule; a fitted model adds nothing
4 CLUE launched vs non-launched library-membership task; excluded from predictive claims

Stage 3 is the clearest case of a label that is not a prediction problem: alert status is an exact function of the structure, so fitting a classifier to reproduce it can only lose information. Stage 4 measures similarity to the composition of a clinical-development library, not the probability of approval. Both were retained as diagnostics precisely because they "demonstrate how apparently strong model performance can arise from labels that do not answer the intended scientific question."[1]

10. Applying this to your own model

The whole notebook reduces to a checklist you can run on any classifier you build, at a cost of a few hours.

  1. Define the domain before you look at the result. Fix the representation, the threshold and the distance criterion in a configuration file, and state them in your methods section. A domain tuned to the accuracy it produces is not a domain.
  2. Use a structural criterion and check whether a descriptor criterion adds anything. Here it added 1–2 percentage points at the cost of an ill-conditioned matrix. Compute the condition number and the numerical rank of your covariance before inverting it, and prune or shrink if $\kappa$ exceeds about $10^{10}$.
  3. Run a representation sweep, not only a threshold sweep. Report the span attributable to each, and if the representation dominates — it usually will — describe your fractions as a property of that representation.
  4. Split by cluster, three ways. Training, calibration and test must not share a publication, a laboratory, a patient or whatever your cluster is. Then check that the calibration set is large enough for your level: $n \ge 1/\epsilon - 1$ per class.
  5. Return sets, not only probabilities, and report the singleton, two-class and empty fractions alongside coverage. Mean set size is the efficiency metric.
  6. Test exchangeability. A two-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov comparison of calibration and test nonconformity scores, plus a per-cluster coverage analysis with a dispersion ratio against the binomial expectation. Two cells of code.
  7. Write the right sentence. If the test passes, you may claim coverage. If it fails — report empirical coverage, state that it is calibrated in aggregate over your corpus, and say explicitly that it is not a guarantee for a sample from a new cluster.
  8. Make abstention an output, with a cause. Out-of-domain, uninformative conformal set, and identity conflict are three different refusals and should be reported as three different messages.

Pitfall

Step 1 is the one most often violated, and the violation is hard to see in a finished paper. If you try several thresholds and report the one whose retained set is most accurate, the reported accuracy is conditional on a selection you did not disclose. The paper's own handling is the model to copy: the domain was "prespecified but not optimized", neither AUC nor MCC showed an interior optimum over the threshold grid, "no value was empirically optimal, and the prespecified threshold was retained."[1]

11. Summary

You should now be able to explain the following without notes.

  • A model is defined on the region of space its training data occupy; a prediction outside that region is extrapolation. OECD principle 3 requires the region to be stated.
  • A similarity criterion asks whether a query has a close structural analogue in the training set. At ECFP4 Tanimoto $\ge 0.40$, 93.3% of CLUE, 94.7% of COCONUT and 91.1% of DSSTox fail it, against 4.0% of UI-Ref under leave-one-out.
  • Mahalanobis distance is Euclidean distance after rotating to the principal axes and rescaling to unit variance; it is affine-invariant and its level sets follow the data. The chi-square cutoff attached to it is a separate claim requiring multivariate normality.
  • An ill-conditioned covariance destroys that criterion. Here $\kappa \sim 10^{20}$ with numerical rank 21 of 25, so $\Sigma^{-1}$ does not exist; pseudo-inverse and ordinary inverse give 10.84% and 48.11% for the same quantity on the same data. Check $\kappa$ and the rank before inverting.
  • The joint domain excludes 94.6% of CLUE, 97.0% of COCONUT, 92.8% of DSSTox and 13.6% of UI-Ref; the descriptor leg contributes only 1–2 percentage points of that.
  • Out-of-domain fractions are a property of the (molecule, representation) pair. The fingerprint moves CLUE's fraction by about 96 percentage points at a fixed threshold; the whole threshold grid moves it by about 17.
  • Conformal prediction needs a nonconformity score, a held-out calibration set and the quantile $\alpha_{(k)}$ with $k = \lceil (n+1)(1-\epsilon) \rceil$. The guarantee is a counting argument and needs only exchangeability. A level $\epsilon$ requires $n \ge 1/\epsilon - 1$ calibration points per class.
  • Mondrian conformal prediction computes the quantile within each class, so coverage holds conditionally on the true label — the right choice for imbalanced problems.
  • The output is a set: singleton (decisive), two-class (the data do not distinguish), or empty (more atypical than anything in calibration). Each is informative; a probability is not a substitute.
  • Exchangeability is testable. Here it is rejected for all three model-based stages, with KS $D$ = 0.166, 0.088 and 0.222 and per-document coverage 6–8 times more dispersed than binomial sampling allows, so the paper reports empirical coverage and not a guarantee.
  • Abstention with a reason is the correct output when identity is convention-dependent, the molecule is out of domain, or the conformal set is uninformative.

12. Self-assessment

Q1. Why must the applicability domain of the training set be evaluated leave-one-out? Otherwise every training molecule is its own nearest neighbour at Tanimoto 1.00 and the out-of-domain fraction is zero by construction. Leave-one-out asks the question that matters: does this molecule have a close analogue among the other training molecules?
Q2. Two molecules sit at the same Euclidean distance from the training mean, one along the main correlation of the descriptors and one across it. Which is more atypical, and which distance detects that? The one displaced across the correlation. Mahalanobis distance detects it because it rescales each principal direction by its standard deviation; in the worked 2×2 example with $\rho = 0.9$ the two queries have Mahalanobis distances of 1.03 and 4.47 at identical Euclidean distance.
Q3. What does a condition number of 1e20 tell you about the inverse of a covariance matrix in double precision? That none of its digits are meaningful. Double precision carries about 16 significant decimal digits, so an amplification factor of 1e20 leaves nothing. The practical signature is a negative smallest eigenvalue, which is impossible for a true covariance and indicates exact rank deficiency.
Q4. The pseudo-inverse and the ordinary inverse give 10.84% and 48.11% for the same criterion. Which is correct? Neither, in the sense that the criterion is not identified by these data. The pseudo-inverse is the more defensible choice because it measures the distance inside the 21-dimensional subspace the data occupy; the right action is to prune or shrink the descriptor block and to report the sensitivity rather than one number.
Q5. Why is the descriptor criterion described as contributing 1–2 percentage points? Because almost every molecule it excludes was already excluded by the similarity criterion. The joint out-of-domain fraction exceeds the Tanimoto-only fraction by only 1–2 points, so the expensive and unstable leg of the domain changes almost no decisions.
Q6. A reviewer asks you to justify the 0.40 similarity threshold. What is the strongest answer, and what is the stronger analysis behind it? That the conclusion does not depend on it: across the whole threshold grid at least 70.9% of every external library remains outside the domain. The stronger analysis is the representation sweep, which shows the fingerprint moves the answer by about 96 percentage points at a fixed threshold, so the honest reporting unit is "the ECFP4-based domain", not "the domain".
Q7. Nine calibration molecules, miscoverage 0.05. What is the quantile? There is none. $k = \lceil 10 \times 0.95 \rceil = 10 > 9$, so no calibration score is large enough to serve as the quantile. A level of 0.05 needs at least $1/0.05 - 1 = 19$ calibration points per class — the condition on which stage 1A of the cascade fails.
Q8. What exactly does exchangeability require, and why does a document-grouped split threaten it? That the joint distribution of the calibration and test nonconformity scores be invariant to permutation — weaker than independence, and enough for the counting argument. A grouped split puts whole publications on one side or the other, and publications differ in chemotype, laboratory, organism and protocol, so the test scores come from a systematically different distribution.
Q9. Empirical coverage deviates systematically from nominal across five splits. Name two distinct explanations and say how you would tell them apart. Does the direction of the deviation matter? Either the calibration set is too small for the level, or exchangeability fails. Check the first by counting calibration examples per class against $1/\epsilon - 1$; check the second with a two-sample KS comparison of calibration and test scores and a per-cluster coverage analysis. Here the second holds for all three stages and the first additionally for stage 1A. The direction does not decide whether the predictor is valid: our reconstruction under-covers at every level while the published analysis over-covers at the two looser ones, and both are symptoms of a quantile estimated on non-exchangeable data. Over-coverage is the easier failure to miss.
Q10. A prediction set contains both classes. Is that a failure? No. It is the predictor reporting that both labels are consistent with the calibration evidence at the requested confidence. Forcing a singleton by raising the miscoverage level buys decisiveness and loses coverage, and the published analysis shows singleton accuracy generally falls when you do it.
Q11. Why is stage 3 of the cascade not a prediction task, and why was it kept? Structural-alert status is an exact SMARTS function of the structure, so a fitted model can only approximate an answer that is already available. It was kept as a diagnostic, to show how strong apparent performance can arise from a label that does not answer the intended scientific question.
Q12. The earlier interface returned "non-inhibitor, probability 0.538" for a molecule already in the reference set with a measured IC50 of 7.3 µM. What went wrong, and which check catches it? Not the model: the molecular identity. Under one tautomer convention the molecule is UIREF-1160 with nearest-neighbour similarity 1.00; under another it is unseen at 0.34, below the domain floor. The check that catches it is evaluating both standardisation conventions for every query and withholding the prediction when their disagreement changes the domain assessment.

Glossary

Applicability domain
The region of descriptor and structural space represented in the training data, within which a prediction is interpolation rather than extrapolation.
Mahalanobis distance
$\sqrt{(x-\mu)^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)}$; Euclidean distance after rotating to the principal axes and rescaling to unit variance. Affine-invariant.
Condition number
Ratio of the largest to the smallest eigenvalue of a matrix; bounds the amplification of relative error when the matrix is inverted.
Numerical rank
The number of eigenvalues distinguishable from zero at machine tolerance. Below the dimension, the matrix is singular and has no inverse.
Pseudo-inverse
The Moore–Penrose generalised inverse, which inverts the non-null directions of a singular matrix and sets the null ones to zero.
Shrinkage covariance
A covariance estimate pulled towards a scaled identity, guaranteeing positive definiteness and a usable inverse at the cost of some bias.
Nonconformity score
A number measuring how poorly a (molecule, candidate label) pair fits the model; commonly $1 - \hat{p}(y \mid x)$.
Calibration set
Data held out from fitting, used only to convert nonconformity scores into a quantile. Must be exchangeable with the test data.
Prediction set
The set of labels whose nonconformity does not exceed the calibration quantile; may be a singleton, contain both classes, or be empty.
Exchangeability
Invariance of a joint distribution to permutation. Weaker than independence and sufficient for conformal validity.
Mondrian conformal prediction
Conformal prediction with a separate quantile per class, giving coverage conditional on the true label.
Empirical coverage
The measured fraction of test cases whose true label falls in the prediction set. Equals the nominal level only when exchangeability holds.
Dispersion ratio
Observed between-cluster variance of coverage divided by the variance binomial sampling alone would produce. Values above 1 indicate cluster-dependent coverage.
Abstention
Withholding a verdict, with a stated cause — out of domain, uninformative prediction set, or convention-dependent identity.
