A medicinal-chemistry project rarely designs one molecule; it designs a series of closely related molecules and asks, compound after compound, "what did that change do to the activity?" This unit is about answering that question with code. We take a set of related molecules and their measured potencies and extract the structure-activity relationship (SAR): which shared skeleton they are built on, how similar they are to one another, what the common substructure of two molecules is, how a series decomposes into a fixed core plus interchangeable R-groups, which single-atom-to-group swaps change activity and by how much, where the smooth relationship between structure and activity breaks (activity cliffs), and finally how to fit a first additive model that attributes activity to individual substituents. Every method here operates on the molecular graph (Unit 1), the fingerprints of Unit 5, and the clean, standardised, curated data of Units 4 and 7. The slides are a map; this notebook is the territory you can re-run.

1. Structure-activity relationships and the congeneric series

Drug discovery is an optimisation problem run mostly by hand. A chemist synthesises a molecule, a biologist measures how strongly it acts on a target, and the chemist reads the result and designs the next molecule. The relationship being learned — how changes in chemical structure change biological activity — is the structure-activity relationship (SAR). Cheminformatics exists in large part to make that learning faster, more systematic, and less dependent on one expert's memory.

Chemistry box — target, assay, IC50 and pIC50

A drug target is the protein (usually) whose behaviour the drug is meant to change — an enzyme to block, a receptor to switch. An assay is the laboratory experiment that measures how strongly a compound acts on that target. A very common readout is the IC50: the concentration of compound needed to inhibit the target's activity by 50%. A smaller IC50 means a more potent compound (less is needed). Because concentrations span many orders of magnitude (nanomolar to millimolar), we take a logarithm: pIC50 = -log10(IC50 in molar). So IC50 = 1 nM gives pIC50 = 9, IC50 = 1 µM gives pIC50 = 6; a larger pIC50 is more potent, and one unit of pIC50 is a tenfold change in potency. Treat pIC50 as "the activity number, bigger is better" for the rest of this unit.

Throughout, molecules travel as SMILES strings[23] (Unit 3) parsed into graphs by the open-source RDKit toolkit,[24] and we lean on the fingerprints, standardisation and data-curation habits built in Units 4, 5 and 7.

A congeneric series is a group of molecules built on the same skeleton and differing only in small, well-defined pieces — the systematic variations a chemist makes while optimising. It is the natural unit of SAR analysis because the molecules are comparable: if two of them differ by a single substituent and their potencies differ, the substituent is a plausible cause. We will use one small congeneric series throughout this unit so that each method builds on the last. The series below is an illustrative set of N-aryl benzamides: a fixed benzamide core with a substituent R1 on one ring and R2 on the other. The potencies are constructed (not measured) so that the behaviour of every method is transparent and reproducible — we state this openly because inventing data silently would be exactly the sin Unit 7 warns against. Later we also pull a slice of real ChEMBL data to show the same methods on measured potencies.

# An illustrative congeneric series: N-(4-R2-phenyl) 4-R1-benzamide, built combinatorially.
R1_contrib = {"H": 0.0, "F": 0.3, "Cl": 0.6, "OMe": -0.2, "Me": 0.2}   # true per-group effect on pIC50
R2_contrib = {"H": 0.0, "Cl": 0.5, "CN": 0.9, "OMe": -0.3}

    if sub_smiles[r2] is None:

    return f"O=C({ringA})Nc1ccc({sub_smiles[r2]})cc1"

Key idea

SAR analysis is comparison. Almost every tool in this unit answers a comparison question: how similar are two molecules, what do they share, what is the one difference between them, and does that difference move the activity. Hold onto that framing — it unifies similarity, MCS, R-groups, matched pairs and cliffs.

2. Molecular similarity and the similar-property principle

The oldest and most quoted idea in SAR is the similar-property principle: molecules that are structurally similar tend to have similar properties and activities.[1] It is the assumption that makes virtual screening work — if a known active molecule has a structural neighbour, that neighbour is worth testing — and it underlies everything from similarity search to the clustering of large libraries. It is also only a tendency — its reliability varies with the descriptor and the property, as studies of the "neighbourhood behaviour" of descriptors showed early on[18] — and the exceptions (activity cliffs, Section 7) are where the interesting biology hides.

To act on the principle we need a number for "similar". From Unit 5 we have the ingredients: a fingerprint turns a molecule into a fixed-length bit vector (a feature hash of its substructures), and the Tanimoto coefficient measures the overlap of two such bit sets. For bit vectors A and B,

$$T(A,B) = \frac{|A \cap B|}{|A \cup B|} = \frac{c}{a + b - c}$$ where a and b are the numbers of bits set in A and B and c is the number set in both. T runs from 0 (no shared features) to 1 (identical fingerprints). It is the standard choice for molecular fingerprints for good reasons analysed in the literature;[2] many other similarity coefficients exist (Dice, cosine, Tversky) and are compared in reviews of the field.[19]

Chemistry box — a fingerprint is a feature hash of substructures

A Morgan / ECFP fingerprint[3] looks at every atom, grows circular neighbourhoods of radius 1, 2, … around it, hashes each neighbourhood to an integer, and sets that bit. Two molecules sharing many local environments share many bits. For a programmer: it is locality-sensitive feature hashing on the molecular graph. Unit 5 built these; here we only consume them, through the one MFP generator created in the setup cell so all comparisons use identical settings.

2.1 A worked Tanimoto by hand

Before trusting the library, trace the formula on paper. Take two tiny fingerprints over six features, written as bit strings:

$$A = 1\,1\,0\,1\,0\,1 \qquad B = 1\,0\,0\,1\,1\,1$$

Bits set in A: positions 1, 2, 4, 6, so $a = 4$. Bits set in B: positions 1, 4, 5, 6, so $b = 4$. Bits set in both: positions 1, 4, 6, so $c = 3$. Therefore

$$T(A,B) = \frac{c}{a+b-c} = \frac{3}{4+4-3} = \frac{3}{5} = 0.6.$$

The next cell builds exactly these two bit vectors and asks RDKit's Tanimoto routine for the answer, so you can check the hand arithmetic against the machine.

# Confirm the hand-traced Tanimoto on two 6-bit vectors.
from rdkit.DataStructs import ExplicitBitVect
def make_bv(bits):
    bv = ExplicitBitVect(len(bits))
    for i, b in enumerate(bits):
        if b: bv.SetBit(i)
    return bv
A = make_bv([1, 1, 0, 1, 0, 1]); B = make_bv([1, 0, 0, 1, 1, 1])
a, b = A.GetNumOnBits(), B.GetNumOnBits()
c = (A & B).GetNumOnBits()                     # bitwise AND = intersection
print(f"a={a}  b={b}  c (shared)={c}  ->  T = c/(a+b-c) = {c/(a+b-c):.3f}")
print("RDKit TanimotoSimilarity:", round(DataStructs.TanimotoSimilarity(A, B), 3))

2.2 Similarity search

A similarity search takes a query molecule and ranks a collection by Tanimoto to the query — the single most common operation in a cheminformatics system,[4] and the 2-D counterpart of a BLAST search in bioinformatics. The same similarity matrix also drives the unsupervised clustering of large libraries into near-neighbour groups, for which simple threshold-based algorithms are standard.[22] We run one similarity search against our congeneric series, using the most potent compound as the query, and draw the top hits with their similarity scores.

# Similarity search: rank the series by Tanimoto to the most potent compound.
series["fp"] = series.mol.apply(MFP.GetFingerprint)
query = series.loc[series.pIC50.idxmax()]
sims = [DataStructs.TanimotoSimilarity(query.fp, fp) for fp in series.fp]
series["T_to_query"] = sims
ranked = series.sort_values("T_to_query", ascending=False).reset_index(drop=True)
print("query =", query.id, "(pIC50", query.pIC50, ")")
print(ranked[["id", "pIC50", "T_to_query"]].head(6).to_string(index=False))
top = ranked.head(8)

A common trap — "similar" is relative to the fingerprint

There is no absolute molecular similarity. Tanimoto values depend on the fingerprint (Morgan vs path-based), its radius, its length, and whether features are counted or just flagged. A Tanimoto of 0.4 can mean "quite similar" for one fingerprint and "barely related" for another. Always report which fingerprint and settings produced a similarity, and never compare values computed with different settings — the reason we fixed one MFP generator at the top.

3. Bemis-Murcko scaffolds

Chemists speak of a molecule's scaffold — the core skeleton that defines a chemical series, stripped of its decorations. Bemis and Murcko gave this intuition a precise, reproducible definition in a landmark 1996 analysis of the frameworks of known drugs.[5] Their recipe:

  1. Identify the ring systems: every set of rings connected by shared atoms or bonds (a fused bicyclic counts as one ring system).
  2. Identify the linkers: chains of atoms that lie on a path between two ring systems.
  3. The Bemis-Murcko scaffold (also called the Murcko framework) is the union of all ring systems and all linkers. Everything else — the side chains, atoms hanging off the framework that lead nowhere — is discarded.

Chemistry box — ring system, linker, side chain

A ring system is one or more rings fused or bridged together (naphthalene's two fused hexagons are one ring system). A linker is a bridge of non-ring atoms connecting two ring systems — for example the single carbon joining the two phenyls of diphenylmethane. A side chain is a branch that dead-ends without reaching another ring (a methyl, an ethylamino tail). The scaffold keeps rings and linkers and throws away side chains. For a programmer: it is a graph-pruning operation — repeatedly delete terminal non-ring atoms until only rings and the paths between them remain.

Scaffolds matter because they organise chemical space: libraries are grouped by scaffold, patents claim scaffolds, and a project's whole chemistry often lives on one or two of them. The same analysis gives a companion object, the molecule's side-chain inventory, studied in the follow-up paper.[6]

3.1 Worked example by hand: the Murcko scaffold of diphenhydramine

Diphenhydramine (the antihistamine in many sleep aids) has the structure CN(C)CCOC(c1ccccc1)c1ccccc1. Let us extract its scaffold on paper before asking RDKit.

  • Ring systems: two separate benzene rings. Neither is fused to anything, so there are two ring systems, six carbons each.
  • Linkers: the two rings are joined through a single central carbon (the CH that carries both phenyls). That carbon lies on the path between the two rings, so it is a linker atom and is kept.
  • Side chains: hanging off the central carbon is the chain -O-CH2-CH2-N(CH3)2. It dead-ends at the dimethylamino group without reaching a second ring, so the whole -OCCN(C)C branch is a side chain and is deleted. The two N-methyl groups go with it.

What remains is two benzene rings joined by one carbon: diphenylmethane, C(c1ccccc1)c1ccccc1. The cell confirms it and draws the molecule with its scaffold highlighted.

A subtlety — exocyclic double bonds are kept

The textbook rule says "keep rings and linkers", but RDKit's default Murcko also keeps double-bonded atoms attached to the framework, such as a carbonyl oxygen on a ring or linker. Run the next cell on our benzamide series and you will see the scaffold retains the C=O: the standard definition treats a doubly-bonded exocyclic atom as part of the framework, not a side chain. This is a real convention difference, worth knowing before you count scaffolds and wonder why a carbonyl survived.

3.2 The generic framework

One scaffold can appear with different atom types — a pyridine here, a benzene there — that a chemist still regards as "the same shape". Bemis and Murcko therefore also defined the generic framework (or graph framework): take the scaffold and replace every atom by carbon and every bond by a single bond, keeping only the topology.[5] It is the scaffold reduced to a pure graph, and it groups scaffolds that are topologically identical but chemically different.

# Scaffold vs generic graph framework for two different-element cores that share a shape.
pairs = {"N-phenylbenzamide core": "O=C(Nc1ccccc1)c1ccccc1",
         "a pyridyl analogue":     "O=C(Nc1ccccc1)c1ccccn1"}
rows = []
for name, smi in pairs.items():
    m = Chem.MolFromSmiles(smi)
    sc = MurckoScaffold.GetScaffoldForMol(m)
    gen = MurckoScaffold.MakeScaffoldGeneric(sc)
    rows.append((name, Chem.MolToSmiles(sc), Chem.MolToSmiles(gen)))
gdf = pd.DataFrame(rows, columns=["molecule", "Murcko scaffold", "generic framework"])
print(gdf.to_string(index=False))
print("same scaffold?", gdf["Murcko scaffold"].nunique() == 1,
      "| same generic framework?", gdf["generic framework"].nunique() == 1)

The two cores have different Murcko scaffolds (one has a ring nitrogen, one does not) but the same generic framework, because replacing every atom with carbon erases the nitrogen. This two-level view — scaffold for chemical identity, generic framework for topology — is how large libraries are organised into a hierarchy; the scaffold tree[7] extends it by iteratively removing rings to build a navigable scaffold network.

3.3 Scaffold diversity of a real dataset

Our illustrative series has one scaffold by construction. A real project dataset has many, and counting them answers a practical question: how much scaffold diversity does this set of actives cover? The cell below fetches a small slice of real inhibitor data for a single target — the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR, ChEMBL target CHEMBL203, a kinase central to several cancers) — from the ChEMBL database[8] over the network, keeping only IC50 measurements with a usable pChEMBL value. The network call has a short timeout and is wrapped in try/except: if ChEMBL is unreachable (as it may be in a build sandbox), the cell falls back to a small bundled sample so the notebook always runs. When you open this notebook in Colab the live fetch will normally succeed.

# Fetch a small EGFR IC50 slice from ChEMBL; fall back to a bundled sample if the network is unavailable.
import urllib.request, json
def fetch_chembl(target="CHEMBL203", n=400, timeout=20):
    url = ("https://www.ebi.ac.uk/chembl/api/data/activity.json?"
           f"target_chembl_id={target}&standard_type=IC50&pchembl_value__isnull=false&limit={n}")
    req = urllib.request.Request(url, headers={"User-Agent": "cheminf-course"})
    acts = json.load(urllib.request.urlopen(req, timeout=timeout))["activities"]

4. Maximum common substructure

A scaffold is one way to find what a set of molecules share; the maximum common substructure (MCS) is another, and a more general one. The MCS of two (or more) molecules is the largest substructure — the largest connected set of atoms and bonds — present in all of them. Unlike the Murcko scaffold, which is defined per molecule, the MCS is defined across a set and need not be a ring system: it is whatever the molecules actually have in common. Finding it is an instance of the maximum common subgraph problem, which is NP-hard in general;[9] RDKit's rdFMCS solves it efficiently for realistic molecules with a branch-and-bound search and a configurable timeout.

Chemistry box — why the MCS is useful

When you have a handful of actives and want to know "what part is conserved", the MCS gives it directly from the structures, with no need to guess a core. It is used to align a series for drawing (so the common part is in the same place in every picture), to define a core for R-group decomposition (Section 6), and as the basis of a similarity measure (two molecules are similar if their MCS is large relative to their sizes). For a computer scientist: it is the largest common subgraph, the graph analogue of the longest common subsequence.

5. R-group decomposition

Once a series is known to share a core, the natural next step is to split every molecule into that constant core plus the variable pieces attached at defined positions — the R-groups. This is R-group decomposition, and it turns a set of structures into a tidy table: one row per molecule, one column per attachment point, each cell holding the fragment at that point. It is the structural form of the data that classical QSAR (Unit 12) and the Free-Wilson model (Section 8) consume.

Chemistry box — core and R-groups

Write a series as a template with labelled attachment points: core-R1, core-R1-R2, and so on. Each R-group is the actual substituent found at that point in a given molecule (R1 = Cl, R2 = OCH3, …), including "just a hydrogen" when nothing is substituted there. For a programmer: it is parsing each molecule against a template and reading off the fields — like matching a string against a pattern with capture groups, where the captures are subgraphs.

R-group decomposition silently drops (or mis-assigns) any molecule the core does not match. If your "series" is really two series on two scaffolds, forcing one core onto all of it produces a table full of surprises. Always check how many molecules were decomposed versus how many you put in, and prefer a core derived from the MCS or Murcko scaffold of the set rather than one typed by hand.

6. Matched molecular pairs

An R-group table tells you which substituents appear; a matched molecular pair (MMP) tells you what happens when you swap one for another. An MMP is a pair of molecules that differ by a single, well-defined structural change at one site — for example "replace H by Cl at this position" — with everything else held constant. The change itself is called the transformation. By collecting every pair in a dataset that shares the same transformation and averaging the change in activity, you estimate the effect of that transformation across many contexts: "on average, adding a chloro here raises pIC50 by 0.6". This is one of the most useful ideas in modern medicinal chemistry[10] because it is a controlled experiment — the matched context is the control.

Chemistry box — a matched pair is a controlled comparison

If two molecules differ in ten places and one is more potent, you cannot say which change mattered. If they differ in exactly one place, the activity difference is attributable to that one change. MMP analysis finds all such single-change pairs automatically and tabulates the transformations. For a programmer: fragment every molecule by cutting one bond, index the fragments by the constant part (the "key"), and any two molecules that share a key but differ in the variable part form a matched pair — a self-join on the constant fragment.

The algorithm we implement is the fragment-and-index approach of Hussain and Rea.[11] For each molecule we cut every single acyclic bond in turn, producing a (constant context, variable fragment) split; molecules sharing the same context, but with different variable fragments, are a matched pair, and the ordered pair of variable fragments is the transformation. (More chemically guided fragmentation rules, such as RECAP's retrosynthetic bond set,[21] restrict the cuts to bonds a chemist could actually make; our exhaustive single cut is the simplest version.)

# Single-cut fragmentation: split each molecule into (context, variable fragment) at every acyclic single bond.
def single_cut_fragments(mol):
    out = []
    for bond in mol.GetBonds():
        if bond.IsInRing() or bond.GetBondType() != Chem.BondType.SINGLE:
            continue
        if bond.GetBeginAtom().GetAtomicNum() == 1 or bond.GetEndAtom().GetAtomicNum() == 1:
            continue
        frag = Chem.FragmentOnBonds(mol, [bond.GetIdx()], addDummies=True, dummyLabels=[(0, 0)])
        pieces = Chem.GetMolFrags(frag, asMols=True, sanitizeFrags=False)
        if len(pieces) != 2:
            continue
        p0, p1 = (Chem.MolToSmiles(p) for p in pieces)
        # the larger piece is the constant context (key); the smaller is the variable fragment
        if pieces[0].GetNumAtoms() >= pieces[1].GetNumAtoms():
            context, variable = p0, p1
        else:
            context, variable = p1, p0
        out.append((context, variable))
    return out

7. Activity cliffs and the SALI

The similar-property principle promises that similar molecules have similar activity. An activity cliff is a pair of molecules that are very similar yet have very different activity — the principle's dramatic exception.[13] Cliffs are not noise to be smoothed away; they are the most information-rich pairs in a dataset, because a large activity change from a tiny structural change points at a specific, exploitable interaction in the binding site. They are also the pairs that break predictive models, which is why they receive so much attention,[14] and a substantial literature now studies how to find, visualise and exploit them systematically.[25] Network-like similarity graphs that lay a series out as nodes (compounds) and edges (similar pairs), coloured by activity, make such cliffs visible at a glance.[20]

Chemistry box — what a cliff tells you about the target

If adding a single small group makes a compound a hundred times more potent, that group is probably forming a specific new contact with the protein — filling a pocket, making a hydrogen bond, displacing an unhappy water. The cliff localises the SAR to one interaction. Conversely a cliff that kills activity flags a steric clash or a lost contact. Reading cliffs is how medicinal chemists reverse-engineer a binding site they often cannot see directly (that structural view belongs to the companion bioinformatics course).

To rank cliffs we use the structure-activity landscape index (SALI) of Guha and Van Drie.[15] For a pair of molecules i and j with activities A and similarity sim,

$$\mathrm{SALI}_{ij} = \frac{|A_i - A_j|}{1 - \mathrm{sim}_{ij}}$$ The numerator is the activity gap; the denominator is the structural dissimilarity. A large activity gap between near-identical molecules (similarity near 1, denominator near 0) gives a large SALI — a steep cliff. Pairs that are very different or similarly active give small SALI.
# Compute SALI for every pair in the series and rank the steepest cliffs.

8. A first Free-Wilson model

We close with the oldest quantitative SAR model, and the one that ties the whole unit together. In 1964 Free and Wilson proposed that a molecule's activity can be written as a baseline plus the sum of contributions from the substituents it carries.[16] If each R-group at each position has its own additive effect, then

$$\text{pIC50} = \mu + \sum_{\text{positions } p}\ \sum_{\text{groups } g} c_{p,g}\, x_{p,g}$$ where $x_{p,g}=1$ if group g sits at position p in this molecule and 0 otherwise, $c_{p,g}$ is that group's contribution, and $\mu$ is the baseline activity of the reference compound (all positions at their reference group, here H). It is an ordinary linear regression on indicator variables — one-hot encoding of the R-group table.

Chemistry box — additivity, and why it is a strong assumption

Free-Wilson assumes substituent effects are independent and additive: the benefit of a chloro here does not depend on what sits there. That is often approximately true within a congeneric series — which is why the model, and its descriptor-based cousin the Hansch analysis, launched quantitative SAR[17] and why MMP (Section 6) works at all. But it is exactly what an activity cliff violates: a cliff is a pair whose activity is not the sum of its parts. So a Free-Wilson model is both a useful predictor and a cliff detector — the compounds it predicts worst are the non-additive ones.

This connects three threads of the unit: the R-group table (Section 5) is the model's input, the matched-pair effects (Section 6) are essentially its coefficients estimated pairwise, and the activity cliff (Section 7) is the data point it must fail on. We build the design matrix by one-hot encoding our known R1/R2 assignments, fit it with ordinary least squares, and read the coefficients as group contributions.

# Build the Free-Wilson design matrix: one-hot indicators for each non-reference R-group (reference = H).
import itertools
r1_levels = [g for g in R1_contrib if g != "H"]     # reference level H is absorbed into the intercept
r2_levels = [g for g in R2_contrib if g != "H"]
feat_names = [f"R1={g}" for g in r1_levels] + [f"R2={g}" for g in r2_levels]
X = np.array([[1.0] + [1.0 if r.R1 == g else 0.0 for g in r1_levels]
                     + [1.0 if r.R2 == g else 0.0 for g in r2_levels] for r in series.itertuples()])
y = series.pIC50.values
# Ordinary least squares by the normal equations: beta = (X'X)^-1 X'y
beta, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)
intercept = beta[0]; coefs = dict(zip(feat_names, beta[1:]))
print("baseline (H/H) mu = %.2f   [built-in truth: 6.00]" % intercept)
for name, c in coefs.items():
    truth = (R1_contrib if name.startswith("R1") else R2_contrib)[name.split("=")[1]]
    print(f"  {name:8s} contribution {c:+.2f}   [truth {truth:+.2f}]")

A common trap — a high R2 hides the cliff

With one cliff among twenty compounds the overall R2 still looks respectable, which is why you must inspect residuals, not just the summary score. The one compound the model misses is the most interesting molecule in the set — the opposite of an outlier to discard. Unit 12 generalises this model to continuous descriptors (Hansch QSAR) and brings the validation discipline of the AI course to bear; the habit of reading residuals as chemistry starts here.

9. Summary

You should now be able to explain, without notes:

  • The similar-property principle, why it powers similarity search and clustering, and why it is only a tendency whose exceptions matter.
  • Molecular similarity as the Tanimoto coefficient on fingerprints: the formula, a hand calculation, and the fact that "similar" is always relative to a fingerprint and its settings.
  • The Bemis-Murcko scaffold — ring systems plus linkers, side chains removed — extracted by hand and by RDKit, and its generic graph framework for topology-level grouping.
  • The maximum common substructure as the largest shared subgraph of a set, how to compute it with rdFMCS, and its use for alignment, core definition and similarity.
  • R-group decomposition: splitting a series into a constant core and labelled variable R-groups, the tabular input of quantitative SAR.
  • Matched molecular pairs: single-change pairs as controlled experiments, and summarising a transformation by its mean activity change.
  • Activity cliffs and the SALI: ranking the steep, information-rich pairs that violate the similar-property principle and localise SAR to one interaction.
  • The Free-Wilson additive model: fitting substituent contributions by linear regression, reading the coefficients chemically, and using its residuals to detect the non-additive chemistry (cliffs) it cannot represent.

The through-line: SAR analysis is disciplined comparison, and the same congeneric series supports every method, each answering a sharper version of "what did that change do?"

10. Self-assessment

1. State the similar-property principle and give one reason it can fail.Structurally similar molecules tend to have similar activity. It fails at activity cliffs: a tiny structural change can make or break a specific interaction with the target (a new hydrogen bond, a steric clash), producing a large activity change between near-identical molecules.
2. Two 6-bit fingerprints are A=110101 and B=100111. Compute their Tanimoto by hand.a = bits in A = 4; b = bits in B = 4; c = bits in both (positions 1,4,6) = 3. T = c/(a+b-c) = 3/(4+4-3) = 3/5 = 0.60. RDKit returns the same.
3. Why must you report which fingerprint produced a Tanimoto value?There is no absolute similarity: the value depends on the fingerprint type (Morgan vs path-based), radius, length and whether bits are counted. A Tanimoto of 0.4 means different things under different settings, and values from different settings are not comparable.
4. Describe the Bemis-Murcko scaffold of a molecule in three steps.(1) Find all ring systems (fused/bridged rings count as one). (2) Find all linkers — chains of atoms on a path between two ring systems. (3) The scaffold is the union of ring systems and linkers; everything else (side chains that dead-end) is removed. RDKit's default also retains exocyclic double-bonded atoms such as a carbonyl oxygen.
5. What is the Murcko scaffold of ibuprofen (one benzene ring with two aliphatic side chains), and why?Just the benzene ring (c1ccccc1). There is a single ring system and no second ring, so there are no linkers; the isobutyl and propanoic-acid branches are side chains and are removed.
6. How does the generic (graph) framework differ from the scaffold, and what does it group together?The generic framework replaces every atom with carbon and every bond with a single bond, keeping only topology. It groups scaffolds that are the same shape but differ in atom type (e.g. a benzene-cored scaffold and its pyridine analogue share a generic framework but not a scaffold).
7. How does the MCS differ from the Murcko scaffold, and name one use of the MCS.The Murcko scaffold is defined per molecule (rings + linkers); the MCS is defined across a set as the largest connected substructure common to all members, and need not be a ring system. Uses: aligning a series for consistent drawing, defining a core for R-group decomposition, or an MCS-based similarity measure.
8. What makes a pair of molecules a matched molecular pair, and why is the "matched" part important?They differ by a single defined structural change at one site, with everything else identical. The shared context is a control: the activity difference can be attributed to that one change, unlike molecules that differ in many places.
9. An MMP transformation "H to Cl here" has mean ΔpIC50 = +0.6 over 8 pairs but one pair shows +2.0. What does the outlier suggest?Non-additivity: in that one context the chloro does something special (a specific new contact), so the transformation is not transferable there. That pair is a candidate activity cliff and the most informative member of the set.
10. Write the SALI formula and explain why a steep cliff gives a large value.SALI = |A_i − A_j| / (1 − sim_ij). A steep cliff has a large activity gap (big numerator) between near-identical molecules (similarity near 1, so denominator near 0), making the ratio large. Dissimilar or equipotent pairs give small SALI.
11. The Free-Wilson model is a linear regression on what features, and what does its intercept represent?On one-hot indicator variables for each R-group at each position (reference level absorbed into the intercept). The intercept is the baseline activity of the reference compound (all positions at their reference substituent).
12. A Free-Wilson model has R2 = 0.92 but one compound has a residual of +1.3 log units. What should you do and why?Investigate that compound, do not discard it: a large residual means the additive model cannot explain it, which flags a non-additive effect (an activity cliff). It is the most interesting molecule in the set — the chemistry the model is missing — not an error.

Glossary

Activity cliff
A pair of structurally very similar molecules with very different activity; the exception to the similar-property principle, localising SAR to one structural change.
Assay
A laboratory experiment that measures how strongly a compound acts on a target.
Bemis-Murcko scaffold
A molecule's ring systems plus the linkers between them, with side chains removed; the Murcko framework.
Congeneric series
A set of molecules built on the same skeleton, differing only in small defined substituents.
Free-Wilson analysis
A model expressing activity as a baseline plus additive contributions from each substituent at each position; a linear regression on R-group indicators.
Generic (graph) framework
A scaffold with every atom set to carbon and every bond to single; the pure topology of the scaffold.
IC50 / pIC50
IC50 is the concentration giving 50% inhibition (smaller = more potent); pIC50 = −log10(IC50 in molar) (larger = more potent).
Linker
A chain of non-ring atoms lying on a path between two ring systems; kept in the scaffold.
Matched molecular pair (MMP)
Two molecules differing by a single defined structural change (the transformation) at one site.
Maximum common substructure (MCS)
The largest connected substructure present in all molecules of a set.
R-group decomposition
Splitting a series into a constant core plus the variable substituents (R-groups) at defined attachment points.
Ring system
One or more rings fused or bridged together, treated as a single unit.
SALI
Structure-activity landscape index, |ΔA|/(1−similarity); ranks activity cliffs.
Scaffold
The core skeleton defining a chemical series; here the Bemis-Murcko scaffold.
Side chain
A branch of atoms that dead-ends without reaching another ring system; removed from the scaffold.
Similar-property principle
The assumption that structurally similar molecules tend to have similar properties and activities.
Structure-activity relationship (SAR)
The relationship between changes in chemical structure and changes in biological activity.
Tanimoto coefficient
Shared bits over union of bits of two fingerprints; the standard molecular similarity measure.
Transformation
The single structural change defining a matched molecular pair (e.g. H → Cl at one position).
