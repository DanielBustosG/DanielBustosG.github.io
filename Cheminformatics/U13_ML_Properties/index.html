Unit 12 built a linear quantitative structure–activity relationship (QSAR) by hand: a few descriptors, a straight line fitted by the normal equations. This unit keeps the question — can we predict a molecule's property from its structure? — but swaps the model. We hand the structure to four of the standard supervised learners you already know from an introductory machine-learning course — k-nearest-neighbours, support-vector machines, random forests and gradient boosting — and ask them to separate active from inactive compounds and to predict potency as a number. The novelty for a computer scientist is not the algorithms; it is the input (how a molecule becomes a feature vector), the target (which molecular properties are learnable and why), and the traps (why a model that scores 0.95 can still be useless). The slides are a map; this notebook is the territory — run every cell and read its output before moving on.

Learning objectives

  • Turn a set of molecules into two kinds of feature matrix — Morgan fingerprints and physicochemical descriptors — and state what each encodes.
  • Train and compare k-nearest-neighbours, support-vector machines, random forests and gradient boosting for both classification (active vs inactive) and regression (pIC50).
  • Choose metrics that survive class imbalance, and read a confusion matrix, an ROC curve, a predicted-vs-observed plot.
  • Compare fingerprint and descriptor features on the same task and justify a featurisation choice.
  • Extract and distrust feature importances: know the impurity-bias and correlation caveats, and cross-check with permutation importance.
  • Recognise why a single random split flatters a molecular model — the hand-off to Unit 14.

1. From a line to a learner: what changes, what does not

Unit 12's linear QSAR says that a property is a weighted sum of descriptors. That is a strong assumption: it forces the response to move in a straight line as each descriptor changes, and it cannot represent “activity needs a basic nitrogen and a flat aromatic ring together” — an interaction. Real structure–activity relationships are full of such non-linearities and interactions. The machine-learning models in this unit drop the linearity assumption and let the data choose the shape of the relationship. Everything else — featurise the molecule, fit on a training set, evaluate on held-out molecules — is unchanged, and the vocabulary (overfitting, bias–variance, cross-validation) is the one you already have.[18]

Two tensions from introductory machine learning reappear here in a chemical costume. The first is the bias–variance trade-off. A linear model is high-bias: it cannot bend to a curved relationship, so it misses real structure. A deep decision tree is high-variance: it will happily carve the training molecules into perfect leaves and memorise assay noise. The models we use tame variance in two different ways — a random forest averages many high-variance trees, and a support-vector machine regularises a flexible boundary — which is why both generalise far better than a single tree. The second is the “no free lunch” reminder: no model class is best on every dataset, so the professional habit is to run a small panel of strong, different learners and compare them honestly rather than to crown a favourite in advance. That is exactly what this unit does — four learners, one dataset, the same split — and a recurring finding you should expect is that on a good featurisation several reasonable models land within a few points of one another, which tells you the features and the data matter more than the choice among them.

The decisive questions are therefore chemical, not algorithmic. Which properties can be predicted from structure at all? A property is learnable to the extent that it is determined by the molecular graph and is measured consistently. Four families dominate practical cheminformatics,[19] and this unit uses the first as its running example:

  • Potency — how strongly a molecule binds or inhibits a biological target, reported as IC50, Ki or EC50. Largely structure-determined and the backbone of QSAR,[13] though assay noise sets a ceiling on achievable accuracy.
  • Solubility and lipophilicity — how well a compound dissolves in water (logS) or partitions into oil (logP); physical, reproducible, and among the most predictable endpoints.[14]
  • Permeability and absorption — whether a molecule crosses membranes and reaches its site of action; the properties Lipinski's rule of five summarises.[24]
  • Toxicity and off-target effects — important, but noisier and often driven by mechanisms the two-dimensional graph does not capture, so expect lower ceilings.

Machine learning on molecules is now routine across drug discovery,[22] but its reputation rests on honest evaluation; this unit builds the models and Unit 14 stress-tests them.

Chemistry box — potency, IC50 and pIC50

An assay is a laboratory measurement of what a molecule does to a biological system. For inhibitors, the common readout is the IC50: the concentration of compound that reduces the target's activity by half. A smaller IC50 means a more potent compound (less is needed), so IC50 spans many orders of magnitude — from millimolar (weak) to picomolar (very strong). As with pH, we take a negative base-10 logarithm to get a well-behaved linear scale: pIC50 = −log10(IC50 in molar), equivalently 9 − log10(IC50 in nM). A pIC50 of 6 is an IC50 of 1 µM; a pIC50 of 9 is 1 nM, a thousand times more potent. We model pIC50, not IC50, for the same reason we logged everything in Unit 12: it makes the target roughly symmetric and the errors roughly uniform across the range.

# Fetch EGFR (CHEMBL203) IC50 measurements from the ChEMBL REST API in small pages, with retries; fall back on failure.
def fetch_chembl_egfr(n_target=1200, per=300, timeout=30, tries=4):
    base = ("https://www.ebi.ac.uk/chembl/api/data/activity.json?target_chembl_id=CHEMBL203"
            "&standard_type=IC50&standard_relation==&standard_units=nM&limit=%d&offset=%d")
    out, off = [], 0
    while len(out) < n_target:
        d = None
        for attempt in range(tries):                       # small pages + retries avoid truncated-response errors
            try:
                req = urllib.request.Request(base % (per, off), headers={"User-Agent": "python-urllib"})
                with urllib.request.urlopen(req, timeout=timeout) as r:
                    d = json.loads(r.read())               # read fully, then parse (robust to IncompleteRead)
                break
            except Exception:
                if attempt == tries - 1:
                    raise
                time.sleep(1.0)
        acts = d.get("activities", [])
        if not acts:
            break
        out += [(a["canonical_smiles"], float(a["standard_value"])) for a in acts
                if a.get("canonical_smiles") and a.get("standard_value") is not None]
        off += per
    return out

The printout reports the size of the curated set (about one thousand unique molecules when fetched live, 48 in the offline fallback) and the split into active and inactive at our threshold — here close to an even split, around 55 % active. Two curation choices deserve a note. First, we collapse duplicate measurements of the same molecule to their median pIC50: the same compound is often assayed many times, and the median is robust to the occasional wild outlier — exactly the Unit 7 reasoning. Second, the activity threshold of pIC50 ≥ 6 (IC50 ≤ 1 µM) is a convention, not a law of nature; it turns a continuous potency into a yes/no label so that the same dataset can drive both a classifier and a regressor. We will see that the threshold's position controls the class balance, which in turn controls which metrics are trustworthy.

A third fact governs everything downstream and is easy to forget: the labels are noisy. The IC50 of one compound measured in two different laboratories, or even on two different days, routinely disagrees by a factor of two to three — a few tenths of a pIC50 unit — because biological assays are sensitive to protein batch, temperature, readout and a dozen other variables. Public aggregations such as ChEMBL mix results from hundreds of papers, so the scatter is real and irreducible. This sets a ceiling on any model: if the measurement itself is uncertain to 0.3–0.5 pIC50 units, no model can honestly claim an RMSE much below that, and a model that appears to do so is fitting noise. Keeping the ceiling in mind is what separates a sober modeller from someone who over-reads a flattering number. We also chose a single, well-studied target deliberately: EGFR has enough measured compounds that the models have something to learn, and its inhibitors share chemistry, which — as §10 shows — makes the gap between an easy and a hard evaluation visible without being overwhelming. Before any of this, the molecules should be standardised (consistent tautomers, charges and salts stripped), the data-cleaning step Unit 4 treats in full; here we take the ChEMBL canonical SMILES and the light curation above, which is enough for a teaching dataset.

3. Featurisation: fingerprints and descriptors

A learner needs a fixed-length numeric vector, but a molecule is a variable-size graph. Featurisation is the bridge, and it is where the chemistry enters. We use the two featurisers that cover most ligand-based machine learning, both met in earlier units.

A Morgan fingerprint (Unit 5; the open re-implementation of extended-connectivity fingerprints, ECFP)[9] looks at every atom, grows a circular neighbourhood of a chosen radius around it, hashes each neighbourhood to an integer, and folds those integers into a fixed-length bit vector. Bit j is 1 if the molecule contains at least one substructure that hashes to j. For a computer scientist it is a feature hash of the molecule's substructures: sparse, high-dimensional, each bit a (possibly ambiguous) chemical fragment. A descriptor vector, by contrast, is a short list of interpretable whole-molecule numbers — molecular weight, logP, polar surface area (TPSA), hydrogen-bond donor and acceptor counts, ring counts — the Unit 12 QSAR inputs, computed with published additive schemes.[25][26] We compute both with RDKit.[10]

The fingerprint has two hyperparameters that are themselves modelling choices. The radius sets how large a substructure each bit can describe: radius 2 (the common ECFP4 setting we use) looks up to two bonds out from each atom, capturing a functional group and its immediate surroundings; a larger radius encodes bigger, more specific fragments but makes the fingerprint sparser and more prone to overfitting a particular series. The length (we use 1024 bits) sets how many distinct fragments are folded into the vector: shorter vectors collide more — two unrelated fragments forced onto the same bit — while longer ones (2048, 4096) collide less at the cost of memory and speed. Neither choice is sacred; both are worth sweeping in a real project, under the honest evaluation of Unit 14. The descriptor set, by contrast, is chosen by the modeller: we picked ten physically meaningful, cheap-to-compute properties, but RDKit exposes two hundred more, and blindly feeding all of them to a model invites the collinearity and overfitting problems Unit 12 warned about. The cell builds both featurisations from the same molecules.

Chemistry box — what a fingerprint bit means

Each Morgan bit stands for a small atom-centred fragment — for example “an aromatic carbon bonded to a nitrogen, two bonds out”. Because thousands of possible fragments are folded into 1024 bits, two different fragments can land on the same bit (a hash collision), so a bit is a mostly-specific substructure flag, not a guarantee. Keep this in mind in §9 when we ask a model which bits it relied on.

4. The four learners, in one paragraph each

You know these models; here is only what is special when the input is a molecule.

  • k-Nearest-Neighbours (k-NN).[4] Predict a molecule from its k most similar molecules. On fingerprints the natural distance is 1 − Tanimoto (Jaccard distance on bit sets), so k-NN is the direct machine-learning expression of the molecular-similarity principle of Unit 8: similar structures tend to have similar activity. It has no training phase and no capacity to extrapolate beyond its neighbours.
  • Support-Vector Machine (SVM).[3] Find the maximum-margin boundary; with a radial-basis kernel it draws smooth non-linear surfaces in feature space. SVMs are strong on high-dimensional sparse inputs like fingerprints but need their features scaled and their two hyperparameters (C, γ) tuned.
  • Random Forest (RF).[2] An average of many decorrelated decision trees, each grown on a bootstrap sample and a random feature subset. Svetnik and colleagues showed in 2003 that this one algorithm is a remarkably reliable default for QSAR — accurate, hard to overfit, and almost tuning-free.[1] It is our baseline throughout.
  • Gradient Boosting (GBM).[5] Build trees sequentially, each correcting the previous ensemble's errors. Modern histogram-based implementations — XGBoost,[6] and the HistGradientBoosting estimator we use here — are fast and often top accuracy tables; Sheridan and colleagues documented their strength on QSAR benchmarks.[7] The cost is more hyperparameters and a greater appetite for overfitting than a random forest.

We implement all four with scikit-learn.[11]

A word on hyperparameters, because it is where honest and dishonest modelling diverge. Each learner has knobs: k and the distance for k-NN; the penalty C and kernel width γ for the SVM; the number and depth of trees for the forest; the learning rate, tree count and depth for boosting. These control the bias–variance balance, and tuning them can change results. We keep them at sensible, near-default values here (for example 300 trees in the forest, scikit-learn's default C and γ) for two reasons. First, pedagogy: the point of this unit is to see four model families on the same footing, not to squeeze the last percent from one. Second, and more importantly, tuning must not touch the test set. Choosing hyperparameters by their test-set score silently leaks the test labels into the model and inflates the reported performance — one of the subtle leaks Unit 14 dissects. Proper tuning uses a nested cross-validation or a separate validation split; we defer that machinery to Unit 14 and, for now, read the near-default numbers as a fair baseline comparison rather than each model's best possible score. The random forest's reputation for working well out of the box[1] is precisely what makes it the right first model to reach for.

5. Classification: active versus inactive

The first task is a yes/no question: given a molecule, is it active against EGFR? We train all four models on the fingerprint features and score them on the held-out test molecules. Because the classes are imbalanced, raw accuracy (fraction correct) is misleading — a model that calls everything active would already score the active fraction. We therefore report three honest metrics: ROC-AUC (ranking quality: the probability that a random active is scored above a random inactive, 0.5 = chance, 1.0 = perfect), balanced accuracy (the average of the true-active and true-inactive rates, which a trivial majority vote cannot inflate), and Matthews correlation (a single correlation-like summary of the confusion matrix). SVM scores use the signed distance to the decision boundary.

Two refinements are worth naming even though we keep the headline simple. When the positive class is rare and is the one you care about — the usual situation in virtual screening, where actives are a tiny minority of a huge library — the precision–recall curve and its area (PR-AUC) are more informative than ROC, because ROC-AUC can look reassuringly high while the few predicted actives are mostly false alarms. Here the classes are only mildly imbalanced, so ROC-AUC is adequate, but remember the rule: the rarer and more precious the positive class, the more you should lean on precision–recall. The second refinement is calibration: a model's output score (say 0.8) is not automatically a probability of 80 %. Random forests and SVMs in particular produce scores that rank well but are poorly calibrated, which matters if a downstream decision multiplies the number by a cost. Ranking metrics like ROC-AUC are indifferent to calibration; if you need honest probabilities, calibrate them (Platt scaling, isotonic regression) on held-out data — another topic that belongs with Unit 14's treatment of uncertainty.

6. Regression: predicting pIC50 as a number

Thresholding potency into active/inactive discards information: a pIC50 of 5.9 and one of 2.0 are both “inactive” yet wildly different. Regression keeps the number. We train the regression twins of the same four models and report R² (fraction of variance explained, ≤ 1), RMSE (root-mean-square error, in pIC50 units — the typical miss) and Spearman ρ (rank correlation, which cares only about ordering compounds correctly, often what matters for prioritisation).

8. Worked example by hand: a k-NN prediction with Tanimoto

Of the four models, k-NN is the one you can run on paper, and doing so makes the molecular-similarity principle concrete. The algorithm: to predict a query molecule, (1) compute its Tanimoto similarity to every training molecule, (2) take the k most similar, (3) for regression, average their known values — either plainly, or weighted by similarity so closer neighbours count more.

Work the following toy case by hand. A query Q has these similarities and known pIC50 to five library molecules:

Tanimoto to Q0.620.550.480.330.29

With k = 3 the nearest three are A, B, C (similarities 0.62, 0.55, 0.48). The uniform prediction is their plain mean, (7.2 + 6.9 + 7.5) / 3 = 21.6 / 3 = 7.20. The distance-weighted prediction weights each by its similarity: (0.62·7.2 + 0.55·6.9 + 0.48·7.5) / (0.62 + 0.55 + 0.48) = (4.464 + 3.795 + 3.600) / 1.65 = 11.859 / 1.65 = 7.19. The weighting barely moves the answer here because the three neighbours have similar pIC50; it matters more when neighbours disagree. Note that D and E are ignored entirely — and E, the most potent molecule in the table, is simply too dissimilar to inform the prediction. Impurity importance (green) is computed on the training data from how much each feature reduces node impurity; it is biased toward features with many distinct values (continuous, high-cardinality descriptors such as molecular weight or TPSA) and inflates correlated features.[15] Permutation importance (purple) is measured on the held-out set by shuffling one feature and watching the test score fall; it answers the more honest question “does the model's generalisation depend on this feature?”[16] Where the two disagree — a descriptor ranked high by impurity but near zero by permutation — the high impurity rank was an artefact of cardinality or of sharing information with a correlated partner (recall the correlations in Figure 2), not evidence that the feature matters.

Feature importance is not causation

Importance scores describe this model on this dataset, not chemistry. A high score can come from a confounder (an inactive sub-series that happens to be heavy), from correlation with the true driver, or from leakage. Treat importances as a hypothesis-generator to inspect, never as a mechanistic claim — and always cross-check impurity importance against permutation importance.

Beyond the two measures shown here, modern practice adds SHAP values, which attribute a single prediction to its features with a game-theoretic guarantee of consistency, and — the chemist's favourite — matched molecular pair analysis, which isolates the effect of a single structural change by comparing molecules that differ in exactly one group. All of these answer slightly different questions, and all share the same discipline: an importance is a statement about the model, and only a careful experiment makes it a statement about chemistry. The most honest use of a fingerprint-bit importance is to turn it back into a picture a medicinal chemist can judge — which is exactly what the next cell does, mapping the forest's top bits to the substructures they stand for.

10. A first look at honest evaluation (handed off to Unit 14)

Every number above came from one random 75/25 split. That is the kindest possible test, and for molecules it is usually too kind. Drug-discovery datasets are full of analogue series — clusters of near-identical molecules. A random split scatters members of a cluster across train and test, so the model is often asked to predict a molecule that has an almost-twin in training. That measures interpolation within known chemistry, not the deployment question we actually care about: will it work on a genuinely new scaffold? A scaffold split — grouping molecules by their Bemis–Murcko core (Unit 11) and keeping whole scaffolds out of training[28] — is a harder, more realistic test, as is a time split by assay date.[8]

Closely related is the question of when to trust an individual prediction. A model trained on one region of chemical space has no business making confident predictions far outside it, yet it will return a number for any input you give it. The applicability domain is the set of molecules close enough to the training data for the model's predictions to be meaningful; flagging a query as in- or out-of-domain (by its distance to the nearest training molecules, or by the disagreement among a forest's trees) is as important in deployment as the headline accuracy. Pairing each prediction with an honest uncertainty — from an ensemble's spread, or from a conformal-prediction interval with a coverage guarantee — turns a point estimate into something a project can actually act on. These ideas, together with y-randomisation (a sanity check that shuffling the labels destroys the model's apparent skill), are the substance of Unit 14; we flag them here so that the clean numbers above are read with the right scepticism. The cell contrasts random and scaffold splits for our random forest.

11. Summary

You should now be able to explain, without notes:

  • Why we move from a linear QSAR to non-linear learners, and that the machine-learning vocabulary is unchanged — only the input (molecular featurisation) and the failure modes are new.
  • Which molecular properties are learnable (potency, solubility, permeability, with a noise ceiling; toxicity less so) and why, and what IC50, pIC50 and an assay are.
  • How to turn molecules into two feature matrices — sparse high-dimensional Morgan fingerprints and short interpretable descriptor vectors — and when each is preferable.
  • What k-NN, SVM, random forests and gradient boosting each do, and that on fingerprint features the tree ensembles and SVM are usually close while k-NN is the direct expression of molecular similarity.
  • How to evaluate a classifier under class imbalance (ROC-AUC with balanced accuracy and MCC, never plain accuracy) and a regressor (R², RMSE, Spearman), and how to read ROC curves, confusion matrices and predicted-vs-observed plots.
  • Why feature importances mislead — impurity bias toward high-cardinality and correlated features — and how permutation importance corrects the question.
  • Why a single random split flatters a molecular model, and what a scaffold split measures instead — the bridge to Unit 14.

12. Self-assessment

1. A colleague reports 92% accuracy for an active/inactive classifier and is delighted. What is the first question you ask?What fraction of the dataset is active? If 90% are active, a model that predicts “active” for everything already scores 90% accuracy while being useless. Ask for balanced accuracy, ROC-AUC and the confusion matrix; accuracy alone is meaningless under imbalance.
2. Why is pIC50 a better regression target than raw IC50?IC50 spans many orders of magnitude and is strongly right-skewed, so errors are multiplicative. Taking −log10 gives pIC50, a roughly symmetric target with roughly uniform additive errors, which linear and tree models handle far better — the same reason we log-transform in Unit 12.
3. For k-NN on Morgan fingerprints, why is 1−Tanimoto the natural distance rather than Euclidean distance?Fingerprints are sparse bit sets; Tanimoto (Jaccard) measures the overlap of present substructures and ignores the vast number of jointly-absent bits, which is exactly the chemical notion of similarity (Unit 8). Euclidean distance on 0/1 vectors is dominated by the shared zeros and is a poor similarity for sparse data.
4. Trace a k=3 uniform k-NN prediction for a query whose three nearest neighbours have pIC50 6.0, 6.4 and 7.1.The uniform prediction is the plain mean: (6.0 + 6.4 + 7.1)/3 = 19.5/3 = 6.5. Distance weighting would nudge this toward whichever neighbour is most similar, but with k=3 uniform it is simply the average.
5. The random forest's predicted-vs-observed plot shows it under-predicting the most potent compounds. Why, and what does it imply for a discovery project?Tree ensembles average training leaves and cannot extrapolate beyond the training range, so extreme targets regress toward the mean. For a project hunting the next most-potent molecule — which is by definition at the edge or beyond the current range — the model will systematically understate the best candidates; rank-based use (Spearman) is safer than trusting absolute predictions there.
6. Impurity importance ranks molecular weight top; permutation importance ranks it near zero. What happened?Impurity (Gini) importance is biased toward high-cardinality continuous features and toward features correlated with the true driver, and it is computed on the training data. Molecular weight is continuous and correlated with heavy-atom and ring counts (Figure 2), so it absorbs impurity credit without the model's generalisation actually depending on it — which permutation importance, measured on held-out data, reveals.
7. Why can a single fingerprint bit not be read as a definitive “this substructure causes activity”?Two reasons: hashing folds many possible fragments into 1024 bits, so one bit can represent more than one substructure (a collision); and even a cleanly-mapped bit may merely correlate with activity in this series rather than cause it. Bit importance is a hypothesis to inspect, not a mechanism.
8. On this EGFR task fingerprints beat the ten descriptors. Would you expect the same for predicting aqueous solubility? Why?Not necessarily. Solubility is a global physicochemical property driven by polarity, size and hydrogen bonding — exactly what descriptors like logP, TPSA and MW capture — so descriptors are often competitive or better there. Fingerprints excel at series-specific potency, where particular substructures distinguish actives. Match the featuriser to the endpoint.
9. You get ROC-AUC 0.93 on a random split and 0.78 on a scaffold split. Which should you report to someone deciding whether to deploy the model, and why?The scaffold-split 0.78, because deployment means predicting new chemistry with no close training analogue, which the scaffold split approximates. The random-split 0.93 is inflated by near-duplicate molecules shared between train and test. Report both and explain the gap (Unit 14).
10. Why does a gradient-boosting model usually need more care than a random forest on a small molecular dataset?Boosting fits trees sequentially to residuals, so it can keep driving training error down and overfit; it has more hyperparameters (learning rate, number of trees, depth) that interact. A random forest averages independent trees and is famously robust with near-default settings (Svetnik 2003), making it the safer baseline on small, noisy sets.
11. The curation step collapses repeated measurements of the same molecule to their median pIC50. Why the median and not the mean?Replicate assay values occasionally contain gross outliers (a mis-recorded unit, a bad plate). The median is robust to such outliers, whereas a single wrong value can drag the mean far off. This is the same robustness argument made in Unit 7's curation.
12. All four models score within a few points of one another on fingerprints. What does this tell you, and what should you do next?That the featurisation and dataset, not the choice among reasonable models, dominate performance — a common finding. Rather than chase tiny model-ranking differences (which may not survive a different split), invest in better data, better features, and honest evaluation (Unit 14). Report the simplest strong model (often the random forest) with uncertainty.

Glossary

Assay
A laboratory measurement of a molecule's biological effect (e.g. enzyme inhibition), the source of the activity labels we model.
IC50 / pIC50
IC50 is the concentration that halves a target's activity (smaller = more potent); pIC50 = −log10(IC50 in molar) is its log scale, the regression target.
Classification vs regression
Predicting a discrete label (active/inactive) vs a continuous number (pIC50); the same features serve both.
Morgan fingerprint (ECFP)
A fixed-length bit vector hashing each atom's circular substructure neighbourhoods; a sparse, high-dimensional feature hash of the molecule (Unit 5).
Descriptor
An interpretable whole-molecule number (MW, logP, TPSA, H-bond counts, ring counts); few, dense, and often correlated.
Tanimoto / Jaccard
Similarity of two bit sets, |A∩B| / |A∪B|; 1−Tanimoto is the distance used by k-NN on fingerprints (Unit 8).
k-Nearest-Neighbours
Predicts a molecule from its k most similar training molecules; no training phase, no extrapolation.
Support-Vector Machine
A maximum-margin classifier/regressor; with an RBF kernel it fits smooth non-linear boundaries, strong on sparse fingerprints.
Random Forest
An average of many decorrelated bootstrap decision trees; the robust, near-tuning-free QSAR default (Svetnik 2003).
Gradient Boosting
Trees fitted sequentially to the ensemble's residuals (XGBoost, HistGradientBoosting); high accuracy, more tuning, more overfitting risk.
ROC-AUC
Area under the receiver-operating-characteristic curve; the probability a random active outranks a random inactive (0.5 chance, 1 perfect); threshold-free.
Balanced accuracy
Mean of the true-positive and true-negative rates; equals 0.5 for a majority-class guesser, so it survives class imbalance.
Matthews correlation (MCC)
A correlation-like single summary of the confusion matrix, robust to imbalance.
R² / RMSE / Spearman
Regression metrics: variance explained; typical error in pIC50 units; rank correlation (ordering quality).
Impurity (Gini) importance
A tree's built-in feature-importance from impurity reduction; biased toward high-cardinality and correlated features.
Permutation importance
Drop in held-out score when one feature is shuffled; a more honest, model-agnostic importance.
Scaffold split
Train/test split that keeps whole Bemis–Murcko scaffolds out of training, approximating prediction on new chemistry (Unit 11, Unit 14).
Class imbalance
Unequal class sizes; distorts accuracy and motivates balanced metrics or resampling (e.g. SMOTE).
