Every unit so far has treated a molecule as a fixed object: we parsed it, standardised it, turned it into a vector, searched it, scored it and modelled it. This unit lets molecules change. A chemical reaction turns one set of molecules into another, and if we can express that transformation as code we can do three things that matter enormously for drug discovery: build large virtual libraries from a handful of purchasable pieces, estimate how hard a proposed molecule would be to make, and — reading a reaction backwards — ask a computer how a target might be synthesised. The slides sketch these ideas; this notebook makes each one executable, with the chemistry built as we go.

Learning objectives

  • Explain what a chemical reaction is and represent it as a graph-rewriting rule — a reaction SMARTS with atom maps.
  • Apply a reaction in RDKit with ReactionFromSmarts and RunReactants, and read the multiple, sometimes duplicated, product sets it returns.
  • Trace one amide-coupling transformation atom by atom on paper and confirm it in code.
  • Enumerate a combinatorial library from building blocks and profile it for size, properties and drug-likeness.
  • Compute the synthetic-accessibility score (SAScore), decompose it into its fragment and complexity terms, and say what each measures chemically.
  • Explain retrosynthesis and the difference between template-based and machine-learned approaches, and run one single-step disconnection as a reverse reaction.

1. What a chemical reaction is, and why compute with it

Chemistry box — a chemical reaction

A chemical reaction is a process that breaks and forms covalent bonds, turning one set of molecules (the reactants) into another (the products). Atoms are conserved — none are created or destroyed — so a balanced reaction has the same count of each element on both sides. Most of the molecule is a spectator; the change is local, confined to a few atoms around the bonds that break and form. A reaction that joins a carboxylic acid (the -C(=O)OH group of Unit 1) and an amine (the -NH2/-NH- group) into an amide (-C(=O)N-), expelling a water molecule, is called an amide coupling; it is the single most frequently used reaction in medicinal chemistry.

Because the change is local and the rest of the molecule is a spectator, a reaction is naturally expressed as a graph-rewriting rule: find a small subgraph (the reacting functional groups) and replace it with another subgraph (the new bonds), leaving everything else untouched. This is the same abstraction a computer scientist meets in term-rewriting systems, graph grammars, and compiler peephole rules. The payoff is leverage. If we own a catalogue of a few hundred acids and a few hundred amines, a single amide-coupling rule defines hundreds of thousands of amides — a combinatorial library we never have to draw by hand.[1] And because the library was built by a known reaction from purchasable pieces, every member comes with a plausible one-step recipe for making it,[11] which is more than can be said for molecules invented by unconstrained generation.[13]

The cell below draws the archetypal reaction so we have a concrete picture before the formal notation. RDKit stores a reaction as its own object and can render it with the reacting pieces on the left and the product on the right.

# A reaction as a picture: amide coupling drawn reactants -> products.
amide_demo = AllChem.ReactionFromSmarts("[C:1](=[O:2])[OX2H1].[NX3;H2:3]>>[C:1](=[O:2])[N:3]")
Draw.ReactionToImage(amide_demo, subImgSize=(230, 150))

Figure 1. The amide-coupling reaction drawn by RDKit from a reaction SMARTS. The two reactant templates (a carboxylic acid and a primary amine) sit left of the arrow, the amide product right of it. The drawing is schematic — the atoms shown are only the reacting groups, with the rest of any real molecule implied. Read on to see how the arrow is written as text and how it is applied to real building blocks.

2. Reaction SMARTS: a transformation as a pattern

In Unit 10 we met SMARTS, a query language for substructures — a pattern that matches part of a molecule. A reaction SMARTS extends this to a transformation. It has the form

$$\underbrace{\texttt{reactant}_1 \;\texttt{.}\; \texttt{reactant}_2 \;\dots}_{\text{left pattern (matched)}} \;\texttt{>>}\; \underbrace{\texttt{product}_1 \;\texttt{.}\; \texttt{product}_2 \;\dots}_{\text{right pattern (written)}}$$

The >> is the reaction arrow; reactant and product templates are separated by .. The key device is atom mapping: a colon and an integer inside an atom's brackets, as in [C:1], label an atom so that the same label on the right-hand side means "this is the same atom, carried across". Atoms that appear on the left but not the right are deleted (they leave as part of a by-product); atoms on the right but not the left are created; bonds are whatever the right-hand pattern specifies between the mapped atoms. Everything the pattern does not mention is copied unchanged from reactant to product.

Chemistry box — SMILES vs SMARTS vs reaction SMARTS

A SMILES (Unit 3) names one concrete molecule.[4] A SMARTS (Unit 10) is a query that matches a family of substructures (e.g. "any primary amine"). A reaction SMARTS is a query plus a rewrite: it matches reacting groups and specifies the atoms and bonds of the result. The atom-map numbers are the glue that says which matched atom becomes which product atom — without them RDKit cannot know where to attach the new bond.

Consider the amide coupling written carefully:

[C:1](=[O:2])[OX2H1].[N;!$(N=*);!$(N-C=O);!$([N+]);H1,H2:3]>>[C:1](=[O:2])[N:3]

The acid template [C:1](=[O:2])[OX2H1] matches a carbonyl carbon (mapped 1) doubly bonded to an oxygen (mapped 2) and singly bonded to a hydroxyl oxygen [OX2H1] that carries exactly one hydrogen — this last atom is unmapped, so it is discarded (it leaves as part of water). The amine template [N;…;H1,H2:3] matches a nitrogen (mapped 3) with one or two hydrogens, with recursive exclusions (!$(…)) that stop it matching an amide nitrogen, an imine, or a charged nitrogen — a lesson from Unit 10 that a loose query matches things you did not intend. The product [C:1](=[O:2])[N:3] keeps atoms 1, 2 and 3 and draws a new bond between the carbonyl carbon and the nitrogen. The cell below parses this rule and inspects it as an object.

# Parse the amide-coupling reaction SMARTS and inspect its structure as an object.
amide_smarts = "[C:1](=[O:2])[OX2H1].[N;!$(N=*);!$(N-C=O);!$([N+]);H1,H2:3]>>[C:1](=[O:2])[N:3]"
amide_rxn = AllChem.ReactionFromSmarts(amide_smarts)
print("reactant templates:", amide_rxn.GetNumReactantTemplates(),
      "| product templates:", amide_rxn.GetNumProductTemplates())
for i in range(amide_rxn.GetNumReactantTemplates()):
    t = amide_rxn.GetReactantTemplate(i)
    maps = [a.GetAtomMapNum() for a in t.GetAtoms() if a.GetAtomMapNum()]
    print(f"  reactant {i}: {Chem.MolToSmarts(t)}   mapped atoms -> {maps}")
prod0 = amide_rxn.GetProductTemplate(0)
print("  product 0  :", Chem.MolToSmarts(prod0),
      "  mapped atoms ->", [a.GetAtomMapNum() for a in prod0.GetAtoms() if a.GetAtomMapNum()])

reactant templates: 2 | product templates: 1
  reactant 0: [C:1](=[O:2])[O&X2&H1]   mapped atoms -> [1, 2]
  reactant 1: [N&!$(N=*)&!$(N-C=O)&!$([N&+]);H1,H2:3]   mapped atoms -> [3]
  product 0  : [C:1](=[O:2])[N:3]   mapped atoms -> [1, 2, 3]

The printout confirms the grammar: two reactant templates, one product template, with atom maps 1 and 2 on the acid and 3 on the amine, all three reappearing in the product. The hydroxyl oxygen of the acid has no map number, which is why it is not in the product list — it is the atom that leaves. This bookkeeping is the whole content of a reaction rule; the next cell draws the templates so the text and the picture line up.

# Draw the reaction template itself (query atoms), so the mapped atoms are visible.
d = Draw.MolDraw2DCairo(760, 220)
d.DrawReaction(amide_rxn)
d.FinishDrawing()
import io
from PIL import Image
Image.open(io.BytesIO(d.GetDrawingText()))

Figure 2. The amide-coupling template drawn from the parsed reaction SMARTS. Atoms are query atoms (hence the generic appearance), and the carbonyl carbon, carbonyl oxygen and nitrogen are the mapped atoms carried from left to right. Compare with Figure 1: that was a tidy illustrative drawing; this is the actual rule the engine will apply, exclusions and all.

3. Applying a reaction: RunReactants

To fire a reaction we pass RDKit a tuple of reactant molecules, in the order of the templates, and it returns the products. The method is RunReactants, and its return type is the first thing to understand: a tuple of product sets. Each product set is itself a tuple, because a reaction can have several product templates (ours has one). RDKit returns one product set for every way the templates match the reactants — and functional groups often match in more than one way, so the same chemical product can appear several times. The products also come back un-sanitised (Unit 4): aromaticity and valence have not yet been re-perceived, so we call Chem.SanitizeMol before using them. The cell couples acetic acid with ethylamine.

# Fire the amide coupling on acetic acid + ethylamine; sanitise and read the product.
acid  = Chem.MolFromSmiles("CC(=O)O")      # acetic acid
amine = Chem.MolFromSmiles("CCN")          # ethylamine
product_sets = amide_rxn.RunReactants((acid, amine))
print("number of product sets returned:", len(product_sets))
prod = product_sets[0][0]                  # first set, first (only) product template
Chem.SanitizeMol(prod)
print("product SMILES:", Chem.MolToSmiles(prod), " (N-ethylacetamide)")
Draw.MolsToGridImage([acid, amine, prod], molsPerRow=3, subImgSize=(200, 150),
                     legends=["acetic acid", "ethylamine", "product: N-ethylacetamide"])

number of product sets returned: 1
product SMILES: CCNC(C)=O  (N-ethylacetamide)

Figure 3. The amide coupling applied to acetic acid and ethylamine. A single product set is returned, and the product is N-ethylacetamide — the acid's hydroxyl and the amine's hydrogen are gone, replaced by a C–N bond. The by-product water is implicit: the rule simply did not carry the leaving atoms into the product. This is the executable counterpart of Figure 1.

A reaction can return more than one product set, and a beginner is easily confused by it. There are two causes. First, a functional group may genuinely occur more than once (a diacid has two acid groups, so two distinct products). Second, a single group can be matched in two symmetry-equivalent ways, producing the same molecule twice. The standard defence is to canonicalise each product to a SMILES and deduplicate — exactly the InChIKey/canonical-SMILES deduplication idea of Unit 3–4. The cell demonstrates both effects with a symmetric diamine.

# Multiple matches: a symmetric diamine gives duplicate product sets; dedupe by canonical SMILES.
diamine = Chem.MolFromSmiles("NCCCCN")     # 1,4-diaminobutane (two equivalent -NH2 groups)
sets = amide_rxn.RunReactants((Chem.MolFromSmiles("CC(=O)O"), diamine))
raw = []
for ps in sets:
    m = ps[0]; Chem.SanitizeMol(m); raw.append(Chem.MolToSmiles(m))
unique = sorted(set(raw))
print("raw product sets :", len(raw))
print("unique products  :", len(unique))
for s in unique:
    print("   ", s)

raw product sets : 2
unique products  : 1
    CC(=O)NCCCCN

The engine returned two raw product sets but only one unique molecule: both of the diamine's equivalent nitrogens matched, giving the identical mono-amide twice. The lesson is a habit to carry into every enumeration: canonicalise and deduplicate the products, because the raw count from RunReactants double-counts symmetry and mixes genuine regiochemical products with artefacts. (Note that only one nitrogen reacted — RDKit applies the rule once per match; coupling the second nitrogen as well would require running the reaction again on the product, which is how multi-step sequences are modelled.)

4. Worked example by hand: one amide coupling, traced atom by atom

Reaction rules are easy to misread, so we trace one entirely on paper and then confirm it. Take benzoic acid (SMILES OC(=O)c1ccccc1) and methylamine (CN). We apply the amide rule and predict the product and the atom bookkeeping by hand first.

The acid template [C:1](=[O:2])[OX2H1] matches benzoic acid's carboxyl group: the carbonyl carbon becomes atom 1, its double-bonded oxygen atom 2, and the hydroxyl oxygen (with its hydrogen) is the unmapped atom that will leave. The amine template matches methylamine's nitrogen as atom 3 (it has two hydrogens, is not an amide/imine/cation — so it passes the exclusions). The product template [C:1](=[O:2])[N:3] keeps atoms 1, 2, 3 and forges a bond between 1 and 3. By hand:

$$\underbrace{\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{-C(=O)-OH}}_{\text{benzoic acid, }C_7H_6O_2} \;+\; \underbrace{\text{H}_2\text{N-CH}_3}_{\text{methylamine, }CH_5N} \;\longrightarrow\; \underbrace{\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{-C(=O)-NH-CH}_3}_{\text{N-methylbenzamide}} \;+\; \text{H}_2\text{O}$$

The mapped atoms carry across; the C–OH bond breaks, a C–N bond forms, and the atoms removed (one O, two H in total across the two reactants) balance exactly as one water molecule. The table makes the mapping explicit.

atom mapin reactantrole in product
1carboxyl carbon of benzoic acidamide carbonyl carbon
2carbonyl oxygen (C=O)amide carbonyl oxygen
3nitrogen of methylamineamide nitrogen (now N–H)
— (unmapped)hydroxyl O + its H; one amine Hleave as H₂O

The product should therefore be N-methylbenzamide, canonical SMILES CNC(=O)c1ccccc1, formula C₈H₉NO. The cell confirms the structure and checks the atom balance.

# Confirm the hand trace: benzoic acid + methylamine -> N-methylbenzamide, with an atom-balance check.
ba, ma = Chem.MolFromSmiles("OC(=O)c1ccccc1"), Chem.MolFromSmiles("CN")
prod = amide_rxn.RunReactants((ba, ma))[0][0]; Chem.SanitizeMol(prod)
got, expected = Chem.MolToSmiles(prod), Chem.MolToSmiles(Chem.MolFromSmiles("CNC(=O)c1ccccc1"))
print("predicted by hand : N-methylbenzamide  CNC(=O)c1ccccc1")
print("produced by RDKit :", got, " | matches hand trace:", got == expected)
def formula_with_H(m):
    return rdMolDescriptors.CalcMolFormula(m)
print("formulae:", formula_with_H(ba), "+", formula_with_H(ma), "->",
      formula_with_H(prod), "+", formula_with_H(Chem.MolFromSmiles("O")))
Draw.MolsToGridImage([ba, ma, prod, Chem.MolFromSmiles("O")], molsPerRow=4, subImgSize=(190, 150),
                     legends=["benzoic acid", "methylamine", "N-methylbenzamide", "water (leaves)"])

predicted by hand : N-methylbenzamide  CNC(=O)c1ccccc1
produced by RDKit : CNC(=O)c1ccccc1  | matches hand trace: True
formulae: C7H6O2 + CH5N -> C8H9NO + H2O

Figure 4. The hand-traced amide coupling, confirmed. RDKit produces exactly the molecule predicted on paper (CNC(=O)c1ccccc1), and the formulae balance: C₇H₆O₂ + CH₅N → C₈H₉NO + H₂O (seven + one carbons give eight; the oxygens and hydrogens balance once the water is counted). Tracing a rule by hand once, then confirming it, is the only reliable way to be sure a reaction SMARTS does what you think — a loose pattern can silently transform the wrong group.

5. A small catalogue of named reactions

Medicinal chemists rely on a modest set of robust, high-yielding reactions, because a molecule is only useful if it can actually be made. Hartenfeller and colleagues codified 58 such reactions as reaction SMARTS precisely so that in-silico design would stay inside makeable chemistry.[1] We encode five of them below. Each gets a one-line chemistry note.

Chemistry box — five workhorse reactions

  • Amide coupling: acid + amine → amide (seen above); the backbone of peptide and most lead chemistry.
  • Reductive amination: aldehyde/ketone + amine → a new C–N single bond (an alkylated amine); a gentle way to attach an amine.
  • Ester formation: acid + alcohol → ester; common in prodrugs.
  • Sulfonamide formation: sulfonyl chloride + amine → sulfonamide; a very common polar linker.
  • Suzuki coupling: aryl halide + aryl boronic acid → biaryl (a new C–C bond between two rings); the dominant ring-joining reaction in modern medicinal chemistry.

The cell defines each as a reaction SMARTS, applies it to one pair of building blocks, and draws the product next to its reactants.

# A catalogue of five named reactions, each applied to one example pair of building blocks.
catalogue = [
 ("amide coupling",        "[C:1](=[O:2])[OX2H1].[N;!$(N=*);!$(N-C=O);!$([N+]);H1,H2:3]>>[C:1](=[O:2])[N:3]",
                           ("CC(=O)O", "NCc1ccccc1")),
 ("reductive amination",   "[C:1]=[O:2].[N;!$(N=*);!$(N-C=O);H1,H2:3]>>[C:1][N:3]",
                           ("O=Cc1ccccc1", "CCN")),
 ("ester formation",       "[C:1](=[O:2])[OX2H1].[O;H1;$(O[CX4]):3]>>[C:1](=[O:2])[O:3]",
                           ("CC(=O)O", "OCC")),
 ("sulfonamide formation", "[S:1](=[O:2])(=[O:4])[Cl].[N;!$(N=*);H1,H2:3]>>[S:1](=[O:2])(=[O:4])[N:3]",
                           ("CS(=O)(=O)Cl", "Nc1ccccc1")),
 ("Suzuki coupling",       "[c:1][Br].[c:2][B](O)O>>[c:1][c:2]",
                           ("Brc1ccccc1", "OB(O)c1ccncc1")),
]
panels, legends = [], []
for name, smarts, (sa, sb) in catalogue:
    rx = AllChem.ReactionFromSmarts(smarts)
    p = rx.RunReactants((Chem.MolFromSmiles(sa), Chem.MolFromSmiles(sb)))[0][0]
    Chem.SanitizeMol(p)
    panels += [Chem.MolFromSmiles(sa), Chem.MolFromSmiles(sb), p]
    legends += [f"{name}: {sa}", sb, "-> " + Chem.MolToSmiles(p)]
Draw.MolsToGridImage(panels, molsPerRow=3, subImgSize=(210, 140), legends=legends)

Figure 5. Five workhorse reactions, each fired on one example pair (reactant, reactant, product per row). Reading top to bottom: an amide from acetic acid and benzylamine; a secondary amine from benzaldehyde and ethylamine by reductive amination; ethyl acetate from acetic acid and ethanol; a sulfonamide from methanesulfonyl chloride and aniline; and biphenyl-type product (here a pyridyl-phenyl) from a Suzuki coupling. Each rule is a few dozen characters, yet each is a general transformation that will fire on any building blocks bearing the right groups. That generality is what makes library enumeration possible.

6. Combinatorial library enumeration

A combinatorial library is the set of all products obtained by applying a reaction to every combination of compatible building blocks. With $m$ acids and $n$ amines, the amide coupling defines up to $m \times n$ amides. This is the in-silico version of how real screening libraries are built, and it is the engine behind reaction-driven de novo design, where a program proposes molecules by combining catalogue building blocks through known reactions, guaranteeing a synthetic route by construction.[11][12]

Chemistry box — building block

A building block (or reagent) is a small, purchasable molecule carrying one reactive functional group — an acid, an amine, a halide. Vendors sell catalogues of tens of thousands of them. A library enumeration picks compatible building blocks and couples them; the attraction is that every product is, in principle, one reaction away from commercial starting materials.

The cell defines six real carboxylic acids and six real amines and enumerates every amide. We deduplicate as Section 3 taught, and we keep a record of which pair produced each product so the "recipe" is never lost.

# Enumerate an amide library from 6 acids x 6 amines; keep the recipe (which pair made each product).
acids = {"acetic": "CC(=O)O", "benzoic": "OC(=O)c1ccccc1", "nicotinic": "OC(=O)c1cccnc1",
         "phenylacetic": "OC(=O)Cc1ccccc1", "4-fluorobenzoic": "OC(=O)c1ccc(F)cc1",
         "cyclopropanecarboxylic": "OC(=O)C1CC1"}
amines = {"methylamine": "CN", "benzylamine": "NCc1ccccc1", "cyclohexylamine": "NC1CCCCC1",
          "aniline": "Nc1ccccc1", "piperidine": "C1CCNCC1", "morpholine": "C1COCCN1"}
records = {}
for an, asmi in acids.items():
    amol = Chem.MolFromSmiles(asmi)
    for bn, bsmi in amines.items():
        sets = amide_rxn.RunReactants((amol, Chem.MolFromSmiles(bsmi)))
        if not sets:
            continue
        p = sets[0][0]; Chem.SanitizeMol(p)
        records.setdefault(Chem.MolToSmiles(p), (an, bn))     # canonical SMILES -> (acid, amine)
lib = pd.DataFrame([(s, a, b) for s, (a, b) in records.items()], columns=["smiles", "acid", "amine"])
print(f"{len(acids)} acids x {len(amines)} amines = {len(acids)*len(amines)} pairs -> {len(lib)} unique amide products")
print(lib.head(4).to_string(index=False))

6 acids x 6 amines = 36 pairs -> 36 unique amide products
          smiles   acid           amine
        CNC(C)=O acetic     methylamine
CC(=O)NCc1ccccc1 acetic     benzylamine
 CC(=O)NC1CCCCC1 acetic cyclohexylamine
 CC(=O)Nc1ccccc1 acetic         aniline

All 36 pairs gave distinct amides, so the library has 36 unique members, each tagged with the acid and amine that make it. Keeping that tag — the (acid, amine) pair — is not bookkeeping for its own sake: it is the synthesis plan. Every row of the table reads "take this acid, take this amine, run an amide coupling", which is a one-step recipe from commercial reagents. This is the quiet advantage of forward enumeration over free-form molecule generation, and we return to it in Section 11.

Six acids and six amines is a toy; a real enumeration might take a few thousand of each, and the product count grows as the product of the catalogue sizes — a few thousand squared is already tens of millions. Enumeration is also multiplicative across reaction steps: if the amide products were then subjected to a second reaction with a third set of building blocks, the count would multiply again. This is why combinatorial libraries can reach astronomical sizes from modest catalogues, and why the practical skill is not generating them but filtering them down to a testable subset before anything expensive — a descriptor calculation, a docking run in the companion course, or an actual synthesis — is ever attempted. The size of synthetically reasonable chemical space is estimated in the billions and up,[30][31] which is exactly why we cannot draw or test them all and need computational filters. The cell draws a sample of the library.

# Draw a 12-member sample of the enumerated amide library, labelled by the building blocks used.
sample = lib.head(12)
mols = [Chem.MolFromSmiles(s) for s in sample["smiles"]]
legs = [f"{a[:8]}+{b[:8]}" for a, b in zip(sample["acid"], sample["amine"])]
Draw.MolsToGridImage(mols, molsPerRow=4, subImgSize=(200, 150), legends=legs)

Figure 6. Twelve members of the 36-compound amide library, each labelled by the acid and amine that produced it. All share the amide linkage (C(=O)N) installed by the reaction; they differ in the two halves bolted on. This visual uniformity is the signature of a combinatorial library — one reaction, many decorations — and it is both the strength (every member is makeable the same way) and the weakness (libraries are often less diverse than their size suggests) of the approach.

7. Profiling the library: properties and drug-likeness

A library is only the start; we immediately profile it. In Unit 1 and Unit 9 we met the physicochemical descriptors — molecular weight, logP (lipophilicity), hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors, topological polar surface area — and Lipinski's "rule of five", a rule of thumb that flags molecules unlikely to be absorbed orally (MW ≤ 500, logP ≤ 5, HBD ≤ 5, HBA ≤ 10).[7] The cell computes molecular weight and logP across the whole library and plots their joint distribution.

# Property distribution of the library: molecular weight and logP.
mols = [Chem.MolFromSmiles(s) for s in lib["smiles"]]
lib["MW"]   = [Descriptors.MolWt(m) for m in mols]
lib["logP"] = [Crippen.MolLogP(m) for m in mols]
fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(8.6, 3.0))
ax1.hist(lib["MW"], bins=12, color="#2b5d9b", edgecolor="white")

8. Synthetic accessibility: the SAScore

A generator or enumeration can propose molecules that look fine on paper but are nightmares to synthesise — strained rings, dense stereochemistry, exotic fragments. The field needs a cheap number that estimates how hard a molecule is to make, computable from the 2-D structure alone, long before any route is planned. The standard such number is the synthetic accessibility score (SAScore) of Ertl and Schuffenhauer,[2] which RDKit ships as a contributed module.

Chemistry box — synthetic accessibility

Synthetic accessibility is an estimate of how easy or hard a molecule is to make in the laboratory, on a scale from 1 (very easy) to 10 (very hard). It is not a route and it is not a yield; it is a fast heuristic. The SAScore combines two intuitions: a molecule built from common fragments (ones seen often in real compounds) is easier to make, and a molecule with complex features (many stereocentres, fused/bridged/spiro ring systems, macrocycles, large size) is harder.

The score is a sum of two terms, which is the whole idea:

$$\text{SAScore} \;\approx\; \underbrace{-\,\text{fragmentScore}}_{\text{rarity of its pieces}} \;+\; \underbrace{\text{complexityPenalty}}_{\text{rings, stereo, size}}$$ The fragment score is the average "commonness" of the molecule's circular (ECFP-like, Unit 5[6]) fragments, learned from how often each fragment appears in PubChem; the complexity penalty adds up structural awkwardness. The sum is then rescaled to 1–10.

The cell scores a deliberately wide spread — from trivial (ethanol, aspirin) to a complex natural product (paclitaxel/Taxol) — so the scale becomes concrete.

9. Worked example by hand: decomposing the SAScore

To understand the score rather than merely call it, we reproduce it from its published pieces[2] and confirm against RDKit's implementation. The fragment score averages the log-frequency of the molecule's radius-2 Morgan fragments (Unit 5) over a PubChem reference; common fragments contribute positively. The complexity penalty is a sum of closed-form terms:

$$\text{penalty} = \text{size} + \text{stereo} + \text{spiro} + \text{bridge} + \text{macrocycle}$$ $$\text{size}=n_{\text{atoms}}^{1.005}-n_{\text{atoms}},\quad \text{stereo}=\log_{10}(n_{\text{chiral}}+1),\quad \text{spiro}=\log_{10}(n_{\text{spiro}}+1)$$ $$\text{bridge}=\log_{10}(n_{\text{bridgehead}}+1),\quad \text{macrocycle}=\log_{10}(2)\ \text{if any ring}>8\ \text{atoms}$$

A small symmetry correction rewards highly symmetric molecules (few distinct fragments relative to size). The raw score fragmentScore − penalty + symmetry is then rescaled to the 1–10 range. The cell computes every term explicitly for aspirin (simple) and paclitaxel (complex) and checks the total against sascorer.

# Reproduce the SAScore from its two components and confirm against RDKit's sascorer.
mfpgen = rdFingerprintGenerator.GetMorganGenerator(radius=2)
def decompose_sascore(m):
    nze = mfpgen.GetSparseCountFingerprint(m).GetNonzeroElements()
    nf = sum(nze.values())
    frag = sum(sascorer._fscores.get(i, -4) * c for i, c in nze.items()) / nf   # fragment score
    nA = m.GetNumAtoms()
    size   = nA**1.005 - nA
    stereo = math.log10(len(Chem.FindMolChiralCenters(m, includeUnassigned=True)) + 1)
    spiro  = math.log10(rdMolDescriptors.CalcNumSpiroAtoms(m) + 1)
    bridge = math.log10(rdMolDescriptors.CalcNumBridgeheadAtoms(m) + 1)
    macro  = math.log10(2) if any(len(r) > 8 for r in m.GetRingInfo().AtomRings()) else 0.0
    penalty = size + stereo + spiro + bridge + macro
    sym = math.log(nA / len(nze)) * 0.5 if nA > len(nze) else 0.0
    raw = frag - penalty + sym
    sa = 11.0 - (raw + 4.0 + 1) / 6.5 * 9.0
    if sa > 8.0: sa = 8.0 + math.log(sa - 8.0)
    return frag, penalty, max(1.0, min(10.0, sa))
for name in ["aspirin", "paclitaxel"]:
    m = Chem.MolFromSmiles(spectrum[name])
    frag, pen, sa = decompose_sascore(m)
    print(f"{name:11s} fragmentScore={frag:+.3f}  complexityPenalty={pen:.3f}  "
          f"-> SA(ours)={sa:.3f}  SA(rdkit)={sascorer.calculateScore(m):.3f}")

aspirin     fragmentScore=+1.971  complexityPenalty=0.168  -> SA(ours)=1.580  SA(rdkit)=1.580
paclitaxel  fragmentScore=+1.521  complexityPenalty=2.849  -> SA(ours)=5.916  SA(rdkit)=5.916

Our from-scratch score matches RDKit's to three decimals for both molecules, so the decomposition is faithful. That is the central insight of the SAScore and the reason it works: for drug-like molecules, synthetic difficulty is dominated by structural complexity, not by fragment exoticism. 11. Reaction-driven design versus unconstrained generation

Why insist on reactions and synthetic-accessibility scoring at all? Because the alternative — letting an algorithm invent molecules with no regard to how they are made — produces impressive-looking structures that no chemist can synthesise. This is a documented failure mode of generative models: unless synthesisability is built into the objective, a large fraction of proposed molecules are effectively un-makeable.[13][25] Reaction-driven enumeration sidesteps the problem by only ever proposing products of known reactions on real building blocks.[11] The cell contrasts the SAScore distribution of our reaction-built library with that of a set of complex natural products, to make the gap concrete.

12. Retrosynthesis: thinking backwards

Enumeration runs reactions forwards: building blocks in, products out. The harder and more valuable problem is the reverse. Given a target molecule somebody wants, retrosynthesis asks: from what simpler precursors, and by what reactions, could it be made? A chemist solves it by mentally disconnecting bonds — identifying a bond that a known reaction could have formed, splitting the molecule there into precursors, and recursing on each precursor until everything is a purchasable building block. The idea was formalised by Corey, whose program OCSS/LHASA launched computer-assisted synthesis planning in 1969.[18]

Chemistry box — a disconnection

A disconnection is the retrosynthetic opposite of a reaction: you look at a bond in the target, recognise it as one a known reaction forms, and (on paper) cut it to reveal the two precursors that reaction would have used. An amide C(=O)N bond "disconnects" to a carboxylic acid plus an amine — the reverse of Section 3. Chemists draw disconnections with a special open arrow (⇒). A full synthesis plan is a tree of disconnections whose leaves are all buyable.

What makes full retrosynthesis hard is not the single disconnection — it is the search. Each disconnection of a target may be done by many templates, each yielding different precursors; each precursor can itself be disconnected many ways; and the recursion continues until every leaf is a purchasable compound. The branching factor is large and the tree is deep, so the number of candidate routes explodes exactly as the combinatorial library did in Section 6, but now in the reverse direction and without a fixed set of building blocks to bound it. A planner must therefore do two things a chemist does implicitly: decide which disconnection to try first (a policy, usually a learned ranking over templates), and decide when a branch is hopeless and should be abandoned (a value estimate, often leaning on a synthesisability score). This is why modern retrosynthesis looks less like chemistry and more like game-playing AI — the same Monte-Carlo tree search that plays Go was adapted to plan syntheses.[14]

We can nonetheless demonstrate the atomic operation of all of this — a single-step, single-template retrosynthesis — directly: write the amide coupling backwards as a reaction SMARTS and apply it to a target amide to recover its acid and amine precursors. The cell does this for N-benzylbenzamide. Everything a real planner adds is scaffolding around this one move: more templates, a policy to choose among them, and a search over the tree they generate.

# One-step retrosynthesis as a reverse reaction SMARTS: disconnect an amide into acid + amine.
retro_amide = AllChem.ReactionFromSmarts("[C:1](=[O:2])[NX3;!$(N=*);!$([N+]):3]>>[C:1](=[O:2])[OH].[N:3]")
target = Chem.MolFromSmiles("O=C(NCc1ccccc1)c1ccccc1")   # N-benzylbenzamide
precursor_sets = retro_amide.RunReactants((target,))
print("target:", Chem.MolToSmiles(target))
print("retro gives", len(precursor_sets), "precursor set(s):")
seen = set()
for ps in precursor_sets:
    smis = []
    for p in ps:
        Chem.SanitizeMol(p); smis.append(Chem.MolToSmiles(p))
    key = tuple(sorted(smis))
    if key not in seen:
        seen.add(key); print("   ", " + ".join(smis))

12.1 Template-based versus machine-learned retrosynthesis

The disconnection above is a template: a hand- or machine-extracted reaction rule applied in reverse. The two dominant approaches to automated retrosynthesis differ in where the templates come from and how they are scored.

  • Template-based. Reaction templates are extracted automatically from large reaction databases (millions of reactions mined from patents and the literature[21][28]), each capturing a local transformation with its atom mapping — the mapping itself can now be learned without supervision.[23] The coverage and quality of these datasets strongly shape how well a planner generalises, and most public benchmarks rest on the same patent-derived corpus, which skews toward the reactions pharmaceutical chemists favour.[27] A planner applies matching templates in reverse and searches the resulting tree, using a learned model to rank which disconnection to try. Coley's similarity-based method retrieves precedents for templates;[3] Segler and colleagues combined template application with Monte-Carlo tree search and deep neural-network policies;[14] the open-source AiZynthFinder is a widely used implementation,[16] and the commercial Chematica/Synthia encodes expert rules at scale.[19] Templates are interpretable and guarantee chemically reasonable steps, but they cannot propose a transformation never seen in the database.

  • Template-free (machine-learned). The transformation is framed as a sequence-to-sequence translation: the product SMILES is the input "sentence" and the reactant SMILES the output, learned by the same transformer architectures used in natural-language translation.[15] Earlier work learned to predict reaction outcomes from examples;[22] these models can in principle generalise beyond catalogued templates but may hallucinate invalid chemistry and are harder to interpret. Reaction fingerprints[20] and learned complexity scores such as SCScore (trained so that products score higher than their reactants)[17] support both families.

Both are search problems, and both ultimately rest on a synthesisability estimate to prune hopeless branches — bringing us back to the SAScore. In practice, the field is converging on hybrids: a template-based or hybrid machine-intelligence loop that proposes, scores and selects the next molecule or reaction to try.[26] These methods, together with the de-novo generators of Unit 18[24] and the GPCR/ADMET targets of the companion units, are the forward edge of computational molecular design — but every one of them runs on the two primitives this unit built: a reaction as a graph-rewriting rule, and a fast estimate of how hard the result would be to make. The functional-group reasoning of Unit 1[10], the scaffolds of Unit 11[9] and the drug-likeness of Unit 9[8] all feed the scoring; structure-based route evaluation and docking remain in the bioinformatics course, out of scope here.[29] RDKit[5] and its reaction engine[12] provide the executable foundation for all of it.

In the reaction SMARTS [C:1](=[O:2])[OX2H1].[N:3]>>[C:1](=[O:2])[N:3], what happens to the hydroxyl oxygen of the acid, and why?It is matched on the left ([OX2H1]) but has no atom-map number, and it does not appear on the right. A SAScore above roughly 6 is a warning that a proposed molecule may be impractical to synthesise and should be inspected or down-weighted.
8. Why does a reaction-driven enumeration almost guarantee that its products are synthesisable, while an unconstrained generative model does not?Because an enumerated product is, by construction, the result of a known reaction on real (purchasable) building blocks — the route is the enumeration. An unconstrained generator optimises some property score and can produce structures with no plausible route unless synthesisability is explicitly part of its objective.
9. What is a disconnection, and how does it relate to a forward reaction?A disconnection is a retrosynthetic cut: you identify a bond that a known reaction could have formed and split the target there into the precursors that reaction would use. It is the exact reverse of running that reaction forward — an amide bond disconnects to an acid plus an amine.
10. Contrast template-based and template-free (machine-learned) retrosynthesis in one sentence each.Template-based planners apply reaction rules (extracted from reaction databases) in reverse and search the tree of disconnections, giving interpretable, chemically safe steps but no novelty beyond the database. Template-free planners treat retrosynthesis as sequence-to-sequence translation of product SMILES to reactant SMILES, potentially generalising further but risking invalid chemistry and being harder to interpret.
11. Why is atom mapping (the :1, :2, :3 labels) essential rather than cosmetic?Without maps, RDKit cannot know which matched reactant atom corresponds to which product atom, so it cannot place the new bond or decide which atoms survive. The map numbers define the correspondence; removing them would make the transformation ambiguous or invalid.
12. The amide library in this notebook all passed Lipinski's rule of five and all scored low on SAScore. Does that mean every member is a good drug candidate?No. Both are necessary-not-sufficient filters: Ro5 estimates oral absorption and SAScore estimates ease of synthesis, but neither says anything about potency against a target, selectivity, metabolic stability or toxicity. Passing them only means the molecules are not obviously disqualified on those two axes — the modelling of Units 12–14 and the ADMET of Unit 9 address the rest.

Glossary

Amide coupling
The reaction joining a carboxylic acid and an amine into an amide (C(=O)N), expelling water; the most common reaction in medicinal chemistry.
Atom mapping
The :n labels inside atom brackets in a reaction SMARTS that identify which reactant atom becomes which product atom.
Building block (reagent)
A small purchasable molecule bearing one reactive group (acid, amine, halide…) used as input to a reaction or enumeration.
Combinatorial library
The set of all products from applying a reaction to every combination of compatible building blocks; size grows as the product of catalogue sizes.
Disconnection
A retrosynthetic cut of a target bond into the precursors a known reaction would use; the reverse of a forward reaction.
Product set
One tuple of product molecules returned by RunReactants for a single way the templates match; a reaction can return many.
Reaction (chemical)
A process that breaks and forms bonds, converting reactants into products while conserving atoms.
Reaction SMARTS
A SMARTS-based rule reactants >> products with atom maps, expressing a transformation as graph rewriting.
Reaction template
A reaction rule (hand- or machine-extracted) capturing a local transformation; applied in reverse for retrosynthesis.
Retrosynthesis
Planning a synthesis backwards: recursively disconnecting a target into simpler precursors until all are purchasable.
SAScore
Synthetic-accessibility score (Ertl & Schuffenhauer): a 1 (easy) to 10 (hard) estimate combining fragment commonness and structural complexity.
Sanitisation
RDKit's re-perception of aromaticity, valence and implicit hydrogens; required on reaction products before use.
Template-free retrosynthesis
Machine-learned planning that translates product SMILES to reactant SMILES without explicit rules.
