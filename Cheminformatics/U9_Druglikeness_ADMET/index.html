A molecule that binds its target tightly is not yet a drug. It must also survive the journey from a swallowed tablet to the site of action, linger long enough to act, do no harm, and leave again — a cluster of properties abbreviated ADMET. Most candidate molecules fail here, not for lack of potency, and they fail late and expensively. This unit teaches what ADMET means in plain biological terms, then shows how decades of medicinal-chemistry experience were distilled into fast, rule-based filters you can compute on a laptop: Lipinski's rule of five, Veber, Ghose, Egan, Muegge, the rule of three, lead-likeness, the continuous QED score, and the PAINS and Brenk structural alerts. Equally, it shows where every one of these rules misleads — because a rule applied without the chemistry it encodes produces confident, wrong answers.

1. What ADMET is, and why potency is not enough

A compound that blocks its target protein beautifully in a test tube is a long way from being a medicine. Imagine a drug swallowed as a tablet: it must dissolve in the gut, cross the gut wall into the blood, spread to the tissue where its target lives, avoid being chewed up or thrown out too quickly, act, and finally be cleared without poisoning anything on the way. Those stages have names, and together they spell ADMET.

  • Absorption — getting from the gut into the bloodstream. An oral drug must dissolve in water and slip through the fatty wall of the intestinal cells. This is the central tension of Unit 1: too water-loving and it will not cross the membrane; too oil-loving and it will not dissolve. Most of the rules in this unit are really absorption rules.
  • Distribution — where the drug goes once in the blood. It must reach its target tissue; a brain drug additionally has to cross the blood–brain barrier, a much tighter membrane. Binding to blood proteins, and partitioning into fat, all happen here.
  • Metabolism — the body's chemical demolition crew, chiefly liver enzymes (the cytochrome P450 family), modifies the drug, usually to make it more water-soluble and easier to excrete. Metabolism can inactivate a drug, or occasionally turn a harmless molecule into a toxic one.
  • Excretion — removal, mainly by the kidneys (into urine) or the liver (into bile). Too fast and the drug never reaches a useful concentration; too slow and it accumulates.
  • Toxicity — the catch-all for harm: hitting the wrong protein, damaging the liver, interfering with the heart's electrical rhythm, or breaking DNA. Toxicity ends more programmes than any single other cause.

Chemistry box — assay, potency, IC50

An assay is a laboratory measurement of a biological effect — for example, how strongly a compound switches off an enzyme. Potency is how little compound you need to get that effect; a common measure is the IC50, the concentration that halves the target's activity (lower is more potent). Potency answers "does it work on the target?". ADMET answers "will it ever reach the target in a living body, and leave without harm?". A molecule can be superb at the first and hopeless at the second.

Why does this belong in a cheminformatics course? Because all of ADMET is, to a first approximation, controlled by a handful of molecular properties you can compute from the structure alone — size, greasiness, polarity, flexibility, reactivity. Decades of medicinal chemistry were compressed into simple numeric rules over those properties, and whole screening libraries of millions of molecules are filtered by them before a single experiment is run.[17] Modern web tools such as SwissADME[25] package dozens of these predictions behind one button, and graphical models such as the BOILED-Egg estimate gut and brain penetration directly from two computed properties.[26] This unit builds the rules behind such tools from the ground up in RDKit[30] — and, just as importantly, teaches you to distrust them in the right places. The whole topic is a chapter of modern chemoinformatics.[29]

2. The physicochemical properties behind the rules

Every drug-likeness rule in this unit is a set of thresholds on a few numbers computed from the molecular graph. Five of them you already met in Unit 1; the others are close cousins. Defining them precisely now means the rules later are one-liners.

Chemistry box — the six workhorse properties

  • Molecular weight (MW) — the mass of one molecule in g/mol; a proxy for size. Bigger molecules diffuse and cross membranes less easily.
  • logP — the log of the octanol/water partition coefficient; "greasiness". Positive means oil-loving (lipophilic), negative means water-loving. RDKit estimates it with the Crippen atom-contribution method.[22]
  • Hydrogen-bond donors (HBD) — the number of N–H and O–H groups. Each donor must shed its grip on water to cross a membrane, so donors cost absorption.
  • Hydrogen-bond acceptors (HBA) — roughly the number of N and O atoms with a lone pair. Like donors, they anchor a molecule in water.
  • Topological polar surface area (TPSA) — the summed surface of polar (mostly N and O) atoms, in Å², computed from the 2-D graph by a fast fragment method.[21] A single number for "how polar".
  • Rotatable bonds (RotB) — the number of single, non-ring bonds about which the molecule can freely twist; a measure of flexibility. Too much flexibility hurts oral absorption.

Two more appear later: the count of aromatic rings (flat six-membered-style rings; too many correlate with poor developability[24]) and the fraction of sp³ carbon (Fsp3), the share of carbons that are three-dimensional rather than flat — higher Fsp3 is associated with better success, the "escape from flatland" argument.[23] The cell below computes the whole panel for every approved drug and summarises it.

# One function computes the whole property panel from a molecule; reused by every rule below.
def property_panel(mol):
    return dict(MW=Descriptors.MolWt(mol), logP=Crippen.MolLogP(mol),
                HBD=Lipinski.NumHDonors(mol), HBA=Lipinski.NumHAcceptors(mol),
                TPSA=Descriptors.TPSA(mol), RotB=rdMolDescriptors.CalcNumRotatableBonds(mol),
                ArRings=rdMolDescriptors.CalcNumAromaticRings(mol), Fsp3=rdMolDescriptors.CalcFractionCSP3(mol))

def panel_df(mols_dict):
    return pd.DataFrame({n: property_panel(m) for n, m in mols_dict.items()}).T

3. Lipinski's rule of five

In 1997 Christopher Lipinski and colleagues at Pfizer looked at compounds that had reached clinical trials — molecules that had, by implication, survived the absorption hurdle — and asked what their properties had in common.[1] They found that poor absorption or permeability is more likely when a molecule breaks any two of four simple limits. The limits became the most cited rule in drug discovery, the rule of five, so named because every threshold is a multiple of five:

$$\text{MW} \le 500 \;\;\mid\;\; \log P \le 5 \;\;\mid\;\; \text{HBD} \le 5 \;\;\mid\;\; \text{HBA} \le 10$$ A molecule violates the rule of five if it breaks two or more of these four limits. One violation is tolerated; the rule is a coarse filter, not a precise predictor.

The biology behind each limit is the absorption tension of Unit 1. Size (MW) and greasiness (logP) above the caps make a molecule too large or too insoluble to cross the gut wall efficiently; too many hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors mean the molecule is so happily solvated by water that it pays a large energetic price to shed that water and slip through the fatty membrane. Lipinski counted acceptors as the raw number of nitrogen and oxygen atoms and donors as the number of N–H and O–H bonds — deliberately crude, because the rule was meant to be computed instantly on hundreds of thousands of molecules. (RDKit's Lipinski.NumHAcceptors uses a slightly refined definition that excludes, for instance, some nitrogens that do not actually accept; we will see the two counts differ and discuss why.)

The single most common misunderstanding

The rule of five predicts passive oral absorption — and nothing else. It says nothing about potency, selectivity, toxicity, or whether a molecule is a good drug. It does not apply to molecules absorbed by active transporters, nor to drugs given by injection. "Passes Ro5" does not mean "good"; "fails Ro5" does not mean "bad". Lipinski himself framed it as a flag, not a gate.

The cell implements the rule exactly as stated and counts violations for both datasets.

# Lipinski's rule of five: four limits, "violation" = breaking 2 or more.
def ro5_violations(mol):
    p = property_panel(mol)
    flags = {"MW>500": p["MW"] > 500, "logP>5": p["logP"] > 5,
             "HBD>5": p["HBD"] > 5, "HBA>10": p["HBA"] > 10}
    return sum(flags.values()), flags

def ro5_pass_rate(mols_dict):
    v = {n: ro5_violations(m)[0] for n, m in mols_dict.items()}
    return np.mean([x < 2 for x in v.values()]), v

app_rate, app_v = ro5_pass_rate(mols_app)
scr_rate, scr_v = ro5_pass_rate(mols_scr)
print("Rule-of-five PASS rate (<2 violations):  approved %.0f%%   screening %.0f%%" % (100*app_rate, 100*scr_rate))
print("approved drugs that FAIL Ro5 (>=2 violations):", [n for n, k in app_v.items() if k >= 2])
print("screening molecules that FAIL Ro5:", [n for n, k in scr_v.items() if k >= 2])

approved drugs that FAIL Ro5 (>=2 violations): ['atorvastatin']

Notice how weakly the rule separates these two particular sets — both pass at a high rate. The reason is instructive: the screening set is full of small fragments, and being under-sized is not a rule-of-five violation, so fragments sail through. The rule of five only ever flags molecules for being too big, too greasy, or too hydrogen-bonding; it is blind to the other ways a molecule can be unsuitable. The genuinely interesting result is the opposite one — an approved blockbuster, atorvastatin, sits in the failing list. That is the recurring lesson of the unit arriving early: a useful rule still has honourable exceptions, and the exceptions are often the most valuable molecules.

3.1 Worked example by hand: the rule of five for atorvastatin

Let us trace the rule on one molecule entirely by hand, then let the code confirm each number. Atorvastatin (the cholesterol-lowering drug marketed as Lipitor, historically the best-selling medicine of all time) has the molecular formula C₃₃H₃₅FN₂O₅. We evaluate the four limits:

  1. Molecular weight. Summing the standard atomic weights for C₃₃H₃₅FN₂O₅ gives ≈ 558.6 g/mol. This is above 500 → violation 1.
  2. logP. We cannot compute an octanol/water partition coefficient by hand, so we take the Crippen estimate the code reports, ≈ 6.3. This is above 5 → violation 2.
  3. Hydrogen-bond donors. Count N–H and O–H groups in the structure: the carboxylic acid contributes one O–H, the two secondary alcohols contribute one O–H each, and the amide contributes one N–H — four donors. 4 ≤ 5 → no violation.
  4. Hydrogen-bond acceptors (Lipinski raw). Count all N and O atoms: 2 nitrogens + 5 oxygens = 7. 7 ≤ 10 → no violation.

Two violations (MW and logP) means atorvastatin fails Lipinski's rule of five. A molecule that generated tens of billions of dollars would have been rejected by a naive rule-of-five filter. The cell confirms the arithmetic.

4. Veber's rules: flexibility and polarity

The rule of five says nothing about shape. In 2002 Veber and colleagues at GSK studied oral bioavailability in rats and found two further properties that matter independently of size and greasiness:[2] rotatable bonds and polar surface area. Their rule: good oral bioavailability is associated with

$$\text{rotatable bonds} \le 10 \;\;\text{and}\;\; \text{TPSA} \le 140~\text{Å}^2 \;\;(\text{or HBD}+\text{HBA} \le 12)$$ Fewer rotatable bonds means a more rigid molecule; too much flexibility is thought to hurt absorption because a floppy molecule pays a larger entropic penalty to adopt the shape needed to cross a membrane. TPSA captures polarity more smoothly than counting donors and acceptors.

Chemistry box — rotatable bonds and why flexibility costs

A rotatable bond is a single bond, not in a ring, joining two non-terminal heavy atoms — a hinge the molecule can twist around. A rigid ring has none; a long floppy chain has many. Think of it as the molecule's degrees of conformational freedom. A very flexible molecule exists as a cloud of shapes, and only some of those shapes fit through a membrane or into a binding site, so flexibility is a liability for both absorption and potency.

Veber and Lipinski are complementary: a molecule can pass one and fail the other. The cell applies Veber to both datasets and then visualises the two Veber properties together.

# Veber's rule: <=10 rotatable bonds AND TPSA <= 140 A^2.

5. More acceptance filters: Ghose, Egan and Muegge

Lipinski and Veber were followed by several refinements, each fitting slightly different thresholds to slightly different data. Three are worth knowing because cheminformatics tools report them side by side:

  • Ghose (1999) defines a "qualifying range" rather than upper caps: MW between 160 and 480, logP between −0.4 and 5.6, molar refractivity 40–130, and 20–70 atoms.[6] Note the lower bounds — Ghose also rejects molecules that are too small.
  • Egan (2000) uses just two properties, logP and TPSA, fitting an ellipse in that plane from absorption data; it is the basis of the later BOILED-Egg absorption model.[7]
  • Muegge (2001) is a "pharmacophore-point" filter with caps on MW, TPSA, rings, carbons, heteroatoms, donors, acceptors and rotatable bonds, designed to separate drug-like from non-drug-like chemical matter.[8]

No single filter is "correct"; they encode overlapping but non-identical opinions. The cell implements simplified forms of each and tabulates pass rates, which is how a real triage report presents them.

6. Fragments and leads: the rule of three and lead-likeness

A drug discovery project rarely starts from a drug-sized molecule. It usually starts from a small fragment or a lead and grows it. Because adding atoms almost always adds weight and greasiness, the starting point should be below the drug thresholds, leaving room to grow. This insight produced two more rule sets.

Chemistry box — fragment, lead, drug

In a project's life a molecule grows: a fragment is a very small molecule (roughly MW < 300) that binds weakly but is a good starting point; a lead is a moderately optimised molecule (MW ~300–400) with useful potency; a drug is the final, fully optimised molecule. Each stage has looser property limits than the drug it will become — you must leave headroom, because optimisation adds mass and lipophilicity.

Congreve's rule of three (2003) adapts Lipinski for fragment libraries: MW ≤ 300, logP ≤ 3, HBD ≤ 3, HBA ≤ 3 (and often rotatable bonds ≤ 3, TPSA ≤ 60).[9] Lead-likeness (Teague, Oprea and colleagues, 1999–2000) argues that screening libraries should be more restrictive than drug space — roughly MW ≤ 350 and logP ≤ 3 — so there is room to optimise toward, not past, the drug thresholds.[10][11] A related idea, ligand efficiency, normalises potency by size so that a small, modestly potent fragment can be recognised as a better starting point than a large, slightly more potent molecule.[12]

The cell classifies each molecule into the fragment/lead/drug size bands and applies the rule of three to the genuine fragments.

7. QED — drug-likeness as a continuous score

Every rule so far gives a hard yes/no. But drug-likeness is a matter of degree, and a binary flag throws away information: a molecule one unit over a threshold is treated the same as one far over it. In 2012 Bickerton and colleagues proposed the Quantitative Estimate of Drug-likeness (QED), a single number between 0 (unlike known drugs) and 1 (very drug-like).[5]

QED works in two steps. First, for each of eight properties — MW, logP, HBD, HBA, TPSA, rotatable bonds, aromatic rings, and a count of structural alerts — it defines a desirability function $d(x)\in[0,1]$ that is near 1 where approved drugs cluster and falls toward 0 in the tails. These shapes were fitted to the property distributions of ~770 oral drugs. Second, it combines the eight desirabilities into one score as a weighted geometric mean:

$$\text{QED} = \exp\!\left(\frac{\sum_i w_i \ln d_i}{\sum_i w_i}\right)$$ A geometric mean (not an arithmetic one) means a single very-undesirable property drives the whole score toward 0 — you cannot compensate for a terrible property by being excellent elsewhere. RDKit provides QED.default (the standard weights), QED.weights_mean and QED.weights_max.

QED is attractive because it is smooth, ranks molecules rather than merely accepting or rejecting them, and bakes the structural-alert idea of Section 8 directly into one of its eight terms. The cell computes it for both datasets.

8. Structural alerts: PAINS and Brenk

The filters so far judge a molecule by its bulk properties. A different kind of filter looks at substructures — specific arrangements of atoms known to cause trouble regardless of the molecule's size or greasiness. These are structural alerts, and they are matched with the SMARTS substructure queries of Unit 10, packaged in RDKit's FilterCatalog.

Two catalogues matter most. PAINS — Pan-Assay INterference compoundS — are substructures that make a molecule show up as a "hit" in many unrelated biological assays for reasons that have nothing to do with genuinely binding the target.[3]

Chemistry box — how a PAINS fools an assay

An assay usually reports binding indirectly — through a colour change, a fluorescent signal, or an enzyme's output. A PAINS substructure can produce that signal spuriously: some are coloured or fluorescent themselves; some form tiny aggregates that non-specifically soak up proteins; some are chemically reactive and covalently modify the target; some cycle through reduction and oxidation, consuming assay reagents. The result is a molecule that looks active everywhere and is specific nowhere — a false lead that wastes months.

The Brenk list flags substructures that are reactive, metabolically unstable, or associated with toxicity — not assay artefacts but genuine liabilities for a developable molecule.[4] The cell builds a combined PAINS + Brenk catalogue and scans both datasets.

Structural alerts are easy to over-trust. Several approved drugs — medicines taken safely by millions — trip an alert, usually from the broad Brenk list (aspirin's phenol ester, for instance). This is the heart of the matter. How to use a structural alert

An alert is a prompt to look, not an instruction to delete. The correct response to a PAINS or Brenk hit is to inspect the molecule, understand the proposed mechanism of interference, and — if the compound is interesting — design the right control experiment or a small structural change to test it. Silently filtering out every alerting molecule throws away good chemistry and bakes a decades-old assay bias into your library. Medicinal-chemistry judgement, not an automated gate, is the intended user.

A chart like this is how a cheminformatician eyeballs whether a new library resembles known drugs before committing to a screen — a qualitative companion to the quantitative filters above.

10. The limits of the rules — read this twice

Everything above is useful and everything above is dangerous, for the same reason: it compresses a vast, nuanced biology into a few thresholds fitted to a particular slice of history. Here is the honest accounting.

The rules describe where drugs have been, not where they can be. Lipinski's thresholds were reverse-engineered from the oral drugs and clinical candidates of the 1990s. They are a photograph of a historical distribution, and using them as gates freezes that distribution in place — rejecting the novel chemistry that expands it. Analyses of two decades of approved drugs show the property distributions have themselves drifted over time,[18] so a fixed rule ages.

"Beyond rule of five" chemistry is real and oral. Whole classes of successful oral drugs break the rule of five routinely — macrocycles, peptide-like molecules, and targeted-protein-degradation molecules among them. Studies of drugs and clinical candidates in this "beyond Ro5" space show that oral absorption remains achievable well outside the classic box, often through conformational tricks (hiding polar groups internally) that a flat property count cannot see.[19][20] The BDDCS framework argues that solubility and metabolism, not Lipinski's descriptors alone, govern a molecule's fate.[27]

The rules are about one route and one mechanism. They predict passive oral absorption. A drug given by injection, inhalation or infusion need not obey them at all; a drug carried across the gut by an active transporter can ignore them. None of the rules says anything about potency, selectivity, or whether the molecule will actually treat a disease.

Thresholds are soft and definitions vary. We already saw the acceptor count differ between Lipinski's raw definition and RDKit's refined one, enough to flip a borderline verdict. A molecule at MW 501 is not meaningfully different from one at 499. Treating a soft boundary as a hard gate manufactures false precision.

Optimising to pass a rule can make molecules worse. Because the rules reward low molecular weight and moderate lipophilicity, chemists chasing them sometimes inflate other risky properties — a tendency documented in the "molecular obesity" and "sweet spot" literature,[28] and in analyses linking lipophilicity and aromatic-ring count to attrition.[13] This is why richer, continuous schemes such as multi-parameter optimisation — for example the CNS MPO score for brain-penetrant drugs — are often preferred to any single pass/fail rule.[14]

  • The rule of three and lead-likeness, and why a starting fragment or lead must sit below the drug thresholds to leave room to grow.
  • QED as a continuous, weighted-geometric-mean drug-likeness score built from eight desirability functions, and how to read its overlap between drug-like and non-drug-like sets.
  • PAINS and Brenk structural alerts, how to compute them with RDKit's FilterCatalog, what mechanisms they flag, and why an alert is a hypothesis to investigate rather than a verdict.
  • Above all, the limits of every rule: they are historical photographs, soft at the boundaries, specific to passive oral absorption, and silent about potency and toxicity — so they prioritise, they do not decide.

12. Self-assessment

1. A colleague says "this compound is very potent, so it will make a good drug." What is wrong with the reasoning?Potency is only the first requirement. The molecule must also be absorbed, distributed to its target, survive metabolism, be excreted appropriately, and not be toxic — the ADMET properties. Most candidates fail on ADMET, not potency, so potency alone predicts little about success.
2. State the four limits of Lipinski's rule of five and the rule for when a molecule "fails".MW ≤ 500, logP ≤ 5, hydrogen-bond donors ≤ 5, hydrogen-bond acceptors ≤ 10. A molecule fails (is flagged) if it breaks two or more of the four limits; a single violation is tolerated.
3. Atorvastatin has MW ≈ 559 and logP ≈ 6.3, with 4 donors and 7 acceptors. How many rule-of-five violations, and what does that illustrate?Two violations (MW > 500 and logP > 5); donors and acceptors are within limits. So atorvastatin fails the rule of five — yet it is one of the best-selling drugs ever. It illustrates that the rule has important, valuable exceptions and must not be used as a hard gate.
4. Why does the rule of five count donors and acceptors, and what is the biological cost of having many?Each hydrogen-bond donor or acceptor binds water in solution. To cross the fatty cell membrane during absorption, the molecule must shed that water, which is energetically costly. Many donors/acceptors therefore reduce passive membrane permeability.
5. What two properties does Veber's rule add, and what does each capture?Rotatable bonds (molecular flexibility; ≤ 10) and topological polar surface area (overall polarity; ≤ 140 Å², or HBD+HBA ≤ 12). They capture shape and polarity effects on oral bioavailability that size and logP alone miss.
6. Ghose uses a lower bound on molecular weight (≥ 160) as well as an upper bound. Why might a filter reject a molecule for being too small?Very small molecules rarely have enough functional groups to bind a target potently and selectively; they are fragments, not finished drugs. A filter aimed at drug-sized matter therefore rejects both ends. (Fragments are judged instead by the rule of three.)
7. Why should a fragment satisfy the rule of three (MW ≤ 300, logP ≤ 3, …) rather than the rule of five?Optimising a fragment into a drug almost always adds mass and lipophilicity. Starting from a small, lean fragment leaves "headroom" to grow toward — not past — the drug thresholds. A fragment already at the Lipinski ceiling would blow past it once optimised.
8. QED combines eight property desirabilities with a weighted geometric mean. Why a geometric mean rather than an average?A geometric mean goes to zero if any single desirability is zero, so one extremely undesirable property drags the whole score down and cannot be compensated by excellence elsewhere. An arithmetic average would let a molecule hide a fatal flaw behind good averages.
9. Two molecules have QED 0.90 and 0.40. Can you conclude the first is a drug and the second is not?No. QED is a continuous ranking signal with heavily overlapping distributions between drug-like and non-drug-like molecules; small, very polar approved drugs can score low. QED is useful for ranking within a series, not as a pass/fail classifier.
10. What is a PAINS substructure, and name two mechanisms by which one can produce a false "hit" in an assay.A Pan-Assay INterference compound substructure that makes a molecule appear active across many unrelated assays without genuinely binding the target. Mechanisms include intrinsic fluorescence/colour, forming aggregates that non-specifically sequester proteins, chemical reactivity (covalent modification), and redox cycling that consumes reagents.
11. Aspirin triggers a Brenk alert. Should it therefore be removed from a screening library? Explain.No. Aspirin is a safe, effective, approved drug. Structural alerts fire on many innocuous and clinically used molecules (the "phantom PAINS" problem). An alert is a prompt to inspect the molecule and its proposed liability mechanism, not an instruction to delete it.
12. A screening pipeline automatically deletes every molecule that fails the rule of five, fails Veber, or trips any PAINS/Brenk alert. Give two distinct reasons this is a poor design.(1) It discards valuable chemistry — approved oral drugs such as atorvastatin, montelukast and ritonavir would be rejected, and alerts fire on safe drugs. (2) The rules predict only passive oral absorption and encode a historical, soft-boundaried distribution; they say nothing about potency or whether the target needs beyond-Ro5 chemistry, and injectable or actively transported drugs are exempt. Filters should prioritise and flag for human review, not gate automatically.

Glossary

ADMET
Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, Excretion, Toxicity — the properties that decide whether a potent molecule can become a usable drug.
Absorption
Passage of a drug from the gut into the bloodstream; for oral drugs it requires both water solubility and membrane permeability.
Assay
A laboratory measurement of a biological effect, such as how strongly a compound inhibits an enzyme.
Brenk alerts
A catalogue of substructures that are reactive, unstable or toxicity-associated and undesirable in a screening library.
Desirability function
A function mapping a property value to [0, 1], high where drugs cluster and low in the tails; QED multiplies eight of them.
Fsp3
Fraction of carbon atoms that are sp³ (three-dimensional) rather than flat/aromatic; higher values correlate with better developability.
HBA / HBD
Hydrogen-bond acceptors (roughly N and O atoms) and donors (N–H and O–H groups); both anchor a molecule in water and cost membrane permeability.
Lead-likeness
Property limits (e.g. MW ≤ 350, logP ≤ 3) for screening starting points, stricter than drug limits to leave room for optimisation.
Lipophilicity (logP)
Oil-versus-water preference, the log octanol/water partition coefficient; a central ADMET property.
PAINS
Pan-Assay INterference compoundS: substructures that give spurious hits across many assays through fluorescence, aggregation, reactivity or redox cycling.
Potency / IC50
How little compound is needed for a biological effect; IC50 is the concentration that halves a target's activity (lower = more potent).
QED
Quantitative Estimate of Drug-likeness: a 0–1 score combining eight property desirabilities by a weighted geometric mean.
Rotatable bond
A non-ring single bond between two non-terminal heavy atoms; a measure of molecular flexibility.
Rule of five (Lipinski)
MW ≤ 500, logP ≤ 5, HBD ≤ 5, HBA ≤ 10; fail = two or more violations. Predicts passive oral absorption.
Rule of three
The fragment-library analogue of the rule of five: MW ≤ 300, logP ≤ 3, HBD ≤ 3, HBA ≤ 3.
Structural alert
A substructure (SMARTS pattern) flagged as a liability; a hypothesis to investigate, not an automatic rejection.
TPSA
Topological polar surface area (Å²): the summed surface of polar atoms, computed from the 2-D graph; a smooth polarity measure.
Veber's rule
Oral bioavailability criterion: ≤ 10 rotatable bonds and TPSA ≤ 140 Å².
